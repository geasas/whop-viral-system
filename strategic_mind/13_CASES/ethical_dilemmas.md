## The Premise

An ethical dilemma is not a moral uncertainty; it is a conflict between two moral principles that the situation forces into a single decision. The contractor who faces the dilemma does not lack the moral knowledge; he has too much of it — the principle of honesty conflicts with the principle of compassion; the principle of contractual fidelity conflicts with the principle of family responsibility; the principle of competitive fairness conflicts with the principle of self-preservation. The dilemma is the conflict; the resolution is the choice that honors the dominant principle without abandoning the subordinate one.

The Islamic framework provides the resolution in three layers. The first is the **red line** (the *haram* that is never crossed — the *riba*, the *ghish*, the *rishwa* in the absolute sense). The second is the **weighted principle** (the *fiqh* of the priorities, where the higher principle takes precedence over the lower — the principle of harm prevention takes precedence over the principle of contractual fidelity when the contract causes the harm). The third is the **contextual judgment** (the *fiqh* of the reality, where the specific situation determines the application of the principle — the special discount that is permissible in one context and the bribe in another).

Each dilemma below follows the four-part structure: **Situation** (the dilemma in the contractor's life), **Ethical analysis** (the conflict between the principles), **Islamic ruling** (the *fiqh* resolution, with the sources cited), and **Strategic response** (the specific action the contractor takes when the dilemma arrives).

A note on the *fiqh* citations: I cite the classical sources (the *Muwatta*, the *Mudawwana*, al-Ghazali, Ibn Taymiyyah) where the ruling is established; I cite the modern *fatawa* (Dar al-Ifta, the International Islamic Fiqh Academy) where the contemporary application requires the modern ruling. The user should consult a qualified *mufti* for the specific rulings on the specific cases; this file provides the framework and the cited sources, not the *fatwa* for the specific case.

## Dilemma 1 — The Client Asks for a Bribe to Win the Bid

### Situation

The contractor is bidding for a project with a major developer. The developer's procurement manager, in a private conversation, makes clear that the bid will be awarded not on the merits but on the *rishwa* — the personal payment to the procurement manager. The amount is significant (the procurement manager asks for EGP 200,000–500,000, depending on the project size, in cash or through a front company). The contractor calculates: the project's profit margin is 15–20%; the *rishwa* is 5–8% of the project value; the project's net profit, after the *rishwa*, is 7–12%.

The dilemma: refuse the *rishwa* and lose the bid (the competitors who pay the *rishwa* will win), or accept the *rishwa* and win the bid (the *rishwa* is the *haram* that the contractor's *aqeedah* prohibits).

### Ethical analysis

The conflict is between the principle of competitive fairness (the bid should be awarded on the merits) and the principle of self-preservation (the contractor must win the bids to keep the business). The competitive fairness is the higher principle; the self-preservation is the lower principle that the higher principle takes precedence over. The conflict is sharpened by the asymmetry: the contractor's refusal does not change the system (the competitors will pay the *rishwa* and the system continues); the contractor's acceptance changes the contractor (the contractor becomes the participant in the corruption that the *aqeedah* prohibits). The refusal is the personal cost for the systemic unchanged; the acceptance is the personal gain for the systemic perpetuation.

The second conflict is between the short-term and the long-term. The short-term calculation (the project's profit, the cash flow, the team's wages) favors the acceptance; the long-term calculation (the reputation, the spiritual state, the trajectory of the firm) favors the refusal. The contractor who calculates only the short-term accepts; the contractor who calculates both the short-term and the long-term refuses.

### Islamic ruling

The *rishwa* (the bribe) is *haram* in the Islamic *fiqh*, with the consensus (*ijma'*) of the schools. The ruling applies to both the giver and the taker. The Prophet ﷺ said, "Allah has cursed the one who gives the bribe and the one who takes it" [Sunan Abu Dawud 3580, narrated by Abdullah ibn Amr — sahih per al-Albani]. The curse (*la'na*) is the most severe expression of the prohibition; the bribe is not merely the disliked (*makruh*); it is the cursed (*mal'un*).

The ruling's reasoning: the bribe is the violation of the justice (*'adl*) that the Islamic social order requires. The bribe awards the contract not on the merit but on the payment; the bribe transfers the wealth from the worthy to the unworthy; the bribe destroys the trust that the market depends on. The Quran says, "And do not consume each other's wealth unjustly or send it [in bribery] to the rulers in order that you may consume a portion of the wealth of the people in sin" [Quran 2:188 — al-Baqara]. The verse explicitly prohibits the bribe as the unjust consumption of the wealth.

The ruling's scope: the prohibition applies even when the contractor is the rightful winner of the bid on the merits. The contractor who would win the bid on the merits and pays the *rishwa* to ensure the award is still in violation; the *rishwa* is the *haram* regardless of the merit. The classical *fiqh* text *al-Mughni* of Ibn Qudama states: "If he gives something to take a right that is his, it is forbidden, because the *rishwa* is the *haram* in itself."

The ruling's exception: the *fiqh* discusses the case of the extortion (*ghasb* or *mithla*) where the ruler's official demands the payment for the performance of the duty he is already obligated to perform (the issuance of the license, the certification of the inspection). Some scholars (the Hanafi school, in particular) permit the payment in the extortion case where the refusal causes the unjust denial of the right. The exception is narrow and contested; the user should consult a qualified *mufti* before relying on it. The general rule is the prohibition; the exception is the narrow case.

### Strategic response

The strategic response is the *refusal with the alternative*. The contractor refuses the *rishwa* (the *haram*); the contractor offers the alternative (the legitimate competitive advantage that wins the bid on the merits). The alternative is the operational output of the strategic mind that has internalized the prohibition.

The alternative bid: the contractor's bid is structured to win on the merits — the price is competitive, the quality is documented, the timeline is credible, the team is identified, the past work is referenced. The bid is the strategic response to the *rishwa* demand; the bid is the demonstration that the contractor does not need the *rishwa* to win.

The alternative network: the contractor's relationship with the developer's senior management (not just the procurement manager) is the alternative to the *rishwa*. The senior management, when aware of the procurement manager's *rishwa* demand, has the incentive to act (the *rishwa* is the developer's loss too — the project goes to the lower-quality contractor who paid the *rishwa*). The contractor's relationship with the senior management is the strategic response.

The alternative market: the contractor's diversification of the client base is the alternative to the *rishwa*-dependent single client. The contractor with 20 clients can refuse the *rishwa* of one; the contractor with one client cannot. The diversification is the strategic response to the *rishwa* pressure.

The long-term calculation: the contractor who refuses the *rishwa* loses the bid and the short-term profit; the contractor who refuses the *rishwa* gains the reputation (the developer's senior management, the other clients, the industry peers who know the contractor refused) and the spiritual state (the *aqeedah* that the *rishwa* would have compromised). The long-term calculation favors the refusal; the long-term is the strategic horizon of the refusal.

## Dilemma 2 — The Subcontractor Underperforms; Firing Him Would Harm His Family

### Situation

The contractor has engaged a subcontractor for a project. The subcontractor's work is below the standard (the quality issues, the delays, the rework); the contractor's project is at risk; the client is pressuring the contractor for the completion. The contractor has the contractual right to terminate the subcontractor and engage a replacement.

The dilemma: terminate the subcontractor (the project is protected, but the subcontractor's family loses the income — the subcontractor has 4–6 children, the wife does not work, the extended family depends on the income) or continue the subcontractor (the family is protected, but the project is at risk — the client's claim, the delay damages, the reputation loss).

### Ethical analysis

The conflict is between the principle of contractual fidelity (the contractor's duty to the client to deliver the project on time and at the agreed quality) and the principle of compassion (the duty to the subcontractor's family who would be harmed by the termination). The contractual fidelity is the formal duty; the compassion is the moral duty. The conflict is sharpened by the asymmetry: the termination is the formal right; the compassion is the moral restraint on the formal right.

The second conflict is between the short-term and the long-term. The short-term calculation (the project's completion, the client's satisfaction) favors the termination; the long-term calculation (the subcontractor's family, the industry reputation, the spiritual state) favors the compassion. The contractor who calculates only the short-term terminates; the contractor who calculates both the short-term and the long-term seeks the alternative.

### Islamic ruling

The Islamic ruling is the *harm minimization* principle: *la darar wa la dirar* (no harm and no reciprocating harm). The principle is a Prophetic *hadith* [Muwatta Malik 1435, narrated by Abu Sa'id al-Khudri — sahih; the *hadith* is one of the foundational maxims of the Islamic *fiqh*]. The principle prohibits the harm and the reciprocating harm; the termination of the subcontractor (the harm to the family) is the harm that the principle addresses, but the failure to deliver the project (the harm to the client) is also the harm that the principle addresses. The dilemma is the conflict of two harms; the resolution is the minimization of the total harm.

The classical *fiqh* resolution: when two harms conflict, the lesser harm is chosen to avoid the greater harm. The principle is *irtikaab al-ahwan al-sharrayn* (the committing of the lesser of two evils). The lesser harm in this dilemma is the harm to the subcontractor's family (the temporary loss of the income, the search for the new work) that the contractor can mitigate through the compassionate termination (the notice, the severance, the recommendation); the greater harm is the harm to the client (the project's failure, the delay damages, the potential bankruptcy if the project is critical) that the contractor cannot mitigate. The lesser harm is the termination; the greater harm is the failure to terminate.

The ruling's nuance: the *fiqh* requires the contractor to *mitigate* the lesser harm. The compassionate termination (the 2–4 week notice, the severance payment equal to 1–2 months of the subcontractor's wages, the recommendation to the other contractors, the assistance in the subcontractor's transition) is the operationalization of the harm mitigation. The termination without the mitigation is the harm without the compassion; the termination with the mitigation is the harm with the compassion.

The ruling's alternative: the *fiqh* also considers the alternative of the *correction* before the termination. The contractor's duty to the subcontractor includes the warning, the retraining, the second chance. The Prophet ﷺ said, "Whoever does not show mercy to the people, Allah will not show mercy to him" [Sahih al-Bukhari 7376, narrated by Jarir ibn Abdullah — Tier 1, classical]. The mercy is operationalized as the warning, the retraining, the second chance; the termination is the last resort after the correction has failed.

### Strategic response

The strategic response is the *graded intervention* — the sequence of the warning, the retraining, the second chance, the compassionate termination.

The graded intervention:
- **Week 1**: The warning — the contractor documents the quality issues, the delays, the rework; the contractor meets with the subcontractor, presents the documentation, requests the improvement plan, sets the timeline (2–4 weeks) for the improvement.
- **Week 2–4**: The retraining — the contractor's foreman works with the subcontractor's team to address the quality issues; the contractor provides the additional training, the tools, the supervision. The retraining is the contractor's investment in the subcontractor's improvement.
- **Week 5–6**: The second chance — the subcontractor is given the second chance to deliver the work at the agreed quality. The second chance is the operationalization of the mercy.
- **Week 7 (if the second chance fails)**: The compassionate termination — the contractor terminates the subcontractor with the notice (2–4 weeks), the severance (1–2 months of wages), the recommendation (to the other contractors), the assistance (in the transition). The compassionate termination is the operationalization of the harm minimization.

The strategic response's reasoning: the graded intervention is the operationalization of the *fiqh* principles (the harm minimization, the mercy, the lesser of two evils) in the specific sequence that the dilemma requires. The intervention protects both the project (the client's right) and the family (the subcontractor's right) through the sequence of the correction and the compassionate termination.

## Dilemma 3 — The Client's Payment Is Delayed; the Subcontractor Needs Payment Now

### Situation

The contractor's client (the developer) has delayed the payment of the milestone by 60–90 days beyond the contractual date. The contractor's subcontractor, who performed the work for the milestone, needs the payment now — the subcontractor's team's wages are due, the subcontractor's suppliers are demanding the payment, the subcontractor's own family (the same family of Dilemma 2) is at risk.

The dilemma: the contractor pays the subcontractor from the contractor's own cash reserves (the contractor's working capital is depleted, the bank financing would be the *riba* the contractor has refused), or the contractor delays the subcontractor's payment (the subcontractor's family is harmed, the subcontractor may refuse to continue, the project is at risk).

### Ethical analysis

The conflict is between the contractor's duty to the subcontractor (the payment for the work performed) and the contractor's duty to himself (the preservation of the working capital that the business depends on). The duty to the subcontractor is the contractual duty and the moral duty; the duty to the working capital is the strategic duty. The conflict is sharpened by the asymmetry: the contractor's payment of the subcontractor from his own reserves is the interest-free loan to the subcontractor (the *qard hasan*) that the contractor cannot afford; the contractor's delay of the subcontractor's payment is the harm to the family that the contractor's *aqeedah* prohibits.

The second conflict is between the *riba* prohibition (the contractor cannot finance the gap with the bank loan) and the *ghanimah* obligation (the contractor must pay the subcontractor from the *halal* source). The *riba* is the *haram* the contractor has refused; the *ghanimah* is the *halal* the contractor must find.

### Islamic ruling

The Islamic ruling has two components. The first is the ruling on the contractor's duty to the subcontractor: the duty is the immediate payment for the work performed, the obligation that the Prophetic *hadith* "Give the worker his wage before his sweat dries" [Sunan Ibn Maja 2443, narrated by Abdullah ibn Umar — Tier 1, classical] establishes. The duty is not contingent on the contractor's receipt of the client's payment; the duty is the obligation to the worker (the subcontractor) for the work performed. The classical *fiqh* text *al-Mughni* states: "The wage is the obligation of the one who engaged the worker, not the obligation of the one for whom the work was performed." The contractor's duty to the subcontractor is independent of the client's duty to the contractor.

The second is the ruling on the *riba* prohibition: the contractor may not finance the gap with the *riba*-based bank loan. The Quran says, "Allah has permitted trade and forbidden *riba*" [Quran 2:275 — al-Baqara]. The prohibition is absolute; the contractor may not invoke the cash flow gap as the justification for the *riba*.

The resolution: the contractor pays the subcontractor from the *halal* sources available — the cash reserves, the *qard hasan* (the interest-free loan) from the family or the friends, the *murabaha* or the *musharaka* financing from the Islamic bank, the sale of an asset to raise the cash. The contractor's strategic move is the *halal* financing of the subcontractor's payment, not the *riba* financing and not the delay.

The ruling's nuance: if the contractor genuinely cannot pay the subcontractor from the *halal* sources, the ruling requires the *honest disclosure* — the contractor tells the subcontractor the truth (the client's payment is delayed, the contractor's cash reserves are insufficient), the contractor offers the partial payment (the wages of the workers, the priority), the contractor commits to the payment schedule (the date the client's payment is expected, the date the subcontractor will be paid). The honest disclosure is the operationalization of the *amanah* (the trust) that the contractor owes the subcontractor. The Prophet ﷺ said, "There is no faith for the one who is not trustworthy, and no religion for the one who does not keep his covenant" [Musnad Ahmad 12312, narrated by Anas ibn Malik — sahih per al-Albani].

### Strategic response

The strategic response is the *halal financing of the subcontractor's payment* combined with the *honest disclosure* and the *client-recovery action*.

The halal financing: the contractor identifies the *halal* sources of the payment — the cash reserves (the 3–6 month reserve the contractor should have, per Case 3 of `personal_business_cases.md`), the *qard hasan* from the family or the friends, the *murabaha* from the Islamic bank, the sale of an asset. The halal financing is the operationalization of the *riba* prohibition.

The honest disclosure: the contractor tells the subcontractor the truth. The truth is the *amanah* the contractor owes the subcontractor; the lie (the false promise of the immediate payment) is the *ghish* the *aqeedah* prohibits. The honest disclosure includes the partial payment (the wages of the workers, the priority), the payment schedule (the date the client's payment is expected), the contractor's commitment to the schedule.

The client-recovery action: the contractor's strategic move against the client who has delayed the payment is the *graduated response* — the polite letter citing the contract clause, the meeting with the client's senior management, the formal notice of the breach, the suspension of the work (if the contract permits), the legal action (if necessary). The client-recovery action is the operationalization of the contractor's right; the right is exercised through the lawful means, not the *ghish* or the *riba*.

The long-term response: the contractor's diversification of the client base (so that the single client's delay does not produce the cash flow crisis) and the building of the cash reserve (so that the contractor can absorb the delay without the subcontractor crisis) are the long-term strategic responses to the recurring dilemma.

## Dilemma 4 — The Competitor Is Spreading False Rumors About You

### Situation

The contractor learns that a competitor is spreading false rumors about him — the rumors that the contractor's work is substandard, that the contractor is in financial difficulty, that the contractor has been blacklisted by a major client, that the contractor's team is unreliable. The rumors are reaching the contractor's clients and the developer community; the contractor's recent bid was declined with the reference to the rumors.

The dilemma: respond to the rumors (the public denial, the legal action, the counter-rumors about the competitor) or ignore the rumors (the risk that the rumors continue to spread, the risk that the silence is taken as the confirmation).

### Ethical analysis

The conflict is between the principle of reputation protection (the contractor's duty to defend his reputation, which is the asset his business depends on) and the principle of dignified restraint (the duty to avoid the public dispute that degrades both parties). The reputation protection is the legitimate duty; the dignified restraint is the moral restraint on the legitimate duty. The conflict is sharpened by the asymmetry: the competitor's false rumors are the *ghish* (the deception) the *aqeedah* prohibits; the contractor's counter-rumors would be the same *ghish* the *aqeedah* prohibits.

The second conflict is between the short-term and the long-term. The short-term calculation (the immediate defense, the public denial, the legal action) favors the response; the long-term calculation (the reputation built over years, the work that speaks for itself, the dignity that the silence preserves) favors the restraint. The contractor who calculates only the short-term responds; the contractor who calculates both the short-term and the long-term seeks the alternative.

### Islamic ruling

The Islamic ruling on the false rumor (*ghiba* or *buhtan*) is the prohibition. The *ghiba* (the backbiting) is the mention of the brother with what he would dislike; the *buhtan* (the slander) is the mention with what is false. The Quran says, "And do not spy or backbite each other. Would one of you like to eat the flesh of his brother when dead? You would detest it" [Quran 49:12 — al-Hujurat]. The *buhtan* is the greater prohibition; the Prophet ﷺ said, "The slanderer will not enter Paradise" [Sahih al-Bukhari 6056, narrated by Abu Hurayra — Tier 1, classical].

The contractor's response: the *fiqh* permits the defense against the *buhtan*. The defense is the legitimate right; the defense may be the public denial (the statement of the truth), the legal action (the lawsuit for the defamation), the demand for the retraction (the request that the competitor retract the false rumors). The defense is not the *ghiba*; the defense is the operationalization of the justice (*'adl*) that the *aqeedah* requires.

The ruling's restraint: the *fiqh* also requires the restraint. The defense is the legitimate right; the counter-rumors (the false rumors about the competitor in retaliation) are the *buhtan* the contractor is prohibited from. The defense is the truth; the counter-rumors are the falsehood. The Prophet ﷺ said, "Be true, for the truth leads to righteousness, and righteousness leads to Paradise" [Sahih al-Bukhari 6094, narrated by Abdullah ibn Mas'ud — Tier 1, classical]. The defense is the truth; the contractor's duty is the truth, not the counter-falsehood.

The ruling's priority: the *fiqh* prioritizes the private response over the public response. The contractor's first response is the private (the direct communication with the competitor, the demand for the retraction, the mediation through the industry association); the public response (the public statement, the legal action) is the second step if the private response fails. The private response is the operationalization of the dignity; the public response is the operationalization of the justice when the private response has failed.

### Strategic response

The strategic response is the *graded defense* — the private, the public, the legal.

The private response:
- **Step 1**: The direct communication — the contractor calls the competitor and the competitor's senior management, states the truth (the false rumors are false, the evidence is the contractor's record), demands the retraction. The direct communication is the operationalization of the dignity and the truth.
- **Step 2**: The mediation — the contractor engages the industry association (the Federation of Contractors, the chamber of commerce) to mediate the dispute. The mediation is the operationalization of the community's role in the dispute resolution.
- **Step 3**: The documentation — the contractor documents the false rumors (the witnesses, the written communications, the impact on the bid), the documentation is the foundation for the public and the legal responses.

The public response (if the private fails):
- **Step 4**: The public statement — the contractor publishes the truth (the contractor's record, the false nature of the rumors, the competitor's identity if the documentation supports it) in the industry channels (the association's newsletter, the trade press, the contractor's own website). The public statement is the operationalization of the justice; the public statement is the truth, not the counter-falsehood.
- **Step 5**: The client communication — the contractor communicates directly with the clients who have heard the rumors, presents the truth, the documentation, the evidence. The client communication is the operationalization of the *amanah* the contractor owes the clients.

The legal response (if the public fails):
- **Step 6**: The legal action — the contractor files the lawsuit for the defamation, the trade libel, the unfair competition. The legal action is the operationalization of the right through the lawful means.

The long-term response: the contractor's reputation-building discipline (the consistent quality, the fair dealing, the integrity — see `14_ETHICS/long_term_reputation.md`) is the long-term strategic response. The reputation built over years is the asset that the false rumor cannot destroy; the reputation that the false rumor can destroy was not the reputation built on the discipline but the reputation built on the marketing.

## Dilemma 5 — The Supplier Offers a "Special Discount" That Seems Too Good

### Situation

The contractor's regular supplier offers a "special discount" on a major materials order — the discount is 30–40% below the market price, the discount is "for a limited time", the discount is "with a special payment arrangement" (the cash payment to a different account, the invoicing through a different company, the delivery without the standard documentation). The contractor calculates: the discount saves the project 10–15% of the materials cost; the project's margin improves significantly.

The dilemma: accept the discount (the project's margin improves, but the "special arrangement" is suspicious — the materials may be counterfeit, the supplier may be dumping the stolen goods, the supplier may be evading the taxes, the supplier may be bribing the contractor to look the other way) or refuse the discount (the project's margin is preserved at the standard level, but the contractor avoids the risk of the counterfeit materials, the tax evasion, the bribe).

### Ethical analysis

The conflict is between the principle of cost minimization (the contractor's duty to the project's profitability) and the principle of integrity (the duty to avoid the suspicious transaction that may be the *haram*). The cost minimization is the legitimate duty; the integrity is the moral restraint on the legitimate duty. The conflict is sharpened by the asymmetry: the discount is the apparent gain; the suspicion is the possible loss (the counterfeit materials that fail the inspection, the tax evasion that implicates the contractor, the bribe that the contractor has accepted unknowingly).

The second conflict is between the short-term and the long-term. The short-term calculation (the project's margin) favors the acceptance; the long-term calculation (the risk of the counterfeit, the legal exposure, the reputation) favors the refusal.

### Islamic ruling

The Islamic ruling on the suspicious transaction is the principle of *ittiba' al-dhan* (the avoidance of the suspicion). The Prophet ﷺ said, "Leave what makes you doubt for what does not make you doubt" [Sunan al-Tirmidhi 2518, narrated by al-Hasan ibn Ali — sahih]. The principle requires the contractor to leave the suspicious transaction (the discount that "seems too good") for the non-suspicious transaction (the standard market price with the standard documentation).

The ruling's reasoning: the suspicious transaction is the transaction that may be the *haram* (the counterfeit materials that are the *ghish*, the stolen goods that are the *ghasb*, the tax evasion that is the violation of the *sharia* obligation to pay the *zakat* and the taxes, the bribe that is the *rishwa*). The contractor who accepts the suspicious transaction may be accepting the *haram* unknowingly; the *ittiba' al-dhan* principle requires the avoidance of the *haram* even at the cost of the apparent gain.

The ruling's specific applications:
- **The counterfeit materials**: the materials that do not meet the standard are the *ghish* (the deception) the *aqeedah* prohibits. The contractor who installs the counterfeit materials in the client's project is the contractor who has deceived the client; the *ghish* is the *haram* regardless of the discount.
- **The stolen goods**: the goods that are stolen are the *ghasb* (the usurpation) the *aqeedah* prohibits. The contractor who buys the stolen goods is the contractor who has participated in the *ghasb*; the *ghasb* is the *haram* regardless of the discount.
- **The tax evasion**: the transaction that evades the taxes (the cash payment to the different account, the invoicing through the different company) is the violation of the *sharia* obligation to pay the taxes. The Quran says, "And fulfill the covenant" [Quran 17:34 — al-Isra']; the covenant includes the tax obligation the contractor owes the state. The tax evasion is the *haram* regardless of the discount.
- **The bribe**: the discount that is the bribe (the "special arrangement" that benefits the contractor personally, not the project) is the *rishwa* the *aqeedah* prohibits (see Dilemma 1).

The ruling's resolution: the contractor refuses the suspicious discount. The refusal is the operationalization of the *ittiba' al-dhan* principle. The refusal may lose the apparent gain; the refusal preserves the integrity that the long-term trajectory depends on.

### Strategic response

The strategic response is the *refusal with the alternative* — the contractor refuses the suspicious discount, the contractor offers the alternative (the legitimate discount that the supplier can offer with the standard documentation).

The refusal: the contractor states the refusal to the supplier — the contractor cannot accept the "special arrangement" (the different account, the different company, the missing documentation); the contractor can accept the discount with the standard arrangement (the same account, the same company, the full documentation). The refusal is the operationalization of the *ittiba' al-dhan*.

The alternative: the contractor requests the legitimate discount — the volume discount, the long-term customer discount, the early-payment discount — that the supplier can offer with the standard documentation. The legitimate discount is the alternative to the suspicious discount.

The due diligence: the contractor's due diligence on the supplier (the supplier's reputation, the supplier's licensing, the supplier's tax compliance, the supplier's quality certifications) is the long-term strategic response. The due diligence is the operationalization of the *ittiba' al-dhan* principle across the supplier relationships.

The long-term response: the contractor's diversification of the supplier base (so that the single supplier's "special discount" does not produce the temptation) and the building of the long-term supplier relationships (so that the supplier's legitimate discounts are available) are the long-term strategic responses.

## Dilemma 6 — The Project Has a Hidden Defect; the Client May Not Notice

### Situation

The contractor has completed a project. During the final inspection, the contractor's foreman discovers a hidden defect — a defect that is not visible to the client's inspection (the defect is behind the wall, in the conduit, in the panel's internal wiring), a defect that may or may not produce a problem in the future (the defect may be cosmetic, the defect may be a code violation, the defect may be a fire hazard). The client's inspector has signed off on the project; the client has made the final payment; the contractor is the only one who knows about the defect.

The dilemma: disclose the defect (the contractor repairs it at his own expense, the contractor's relationship with the client is preserved, the contractor's integrity is intact) or conceal the defect (the contractor saves the repair cost, the client may never discover the defect, the contractor's profit on the project is preserved).

### Ethical analysis

The conflict is between the principle of honesty (the duty to disclose the defect) and the principle of self-interest (the cost of the repair, the risk of the client's reaction, the impact on the relationship). The honesty is the moral duty; the self-interest is the strategic consideration. The conflict is sharpened by the asymmetry: the disclosure is the cost (the repair, the client's possible reaction); the concealment is the risk (the future discovery, the legal liability, the reputation loss, the spiritual burden).

The second conflict is between the short-term and the long-term. The short-term calculation (the repair cost, the client's reaction) favors the concealment; the long-term calculation (the future discovery, the legal liability, the reputation, the spiritual state) favors the disclosure.

### Islamic ruling

The Islamic ruling on the concealment of the defect (*tatbi' al-'ayb*) is the prohibition. The Prophet ﷺ said, "Whoever cheats us is not of us" [Sahih Muslim 101, narrated by Abu Hurayra — Tier 1, classical]; the *hadith* is the foundational text on the commercial integrity. The Prophet ﷺ also said, in the *hadith* of the sale with the defect: "The seller and the buyer have the option [to cancel the sale] as long as they have not parted, and if both spoke the truth and made clear the defects of the goods, their transaction would be blessed; and if they concealed and told lies, the blessing of their transaction would be obliterated" [Sahih al-Bukhari 2114, narrated by Hakim ibn Hizam — Tier 1, classical].

The ruling's reasoning: the concealment of the defect is the *ghish* (the deception) that the *aqeedah* prohibits. The contractor who installs the work with the defect and conceals the defect is the contractor who has deceived the client; the *ghish* is the *haram* regardless of the cost of the repair.

The ruling's scope: the disclosure is required even when the defect is hidden and the client may never discover it. The classical *fiqh* text *al-Mughni* states: "If the seller knows of a defect in the goods, he must disclose it; if he conceals it, the sale is the *ghish* and the buyer has the option to return the goods." The contractor's installation of the defective work is the sale of the defective goods; the disclosure is the obligation.

The ruling's specific application to the defect's severity:
- **The cosmetic defect** (the visible imperfection that does not affect the function): the disclosure is required; the repair is the contractor's obligation if the client requests it; the contractor's reputation is preserved by the disclosure.
- **The code violation** (the deviation from the code that does not immediately produce the hazard): the disclosure is required; the repair is the contractor's obligation; the contractor's licensing may be at risk if the code violation is discovered by the inspection.
- **The fire hazard** (the defect that may produce the fire, the injury, the death): the disclosure is required; the repair is the immediate obligation; the concealment of the fire hazard is the criminal liability in addition to the *haram*.

The ruling's resolution: the contractor discloses the defect to the client, repairs the defect at his own expense, and the relationship with the client is preserved. The disclosure is the operationalization of the *amanah* (the trust) the contractor owes the client; the repair is the operationalization of the *ghish* prohibition.

### Strategic response

The strategic response is the *immediate disclosure with the repair plan*.

The immediate disclosure: the contractor informs the client of the defect immediately — the phone call, the meeting, the written documentation of the defect (the location, the nature, the severity, the cause). The immediate disclosure is the operationalization of the *amanah*; the disclosure is the truth, not the concealment.

The repair plan: the contractor presents the repair plan — the timeline (immediate for the fire hazard, scheduled for the code violation, coordinated with the client for the cosmetic), the cost (the contractor's expense), the warranty (the repaired work is warranted). The repair plan is the operationalization of the *ghish* prohibition.

The relationship preservation: the contractor's disclosure and repair preserve the relationship with the client. The client who has been informed of the defect and has seen the contractor's repair is the client who trusts the contractor for the next project. The relationship is the long-term asset; the concealment is the short-term gain that destroys the long-term asset.

The spiritual state: the contractor's disclosure and repair preserve the spiritual state. The contractor who has concealed the defect carries the burden of the concealment — the spiritual weight of the *ghish* that the *aqeedah* prohibits. The contractor who has disclosed the defect and repaired it carries the relief of the integrity; the spiritual state is the asset the concealment would have destroyed.

The long-term response: the contractor's quality discipline (the inspection process that catches the defects before the final inspection, the foreman's training that prevents the defects, the supplier's quality control that rejects the defective materials) is the long-term strategic response. The defect that is caught in the contractor's inspection is the defect that does not produce the dilemma; the quality discipline is the operationalization of the *ghish* prohibition through the prevention.

## Cross-Dilemma Synthesis: The Six Principles

Reading the six dilemmas together, six principles emerge that the contractor will use as the framework when the next dilemma arrives.

**First: The *haram* is never crossed, regardless of the cost.** Dilemma 1 (the *rishwa*), Dilemma 5 (the suspicious discount), Dilemma 6 (the *ghish* of the concealed defect). The *haram* prohibition is absolute; the short-term calculation does not override the prohibition; the long-term calculation favors the prohibition.

**Second: The harm minimization resolves the conflict of two harms.** Dilemma 2 (the subcontractor's family vs. the project), Dilemma 3 (the *riba* vs. the delay). The lesser harm is chosen to avoid the greater harm; the harm is mitigated through the compassionate action.

**Third: The honest disclosure is the operationalization of the *amanah*.** Dilemma 3 (the honest disclosure to the subcontractor), Dilemma 4 (the truth in the defense), Dilemma 6 (the disclosure of the defect). The honest disclosure is the duty the contractor owes the counterparties; the disclosure is the truth, not the concealment.

**Fourth: The graded intervention is the operationalization of the mercy.** Dilemma 2 (the warning, the retraining, the second chance, the compassionate termination), Dilemma 4 (the private, the public, the legal). The graded intervention is the sequence that honors both the principle of the correction and the principle of the dignity.

**Fifth: The diversification is the strategic response to the recurring dilemma.** Dilemma 1 (the client base diversification), Dilemma 3 (the cash reserve), Dilemma 5 (the supplier base diversification). The diversification is the long-term strategic response that reduces the recurrence of the dilemma.

**Sixth: The long-term calculation favors the ethical response.** Every dilemma. The short-term calculation favors the *haram* (the *rishwa*, the *riba*, the *ghish*, the concealment); the long-term calculation favors the *halal* (the refusal, the disclosure, the integrity). The contractor who calculates both the short-term and the long-term chooses the *halal*; the contractor who calculates only the short-term chooses the *haram*.

## What I Still Don't Know

The honest gaps:

- The Islamic rulings cited are the classical and the modern *fiqh* positions; the specific application to the contemporary Egyptian contractor requires the *fatwa* of the qualified *mufti* for the specific case. I have provided the framework and the sources, not the *fatwa*.
- The Egyptian legal framework (the contracts law, the competition law, the consumer protection law, the labor law) interacts with the Islamic rulings; a fuller treatment would discuss the specific Egyptian statutes that apply to each dilemma.
- The "special discount" dilemma (Dilemma 5) has many specific variants (the volume discount, the long-term customer discount, the early-payment discount) that may be legitimate; the suspicious variant is the one with the "special arrangement" (the different account, the different company, the missing documentation). I have addressed the suspicious variant; the legitimate variants are not dilemmas.
- The defect disclosure dilemma (Dilemma 6) has the variant of the defect that is discovered after the warranty period has expired; the contractor's obligation in this variant is the subject of *fiqh* discussion that I have not fully addressed.
- The competitor's rumors (Dilemma 4) has the variant of the rumors that are partially true (the contractor did have a project delay, the contractor did have a payment dispute with a client); the response to the partially-true rumor is the subject of the *fiqh* of the *tawbah* (the repentance) and the *'itiraf* (the acknowledgment) that I have not fully addressed.
