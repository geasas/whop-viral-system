# Reading Situations — How to Perceive Before Acting

> **Purpose**: The first step of any strategic decision is perceiving the situation accurately. Most failures begin here. This file teaches how to read a situation before doing anything else.

---

## The Premise

You cannot diagnose what you cannot see. You cannot strategize about a situation you've misread. Most strategic failures are not failures of strategy — they are failures of perception. The strategist saw the wrong situation, then applied a perfectly good strategy to the wrong problem.

This file is the discipline of perceiving situations accurately before any other step.

---

## The Five Lenses of Situation Reading

To read a situation, look through five different lenses. Each reveals something the others miss.

### Lens 1: The Surface Lens (What is happening?)

What is the visible, observable situation? What is being said? What is being done? What are the explicit facts?

**Example**: A client called and asked me to revise my quote. The new quote should be 15% lower.

This is the surface. Don't stop here, but DO start here. Many strategists skip the surface in pursuit of "deeper meaning" and miss basic facts.

**Action**: Write down the observable facts. No interpretation yet.

### Lens 2: The Interest Lens (Why is this happening?)

What does each party have to gain or lose from this situation? Why is this happening NOW?

**Example**: Why is the client asking for 15% lower NOW, not when they first got the quote?
- Possibility A: They got a lower quote from a competitor and want to give me a chance to match.
- Possibility B: They've decided on me and want to test if I'll go lower.
- Possibility C: They had a budget cut and need to reduce scope.
- Possibility D: They're trying to extract value because they think I'm desperate.

Each possibility implies a different situation.

**Action**: List at least 3 possible "whys" before settling on one.

### Lens 3: The Power Lens (Who has leverage, and how much?)

Who in this situation has the ability to walk away? Who has alternatives? Who has time pressure?

**Example**: 
- If the client has 3 other contractors lined up, they have leverage.
- If I'm the only qualified one for the specific scope, I have leverage.
- If the project is time-pressured (client needs to start in 2 weeks), the client loses leverage.

Power is not static; it shifts through the negotiation. Map the current power and the trajectory.

**Action**: Identify who has leverage NOW, who will have leverage in 30 days, who will have leverage at the end.

### Lens 4: The History Lens (What has happened before?)

Has this situation happened before? With this person? In this market? What was the outcome?

**Example**: Have I quoted this client before? Did they negotiate? Did they pay on time? Did they come back?

History is the most reliable predictor, but only if it's YOUR history with this specific person. Generic industry history is less reliable.

**Action**: Recall or check records of past interactions with this person and similar situations.

### Lens 5: The Future Lens (What will this lead to?)

What happens 1 month, 6 months, 1 year after each possible outcome?

**Example**: 
- If I accept the 15% lower quote, I get the contract but establish a precedent of negotiating down. Future quotes will be tested.
- If I refuse and they walk, I lose this deal but establish a precedent of firm pricing.
- If I offer a 7% reduction with a justification (smaller scope, longer timeline), I get the deal and establish a precedent of principled negotiation.

The future lens reveals the precedent being set. Decisions are not one-off; they set trajectories.

**Action**: For each option, identify the precedent it sets.

## The Three Traps in Situation Reading

### Trap 1: The Urgency Trap

When a situation is presented as urgent, the strategist is pressured to act before reading. This is the most common trap.

**Symptom**: "I need an answer by tomorrow morning."

**Reality**: Most "urgency" is manufactured by the other party to prevent you from reading the situation. Real urgency exists (a fire, a payment deadline) but is rarer than claimed.

**Counter**: When told it's urgent, ask: "What happens if I take 48 hours instead?" The answer reveals whether it's truly urgent. If they say "the deal is off," it wasn't urgent — they were pressuring. If they say "we'll lose the supplier," that's a real deadline.

### Trap 2: The Single-Explanation Trap

Most situations have multiple plausible explanations. The strategist jumps to one and acts on it.

**Symptom**: "He's doing this because he wants a better price."

**Reality**: He might want a better price. He might also be testing my reaction. He might be reacting to internal pressure. He might be setting up a future negotiation. He might have misunderstood something.

**Counter**: Generate at least 3 explanations before acting on any one. Use the multi-hypothesis engine (`00_META/multi_hypothesis_engine.md`).

### Trap 3: The Self-Centered Trap

The strategist assumes the situation is about THEM. Most situations are about the OTHER party's situation, not about the strategist.

**Symptom**: "He's trying to take advantage of me."

**Reality**: He's probably not thinking about you much at all. He's thinking about his budget, his boss, his cash flow, his reputation. You're a variable in his equation, not the subject of it.

**Counter**: Re-read the situation from the other party's perspective. What is HE going through? What are HIS pressures?

## The 7-Question Reading Protocol

When facing a new situation, ask these 7 questions in order:

1. **What is the surface situation?** (What is happening, observable, factual?)
2. **Who are the parties involved, and what are their interests?**
3. **Who has leverage NOW, and who will have leverage LATER?**
4. **What has happened before in similar situations with these parties?**
5. **What are 3+ plausible explanations for what's happening?**
6. **What precedent does each possible action set?**
7. **What is the OTHER party's situation, beyond me?**

If you can answer all 7 with confidence, you've read the situation. If you can't answer one, you need more information before acting.

## Worked Example

**Situation**: A developer I've worked with for 2 years has emailed asking if I can lower my quote by 20% on a new project. He says another contractor offered less. He says he'd prefer to work with me but the gap is significant. He wants an answer in 48 hours.

### Reading through the 5 lenses:

**Lens 1 (Surface)**: Developer wants 20% reduction. Cites a competitor. 48-hour deadline.

**Lens 2 (Interest)**: Why now? Possibilities:
- A: He really got a lower quote and wants me to match.
- B: He didn't get a lower quote; he's bluffing to test my pricing.
- C: He got a lower quote but the competitor is unreliable; he wants me to match so he doesn't have to switch.
- D: He has a budget cut and needs any reduction he can get; the competitor story is leverage.

**Lens 3 (Power)**: 
- NOW: He has leverage (he has an alternative).
- 48 HOURS LATER: If I say no, the leverage shifts (he must either accept my price or take the risk on the competitor).
- 1 YEAR LATER: If I lower 20% now, future negotiations will start from this lower base.

**Lens 4 (History)**: In 2 years, has he negotiated before? Have his previous "competitor offers" been real? Have I ever lowered my price before? (Check records.)

**Lens 5 (Future)**: 
- Accept 20% lower: Sets precedent that 20% negotiation works. Future quotes start lower in his mind.
- Refuse: Lose the project. Maintain pricing power for future.
- Counter at 10%: Sets precedent that negotiation works but has limits. Future quotes start with this ceiling.

### The 7 questions:

1. Surface: He wants 20% lower, cites competitor, 48-hour deadline.
2. Parties: Him (developer, has 2-year relationship with me, has competitor offer). Me (electrical contractor, has 2-year track record with him, has pricing based on real costs).
3. Power: He has alternative NOW. If I refuse, he faces risk on competitor (uncertain quality). My power increases after refusal if competitor is unreliable.
4. History: Need to check records.
5. Explanations: 4 listed above. Most likely (based on 2-year relationship and him saying "I'd prefer to work with you"): B or D — he's using the competitor as leverage, not because he wants to switch.
6. Precedent: Accepting 20% sets a bad precedent. Counter at 10% sets a moderate precedent. Refusing maintains pricing power.
7. His situation: He has a budget pressure OR he has a board / partner pressuring him OR he wants to look good to his investors by getting better pricing.

### Reading conclusion:

The situation is NOT "I need to lower my price to keep the client." The situation is "The client is testing whether I'll lower my price; if I lower it 20%, I lose pricing power for the next 5 years of our relationship; if I refuse politely with explanation, I likely keep both the project and the pricing power, because the alternative is uncertain for him."

The right move: Counter at 7-10% reduction with justification (specific scope adjustment or payment terms), and explicitly note this is the floor. Don't accept the 20%. Don't refuse outright.
