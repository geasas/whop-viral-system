# Power Structure Mapping — Who Really Has Power

> **Purpose**: In any organization or relationship, there is the formal power structure (the org chart) and the real power structure (who actually decides). They are rarely the same. This file teaches the system to map the real structure.

---

## The Premise

The org chart shows you the formal hierarchy. It tells you who signs the contracts, who approves the budgets, who reports to whom. It does NOT tell you:
- Who actually makes the decisions
- Who has the ear of the decision-maker
- Who can veto without anyone knowing
- Who is the gatekeeper who controls information flow
- Who is the rising star whose opinion is increasingly weighted
- Who is the declining power whose title still says "Director" but whose opinions no longer carry weight

If you act on the formal structure, you'll pitch to the wrong person, negotiate with the wrong party, and miss the real decision-maker.

---

## The Five Power Roles

In any organization or relationship network, there are five power roles. Identify each in any situation.

### Role 1: The Decider

The person who actually makes the decision. This is often NOT the person with the highest title.

**How to identify**:
- The Decider is the person whose "no" cannot be overturned by anyone else
- The Decider is the person who can commit resources without further approval
- The Decider is the person everyone defers to in meetings, even if someone else is presenting

**Note**: The Decider may be hidden. Some organizations deliberately obscure who the Decider is, to avoid direct lobbying.

### Role 2: The Influencer

The person who shapes the Decider's thinking. They don't make the decision, but the decision usually follows their recommendation.

**How to identify**:
- The Influencer is the person the Decider consults before deciding
- The Influencer is the person whose opinion is sought on the topic
- The Influencer is the person whose recommendation is almost always accepted

**Note**: An Influencer on one topic may be irrelevant on another. Influence is domain-specific.

### Role 3: The Gatekeeper

The person who controls access to the Decider or to information. They don't decide, but they shape what the Decider sees.

**How to identify**:
- The Gatekeeper is the person who screens calls, emails, meeting requests
- The Gatekeeper is the person who prepares briefings for the Decider
- The Gatekeeper is the person who can delay or accelerate your access

**Note**: Gatekeepers are often underestimated. They have low formal power but high structural power. Angering a gatekeeper is one of the most common strategic errors.

### Role 4: The Veto Holder

The person who can block a decision without making it. They don't say yes; they say "I won't say no — yet."

**How to identify**:
- The Veto Holder is the person whose approval is needed but not sufficient
- The Veto Holder is the person the Decider won't override
- The Veto Holder is often in a "support" role: legal, finance, compliance, IT

**Note**: Veto Holders are often invisible until they exercise the veto. The strategic mind identifies them BEFORE the veto, not after.

### Role 5: The Implementer

The person who will actually execute the decision. They have low formal power in the decision but high practical power in execution.

**How to identify**:
- The Implementer is the project manager, the team lead, the operational manager
- The Implementer is the person who will translate the decision into action
- The Implementer is the person whose buy-in determines whether the decision works

**Note**: A decision the Implementer disagrees with will be executed poorly. The Implementer's tacit buy-in is necessary for the decision to succeed.

## Mapping Methodology

To map a power structure for a specific decision:

### Step 1: Identify the decision

Be specific. "What contractor to hire" is a decision. "How to engage the contractor" is a different decision. The power map is decision-specific.

### Step 2: List all parties who will be involved or affected

Include everyone, even those who seem peripheral. Often the peripheral person is the Veto Holder.

### Step 3: For each party, ask the 5-role questions

- Can this person say yes and have it stick? (Decider)
- Does this person shape the Decider's thinking? (Influencer)
- Does this person control access to the Decider? (Gatekeeper)
- Can this person block the decision? (Veto Holder)
- Will this person execute the decision? (Implementer)

A person may occupy multiple roles. (The Decider is often also the Influencer; the Gatekeeper is often a Veto Holder.)

### Step 4: Identify the relationships

Who talks to whom? Who owes whom? Who is married to whom? Who went to school with whom?

The relationships often explain why a formally weak person has real influence.

### Step 5: Map the map

Draw the structure on paper or in a doc. Nodes (people), arrows (influence relationships), labels (role). The visual map reveals patterns the text doesn't.

### Step 6: Update the map periodically

Power structures shift. The Decider today may be the Influencer in 6 months. The Gatekeeper today may be the Implementer in a year. Review the map before any major decision.

## Common Power-Structure Patterns

### Pattern 1: The Founder-CEO

The Decider is also the Founder and CEO. They have ALL roles. No Influencer matters; no Veto Holder matters (unless they're a board member with veto rights).

**Strategic implication**: Pitch directly to the Founder-CEO. Don't waste time with intermediaries. They have no power.

### Pattern 2: The Family Business

The Decider is the family head. The Influencer is the spouse. The Gatekeeper is the eldest son. The Veto Holder is the family member who controls the finances. The Implementer is the most competent non-family employee.

**Strategic implication**: Pitch to the Decider with the spouse in the room. Don't anger the eldest son. Get buy-in from the financial-keeper. Befriend the Implementer.

### Pattern 3: The Bureaucracy

The Decider is the Department Head. The Influencer is the Senior Advisor. The Gatekeeper is the Executive Assistant. The Veto Holders are Finance, Legal, and Compliance. The Implementer is the Project Manager.

**Strategic implication**: Pitch to the Decider through the EA. Get the Senior Advisor's backing first. Get Finance and Legal reviews early. Build a relationship with the Project Manager.

### Pattern 4: The Co-Founders

Two co-founders who are jointly the Deciders. They often disagree. The Influencer is the third co-founder (the tiebreaker). The Gatekeeper is the Operations Manager (who serves both). The Veto Holder is whichever co-founder the Decider is currently closer to.

**Strategic implication**: NEVER play the co-founders against each other. Pitch to both simultaneously. Get the third co-founder's neutral or supportive stance. Be aware of the current alliance structure (which is dynamic).

### Pattern 5: The Government Client

The Decider is the Director-General. The Influencer is the Technical Committee. The Gatekeeper is the Procurement Officer. The Veto Holder is the Compliance/Audit Department. The Implementer is the Project Engineer.

**Strategic implication**: Follow procurement procedures precisely. Don't try to shortcut. Build a relationship with the Project Engineer (they'll be your partner for years). Get the Technical Committee's positive recommendation early. Don't anger Compliance — they can void a contract.

## Power Mapping in Egypt's Construction Sector

Specific patterns observed in Egypt:

### The Developer's Power Map

- **Decider**: The Chairman (often the founder of the development company)
- **Influencer**: The Chairman's spouse or eldest son (in family-run developers)
- **Gatekeeper**: The Chairman's Executive Secretary
- **Veto Holders**: The CFO (financial veto), the Technical Office Manager (technical veto)
- **Implementer**: The Project Manager (often a consulting engineer or external PM)

**Strategic moves**:
- Get the Executive Secretary to put your name in front of the Chairman
- Get the Technical Office Manager to review your proposal BEFORE submitting to the Chairman (so it doesn't get shot down technically)
- Get the CFO's preliminary OK on payment terms (so financial approval is fast)
- Build a relationship with the Project Manager (your day-to-day interface for 2+ years)

### The Government Tender Power Map

- **Decider**: The Minister (rarely involved directly)
- **Influencer**: The Tender Committee (3-5 members, often technical + financial + administrative)
- **Gatekeeper**: The Tender Office Secretary
- **Veto Holders**: The Central Auditing Organization (post-award), the National Democratic Party apparatus (political vetting, in some eras)
- **Implementer**: The Contracting Department

**Strategic moves**:
- Get a member of the Tender Committee to support your proposal (without violating integrity)
- Submit paperwork PERFECTLY (technical disqualification is common)
- Build relationships with the Contracting Department (they execute the contract)
- Be aware of political sensitivities (don't get involved in sectarian or partisan issues)

### The Subcontractor Power Map

- **Decider**: The Subcontractor's Owner
- **Influencer**: The Owner's brother/partner (often invisible to outsiders)
- **Gatekeeper**: The Owner's wife (often handles the phones and scheduling)
- **Veto Holders**: The Owner's main client (if you conflict with the main client, the subcontractor will drop you)
- **Implementer**: The Subcontractor's site supervisor

**Strategic moves**:
- Don't compete with the subcontractor's main client for work
- Get the site supervisor's buy-in (he determines whether your project is prioritized or delayed)
- Be aware of the family dynamics (in family-run subcontractor businesses, the wife often has more practical power than appears)

## The Power Map Update Rule

Power maps decay. People die, retire, switch jobs, fall out of favor, gain favor. A power map made in January may be obsolete by June.

**Rule**: Before any major decision, ask: "Has the power map changed since I last looked?" If you don't know, the map needs updating.

**Signs of power shift**:
- A person who used to be in meetings is no longer there
- A person whose opinion used to be sought is now silent
- A new person is in a meeting they shouldn't be in (often means they're being groomed for promotion)
- A person's title has changed (promotions are sometimes shifts in apparent power; sometimes they're sideways moves disguised as promotions)

---

## The Anti-Pattern

The biggest anti-pattern: assuming the formal structure is the real structure. The formal structure is what's on the website. The real structure is what you'd see if you sat in their office for a week.

The strategic mind builds the real map, not the formal one. The user's job is to provide the information; the system's job is to interpret it.
