## The Premise

Every decision is preceded by a diagnosis. The diagnosis may be explicit (a structured assessment) or implicit (an unconscious assumption). The implicit diagnosis is the source of most decision failures — the decision-maker didn't think about what kind of decision this is, didn't assess the stakes, didn't check the time horizon, didn't route to the right layer, and acted on a wrong assumption.

The discipline: **make the diagnosis explicit before any decision is taken.**

This file is the diagnosis. Five questions, in order:
1. The 7-question diagnosis (from `02_PERCEPTION/reading_situations.md`)
2. What kind of decision is this? (strategic, tactical, operational)
3. What is the stake? (reversible vs irreversible)
4. What is the time horizon? (immediate, medium, long)
5. Which layer should handle this? (routing)

After diagnosis, the decision is routed to the right layer (see `domain_routing.md`, `mind_routing.md`), the right mind(s) are invoked (see `multi_mind_synthesis.md`), the blind spots are checked (see `blind_spot_checker.md`), the biases are filtered (see `anti_bias_filter.md`), and only then is the action recommended.

Skipping the diagnosis is the most common failure mode of strategic systems. The diagnosis takes 5 minutes; the failure it prevents can cost 5 years.

## Step 1: The 7-Question Diagnosis (from reading_situations.md)

The first step in any diagnosis is reading the situation accurately. This was detailed in `02_PERCEPTION/reading_situations.md`. The seven questions:

1. **What is the surface situation?** (observable, factual, what is happening?)
2. **Who are the parties involved, and what are their interests?**
3. **Who has leverage NOW, and who will have leverage LATER?**
4. **What has happened before in similar situations with these parties?**
5. **What are 3+ plausible explanations for what's happening?**
6. **What precedent does each possible action set?**
7. **What is the OTHER party's situation, beyond me?**

If the contractor cannot answer all seven with confidence, the situation is not yet readable. The right response is not to act but to gather more information. Acting on an unread situation is gambling.

**The application**: A client calls the contractor at 11 PM and demands he attend a 7 AM meeting the next morning to renegotiate a contract that's already signed. The contractor's instinct is to attend (urgency). The 7-question diagnosis:
1. Surface: Client wants to renegotiate a signed contract, 8 hours' notice.
2. Parties: Client (developer, has signed contract, now wants change), Contractor (has signed contract, asked to attend).
3. Leverage: Now, client has leverage (urgency, his schedule). Later, contractor has leverage (contract is signed; renegotiation requires both parties' consent).
4. History: Has the client renegotiated signed contracts before? Check records.
5. Explanations: (a) client found a lower bid elsewhere and wants to renegotiate; (b) client had a budget cut; (c) client is testing the contractor's resolve; (d) client genuinely changed requirements and needs to renegotiate.
6. Precedent: attending on 8 hours' notice sets the precedent that urgency works; declining sets the precedent that renegotiation requires reasonable notice.
7. Client's situation: He has a board meeting tomorrow at which he must report; he needs an answer; the urgency is his, not the contractor's.

The diagnosis: this is a manufactured urgency. The contractor should decline the 7 AM meeting and propose a meeting in 3-5 days, with a written agenda in advance. The renegotiation, if it happens, happens on equal terms.

The diagnosis took 5 minutes. The wrong instinct would have cost the contractor's leverage for the life of the contract.

## Step 2: What Kind of Decision Is This?

Not all decisions are the same. Three tiers:

**Strategic decisions.** Shape the long-term direction of the business or life. Examples: scaling from electrical to general contracting, entering a new market, taking a 5-year exclusive contract, getting married, choosing to study usul al-fiqh seriously. Strategic decisions are made rarely, deliberately, with high deliberation cost. The Sima Yi / Cao Cao / Dong Zhuo minds apply. The multi-hypothesis engine is mandatory.

**Tactical decisions.** Operationalize the strategic direction within a specific context. Examples: bidding a specific project, hiring a foreman, choosing a subcontractor for a job, choosing a supplier for a major purchase, choosing to take a 6-month course on AI. Tactical decisions are made weekly to monthly. The Guo Jia / Liu Bei / Yuan Fang minds apply. The multi-hypothesis engine is recommended.

**Operational decisions.** Day-to-day execution within tactical parameters. Examples: assigning workers to a site today, ordering materials for next week, deciding whether to attend a site meeting or send the foreman, scheduling the weekly review. Operational decisions are made daily. The Lu Bu / Xiahou Dun minds apply. The multi-hypothesis engine is optional but the bias check is mandatory.

**The discipline**: classify the decision before acting. Strategic decisions deserve hours of diagnosis; tactical decisions deserve 30-60 minutes; operational decisions deserve 5 minutes. The mistake is treating strategic decisions as operational ("quick, decide now") or operational decisions as strategic (agonizing for hours over which supplier to call today).

**The application**:
- "Should I take this 5-year exclusive contract with the developer?" — STRATEGIC. Hours of diagnosis. Multi-hypothesis engine. Sima Yi + Cao Cao minds. Bias check (sunk cost if I've invested in the relationship; loss aversion if I fear losing the deal).
- "Should I bid this EGP 2M residential project?" — TACTICAL. 30-60 minutes of diagnosis. Multi-hypothesis engine recommended. Guo Jia + Liu Bei minds. Bias check (overconfidence in pricing).
- "Should I send Ahmed or Mahmoud to site A today?" — OPERATIONAL. 5 minutes of diagnosis. Lu Bu mind (whoever is closer and available). Bias check (favoritism toward Ahmed).

The classification alone prevents the most common decision failure: spending hours on operational decisions and 30 seconds on strategic ones.

## Step 3: The Stake Assessment — Reversible vs Irreversible

Jeff Bezos' distinction: one-way doors and two-way doors.

**Two-way doors (reversible).** If you walk through and don't like what you see, you can walk back. The cost of being wrong is low. Examples: trying a new supplier for one order, taking a 1-month course, attending a networking event, accepting a small bid. Decision discipline: decide quickly; the cost of analysis exceeds the cost of being wrong.

**One-way doors (irreversible).** If you walk through, you cannot easily walk back. The cost of being wrong is high. Examples: signing a 5-year exclusive contract, getting married, taking a bank loan with interest (haram — irreversible in religious terms), buying a major asset, hiring a partner who will be embedded in the business. Decision discipline: decide slowly; the cost of analysis is small compared to the cost of being wrong.

**The discipline**: identify the door type before deciding. The mistake is treating one-way doors as two-way ("we can always change it later") — you cannot, in fact, change it later — and treating two-way doors as one-way ("this is a life-or-death decision" — for a one-month course? No).

**The application**:
- "Take a 1-month AI course online" — two-way door. Decide in 10 minutes. If it doesn't help, you've lost EGP 500 and 30 hours. Cheap.
- "Take a 5-year exclusive contract with the developer" — one-way door. Decide in 1-2 weeks. If it goes wrong, you've lost 5 years of capacity. Expensive.
- "Hire Ahmed as a foreman" — depends. If Ahmed is on probation and easily terminated, two-way door. If Ahmed is brought in as a partner with equity, one-way door. The classification depends on the contract structure.
- "Take a bank loan at 20% interest to finance a project" — one-way door with religious dimension. Even if the project succeeds, the riba is a sin that does not reverse. This is a one-way door in both commercial and religious terms.
- "Buy a warehouse for the business" — one-way door. Capital intensive, slow to reverse.

The discipline extends: most decisions feel reversible but aren't. A "we can always renegotiate" assumption usually fails because renegotiation requires the other party's consent, which may not be given. A "we can always sell the asset" assumption usually fails because asset sales are slow and at depressed prices. Test the reversibility assumption explicitly: under what exact conditions could I reverse this? How long would it take? How much would it cost? If you cannot specify the reversal mechanism, the decision is one-way.

## Step 4: The Time Horizon Assessment

Every decision has a time horizon over which it pays off or fails. The horizon determines which mind applies and which risks matter.

**Immediate (days to weeks).** Operational decisions, urgent tactical responses. The Guo Jia mind (rapid exploitation), the Lu Bu mind (force), the Xiahou Dun mind (execution). Risks: short-term; manageable by immediate action.

**Medium (months to 1-2 years).** Tactical decisions, project execution, relationship building, skill acquisition. The Cao Cao mind (integrated strategy), the Liu Bei mind (heart and legitimacy), the Yuan Fang mind (chaos exploitation within a window). Risks: project-level, market-level; manageable by planning and adaptation.

**Long (3-10+ years).** Strategic decisions, business scaling, family formation, reputation accumulation, spiritual development. The Sima Yi mind (patient long game), the Dong Zhuo mind (macro board control), the Jia Xu mind (survival and timing). Risks: structural, systemic, existential; manageable only by sound fundamentals and time.

**The discipline**: identify the time horizon before deciding. A decision that's right at one horizon may be wrong at another.

**The application**:
- "Accept the contract at lower margin to maintain the relationship." Right at the medium horizon (relationship value); wrong at the long horizon (margin erosion compounds; pricing power is lost). For a one-time strategic client, medium horizon wins. For a long-term pricing policy, long horizon wins.
- "Skip the course on Islamic finance to focus on the project deadline." Right at the immediate horizon (deadline met); wrong at the long horizon (knowledge gap compounds; future Islamic finance decisions are made blind). The long-horizon cost usually exceeds the short-horizon gain.
- "Fire the difficult subcontractor now to keep the project on schedule." Right at the immediate horizon (schedule pressure relieved); wrong at the medium horizon (subcontractor network shrinks; replacements are scarce and expensive). The medium-horizon cost usually exceeds the immediate gain.

The conflict between horizons is the source of most difficult decisions. The discipline: identify which horizon the decision optimizes for, and acknowledge the cost at the other horizons. Most decisions cannot optimize all horizons; the choice is which horizon to serve.

## Step 5: The Decision Type Routing

After the diagnosis, route the decision to the right layer(s).

**Business decisions** → 09_BUSINESS. The layer covers contracting, real estate, halal business, entrepreneurship. Sub-routing within: contracting decisions go to 09_BUSINESS/contracting/, real estate decisions to 09_BUSINESS/real_estate/, halal finance questions to 09_BUSINESS/halal_business/, scaling questions to 09_BUSINESS/entrepreneurship/.

**Personal decisions** → 10_PERSONAL. The layer covers physical (health, training, nutrition, recovery), psychological_self (ambition, resilience, self-knowledge, relationships), spiritual (aqeedah, usul, rizq, dua, tahajjud). Sub-routing within based on the nature of the personal issue.

**Strategic decisions** → 07_MINDS + 08_STRATEGY. The mind layer for which character to invoke; the strategy layer for which classical doctrine applies (Sun Tzu, Machiavelli, 36 Stratagems, 48 Laws, Islamic strategy history, Arabic political classics).

**Ethical decisions** → 14_ETHICS. Any decision with an ethical dimension (which is most) routes through the ethics layer for the red-line check, the harm-minimization check, the honesty-in-deception check, the manipulation-vs-strategy check, the long-term-reputation check.

**Learning decisions** → 11_LEARNING. Decisions about what to study, how to study, which domain to deepen — route through the learning layer. The meta-learning discipline applies; the domain-specific files (physics, mathematics, programming, AI) apply per domain.

**Decision decisions** → 12_DECISION. When the question is itself about how to decide — the diagnosis discipline, the routing discipline, the mind synthesis, the blind spot check, the anti-bias filter — route to this layer.

**The discipline**: most decisions cross domains. A bid decision is business (09), strategic (07+08), ethical (14), and personal (10 — because the contractor's time and risk tolerance are personal). A marriage decision is personal (10), strategic (07+08 — Sima Yi's patience, Liu Bei's heart), ethical (14), and (in the long run) business (09 — the spouse is a partner in the life). The routing is usually multi-domain. See `domain_routing.md` for the full multi-domain routing protocol.

## The Full Diagnosis — A Worked Example

**Situation**: The contractor is offered a 5-year exclusive contract to be the electrical contractor for a major real estate developer's projects in New Cairo and 6 October. The developer wants a 20% lower rate than the contractor's standard. The contract has a minimum-volume clause that is favorable (EGP 30M/year guaranteed) and a right-to-terminate for the developer if the contractor fails to meet quality standards (the contractor's standards are well-established). The developer offers 25% advance payment.

### Step 1: The 7-question diagnosis

1. Surface: 5-year exclusive contract, 20% rate reduction, minimum volume EGP 30M/year, 25% advance, developer-side termination on quality.
2. Parties: Developer (large, multi-project, wants exclusivity and price reduction). Contractor (mid-sized, has strong reputation, currently doing 5-6 projects per year).
3. Leverage: Now, developer has leverage (large contract, exclusivity request). Later, contractor has leverage (exclusivity commits the developer to him). 5 years out, leverage depends on relative growth.
4. History: Has the developer negotiated hard before? Have their exclusivity terms been kept? Have they terminated contracts on quality grounds? Check records.
5. Explanations: (a) developer wants cost certainty and is willing to commit volume for it; (b) developer wants to lock in a contractor before competitors do; (c) developer has budget pressure and needs rate reduction; (d) developer wants to test contractor's pricing flexibility for future negotiations.
6. Precedent: accepting 20% lower sets the precedent that the contractor negotiates on rate; accepting exclusivity sets the precedent that the contractor commits capacity; the minimum volume sets the precedent that the downside is bounded.
7. Developer's situation: They are scaling fast; they need reliable contractors; they are willing to pay a premium for that reliability in the form of multi-year commitment; they want price certainty for their own planning.

### Step 2: What kind of decision?

This is STRATEGIC. 5 years of capacity commitment. The classification determines: hours of diagnosis, multi-hypothesis engine mandatory, bias check mandatory.

### Step 3: The stake

This is a ONE-WAY DOOR. The contractor commits 5 years of capacity. Reversal requires the developer's consent (or a quality default, which would damage the contractor's reputation). The classification determines: high deliberation; the analysis cost is small compared to the cost of being wrong.

### Step 4: Time horizon

This is LONG. 5 years. The classification determines: Sima Yi mind (patience over the 5 years), Cao Cao mind (integrated strategy — what does this enable?), Dong Zhuo mind (macro board — what does this exclude?). The medium horizon (next year) is favorable (volume, revenue stability). The long horizon has both upside (reputation, financial stability) and downside (capacity lock-in, opportunity cost of projects not taken).

### Step 5: Routing

This routes to:
- 09_BUSINESS/contracting/ — for the contracting terms, the volume clause, the pricing decision
- 09_BUSINESS/halal_business/ — for the Islamic finance check (any riba? any gharar?)
- 07_MINDS/sima_yi/, cao_cao/, dong_zhuo/ — for the strategic mind synthesis
- 14_ETHICS/ — for the red-line check (any deception? any harm to third parties?)
- 12_DECISION/ — for the multi-mind synthesis (multiple minds needed) and the blind spot check

The decision is then taken through the multi-mind synthesis (see `multi_mind_synthesis.md`), the blind spot check (see `blind_spot_checker.md`), and the anti-bias filter (see `anti_bias_filter.md`).

### The Diagnosis Conclusion

The situation is: a strategic, one-way-door, long-horizon decision that crosses business, ethical, and personal domains, requiring multiple minds (Sima Yi for patience, Cao Cao for integration, Dong Zhuo for macro board) and a full audit. The right pace is 1-2 weeks of analysis, not 1-2 days. The right mode is deliberation, not reaction. The right standard is "this serves the 10-year plan or it doesn't" — not "this is a good deal on its own terms."

## The Discipline in One Page

1. **7-question diagnosis**: surface, parties, leverage, history, explanations, precedents, other party's situation.
2. **Classify**: strategic, tactical, or operational. The classification sets the deliberation budget.
3. **Stake**: one-way door or two-way door. One-way doors get heavy deliberation; two-way doors get quick decisions.
4. **Time horizon**: immediate, medium, long. Identify which horizon this decision optimizes for; acknowledge the cost at other horizons.
5. **Route**: which layer(s) handle this. Most decisions cross domains — route to multiple.

If the contractor does these five before every consequential decision, his decisions improve dramatically. The diagnosis is 80% of the decision; the action is the remaining 20%. Most decision failures are failures to diagnose.

## Common Pitfalls

**Pitfall 1: Skipping the diagnosis for "obvious" decisions.** The contractor says "this is obvious" and acts. The 7-question diagnosis reveals that the situation is not what it appeared. "Obvious" is the most dangerous word in decision-making.

**Pitfall 2: Treating all decisions as the same tier.** Strategic decisions deserve hours; operational decisions deserve minutes. Treating them the same wastes time on operational decisions and risks strategic ones.

**Pitfall 3: Misclassifying reversible as irreversible.** Most "we cannot change this" assumptions are false. Test the reversibility: under what exact conditions could I reverse this? If you can specify the conditions, the door is two-way.

**Pitfall 4: Misclassifying irreversible as reversible.** More common: "we can always renegotiate" assumes the other party's consent, which may not be given. Treat decisions as irreversible unless you can specify the reversal mechanism.

**Pitfall 5: Single-horizon thinking.** A decision that's right for this year may be wrong for 5 years from now. Identify the horizon; acknowledge the cost at other horizons.

**Pitfall 6: Single-domain routing.** A bid decision is business + strategic + ethical + personal. Routing only to business misses the ethical check (riba?), the strategic check (does this serve the long game?), and the personal check (do I have the bandwidth?).
