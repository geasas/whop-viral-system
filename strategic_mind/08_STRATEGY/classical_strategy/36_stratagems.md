## Why this file exists

The user demanded the 36 Stratagems treated as more than a list. Each stratagem is a compressed strategic move with a mechanism, not a slogan. This file unpacks each of the 36 in six categories, anchors each in an example drawn from the *Ravages of Time* corpus (the project's narrative substrate, scraped from 635 chapters at `/home/z/my-project/data/raw_manga/chapters/`), and applies each to the Egyptian contractor's domain. The categories — Winning, Enemy-Dealing, Attacking, Chaos, Proximate, Defeat — are not decorative. The first three are stratagems for the strong; the last three are stratagems for the desperate, and the *Defeat Stratagems* (31-36) are the most strategically profound: how to lose well, retreat well, and convert loss into future advantage.

## The core principle

The 36 Stratagems answer one question: **given asymmetric information, asymmetric power, and an opponent who thinks he knows more than he does, what move converts his model of the situation into your advantage?** Every stratagem exploits a mismatch between the opponent's belief and reality. The categories are about *whose* belief is being exploited: an enemy's belief about your strength (Winning), about your intentions (Enemy-Dealing), about his own security (Attacking), about the situation's stability (Chaos), about who is friend and who is foe (Proximate), and about whether you have lost (Defeat).

## Islamic ethical note

The *Ravages of Time* examples include deception, treachery, and manipulation. The Islamic ethical framing (see `14_ETHICS/` layer, to be built) is essential: stratagems against competitors (concealment of legitimate information, misdirection of competitive intelligence) are permissible; stratagems against clients (misrepresenting the work, concealing defects, false invoicing) are *haram* and reputation-destroying in a market that runs on word of mouth. The user should read this file as a manual for competitor strategy and dispute defense, not for client deception.

## Chapter 1 — Winning Stratagems (Stratagems 1–6)

These are stratagems for the strong: they require some capacity to act and the freedom to choose engagement.

### 1. Deceive the heavens to cross the ocean (瞒天过海)

The stratagem hides a major action in plain sight by exploiting the opponent's inability to believe that what is visible is real. The bigger the action, the less likely the opponent is to see it because it exceeds his model of possibility.

*Ravages of Time example*: Cao Cao's consolidation of the emperor Xian at Xuchang. He did not hide the fact — he published it loudly as "protecting the emperor." The warlords who should have recognized the move for what it was (a seizure of imperial legitimacy) dismissed it as Cao Cao's characteristic theatrics. By the time they realized, the legitimacy had been transferred.

*Contractor application*: When you take a major strategic move (entering a new market, hiring a senior engineer from a competitor, opening a Cairo office), frame it as routine. The competitor who believes you are "just doing more of the same" does not react. The competitor who sees the move for what it is — a positional shift — reacts. The longer he does not react, the more time you have to consolidate.

### 2. Besiege Wei to rescue Zhao (围魏救赵)

When an ally (or your own flank) is under attack, do not rush to defend. Attack the attacker's home base instead, forcing him to disengage.

*Ravages of Time example*: Yuan Fang's orchestration of the warlord responses to Dong Zhuo. Rather than meet Dong Zhuo's army in the field at Luoyang, the coalition threatened Dong Zhuo's base of power — and forced him to burn Luoyang and retreat to Chang'an, abandoning the capital.

*Contractor application*: When a competitor poaches one of your key clients, do not match his discount (defending the lost ground). Instead, approach the competitor's other clients with a better offer — force him to defend his own base, which diverts his attention from the client he just took from you. The competitor's lost ground is your reclaimed ground.

### 3. Kill with a borrowed knife (借刀杀人)

Use a third party to damage the opponent while preserving your own resources. The third party may be an ally of the opponent, a regulatory body, or an unwitting participant.

*Ravages of Time example*: Dia Chan's manipulation of Lu Bu against Dong Zhuo — Wang Yun did not kill Dong Zhuo himself; he engineered the situation so that Dong Zhuo's own bodyguard (Lu Bu) killed him. The "borrowed knife" was the strongest warrior in the realm.

*Contractor application*: When a subcontractor consistently underdelivers on your projects, do not absorb the cost. Document the failures and let the subcontractor's other clients (the developers who hired him directly) discover the pattern through their own consultants. The competitor's own clients become your enforcement mechanism — you do not have to litigate; the market will.

### 4. Wait at leisure for the exhausted enemy (以逸待劳)

Do not chase the opponent. Let him come to you, exhausted by travel, by his own supply chain, or by his own internal conflicts. The resting defender has the advantage.

*Ravages of Time example*: Sima Yi's campaign against Zhuge Liang's Northern Expeditions. Sima Yi refused to engage. Zhuge Liang sent women's clothing to insult him; Sima Yi accepted the clothing. Zhuge Liang's army exhausted itself in supply lines across the Qinling mountains; Sima Yi's army stayed fed and rested. Zhuge Liang died of exhaustion at Wuzhang Plains.

*Contractor application*: When a competitor is bidding aggressively on every project in your market — burning cash, burning relationships, burning subcontractors — do not match the bid. Wait. The competitor will exhaust his capital and his subcontractor relationships within 12-18 months. When he collapses, his clients will come to you, and you will bid from a rested position.

### 5. Loot a burning house (趁火打劫)

When the opponent is in genuine crisis (a cash crunch, a regulatory action, a public scandal), press the advantage. The opponent cannot fight on two fronts.

*Ravages of Time example*: Cao Cao's absorption of Yuan Shao's forces after the defeat at Guandu. Yuan Shao was already broken internally; Cao Cao moved to capture Yuan Shao's territory before the sons could consolidate, then exploited the rivalry between Yuan Tan and Yuan Shang to finish both.

*Contractor application*: When a competitor loses a major project to litigation or regulatory action, his subcontractors become available, his site engineers become available, his clients need a new contractor. Move within 30 days. The competitor cannot defend a position he is no longer holding.

### 6. Make a sound in the east, then strike in the west (声东击西)

Direct the opponent's attention to a false target. Strike where his attention is not.

*Ravages of Time example*: Sun Ce's feint toward Liu Biao in Jingzhou while actually consolidating Jiangdong. The warlords who feared Sun Ce would attack them westward missed the real movement south and east — and by the time they noticed, Sun Ce had built a kingdom.

*Contractor application*: When bidding on a major project, publicly indicate interest in a different project (one you do not actually want). Competitors will allocate their bidding attention to the project you have signaled, leaving the project you actually want with fewer bidders. This is misdirection of competitive intelligence, fully ethical within the rules of competitive bidding.

## Chapter 2 — Enemy-Dealing Stratagems (Stratagems 7–12)

These stratagems exploit the opponent's false beliefs about your intentions.

### 7. Create something out of nothing (无中生有)

Fabricate a threat or an opportunity that does not exist. The opponent reacts to the fabrication, exposing his real positions and priorities.

*Ravages of Time example*: Zhuge Liang's "empty fort strategy" at Xicheng. With only a small garrison, he opened the gates and played the lute on the wall. Sima Yi, believing Zhuge Liang would not be so exposed unless he had an ambush, withdrew. Zhuge Liang created a threat (the ambush) out of nothing (an empty fort).

*Contractor application*: When you suspect a competitor is preparing to enter a market segment you dominate, leak (through an industry contact, not directly) that you are about to launch a major initiative in that segment. If the competitor reacts by delaying entry, you have bought time without spending capital. If the competitor accelerates entry, you have learned that his commitment is real, and you can plan accordingly.

### 8. Openly repair the plank road, but sneak through Chencang (暗度陈仓)

Announce one move (the obvious, visible one) while secretly executing another. The opponent defends against the announced move; you arrive through the unannounced one.

*Ravages of Time example*: Han Xin's stratagem that gives this tactic its name — pretending to repair the plank road through the mountains (announced) while actually moving his army through Chencang (secret). In the Ravages corpus, Sima Yi's early career follows this pattern: he publicly served Cao Cao as a scholar-official while privately building the network of alliances and military experience that would later enable the Sima clan's seizure of power.

*Contractor application*: Publicly commit to one specialty (e.g., "we are focused on residential electrical"). Privately develop capability in a second specialty (e.g., solar PV installation). When the market shifts, you can pivot without announcement; competitors who tracked only your public positioning are caught flat-footed.

### 9. Watch the fire from across the river (隔岸观火)

When two opponents fight each other, do not intervene. Let them exhaust each other. Then move.

*Ravages of Time example*: Cao Cao watching the warlord civil wars in the north after Dong Zhuo's fall. While Yuan Shao and Gongsun Zan destroyed each other, Cao Cao consolidated Yan province. By the time Yuan Shao turned south, Cao Cao was already strong enough to win at Guandu.

*Contractor application*: When two developers engage in a price war (or a bidding war for land, or a litigation war), do not ally with either. Both will be weakened; both will need suppliers who can offer flexible terms. Position yourself as the supplier who works with both, and when the war ends, both will owe you.

### 10. Hide a knife in a smile (笑里藏刀)

Present a warm, friendly surface while maintaining a hard, adversarial intent underneath. The opponent relaxes his guard; the strike, when it comes, is uncontested.

*Ravages of Time example*: Lu Bu's repeated betrayals — first of Ding Yuan (his adoptive father), then of Dong Zhuo (his second adoptive father). Lu Bu presented himself as a loyal son to each; each was killed by him. The Ravages narrative treats this as a warning: a surface of warmth with no internal loyalty is a knife in a smile.

*Contractor application*: When a client or competitor has wronged you (a late payment, an undercut bid), do not show your anger. Maintain the surface of the relationship. Document everything. When the leverage shifts (you have an opportunity they need, or they need a favor), the documentation becomes the knife — not as vengeance, but as the price of future cooperation. The client who wronged you and now needs you will pay a premium for the same warmth he previously abused.

### 11. Let the plum tree wither in place of the peach (李代桃僵)

Sacrifice a part to save the whole. When you cannot save everything, identify what must be sacrificed and cut it cleanly, before the opponent forces the loss on worse terms.

*Ravages of Time example*: Liu Bei's abandonment of his family at Changban to escape Cao Cao's cavalry. A brutal decision; but a Liu Bei who stopped to save his family would have been captured, and the family would have been captured with him. The sacrifice of the personal (in the moment) preserved the political (the long-game claim to legitimacy). He retrieved his family later; he could not have retrieved a captured empire.

*Contractor application*: When a project goes bad (a cost overrun you cannot recover), abandon it cleanly. Pay what you owe, settle with the subcontractors, walk away. Do not "rescue" a bad project by sinking more capital into it — you will lose the project *and* the capital, *and* the reputation. The clean loss preserves the firm for the next project.

### 12. Seize the opportunity to lead the sheep away (顺手牵羊)

Take small advantages as they appear, even if they are not the main objective. Accumulated small gains become large gains.

*Ravages of Time example*: Cao Cao's constant collection of small advantages — every surrendered warlord's troops absorbed, every defeated general recruited (Zhang Liao, Xu Huang), every minor territory annexed. None of these alone was decisive; cumulatively, they built the army that won at Guandu.

*Contractor application*: When a project site has leftover material from another contractor, offer to buy it at a discount. When a subcontractor you respect is between projects, hire him on retainer for two months. When a developer's consultant is unsure about a code requirement, offer free advice. None of these is a deal; cumulatively, they build a position that competitors who focus only on the "big deal" cannot match.

## Chapter 3 — Attacking Stratagems (Stratagems 13–18)

These stratagems attack the opponent's structure, alliances, or psychology.

### 13. Beat the grass to startle the snakes (打草惊蛇)

Probe the situation before committing. The opponent who is startled reveals his position, his strength, his response pattern.

*Ravages of Time example*: Yuan Fang's careful probing of Yuan Shao's inner court before committing to a course of action. By floating test proposals, he identified which advisors were loyal, which were bought, which were weak — and he arranged his strategy to exploit the revealed map.

*Contractor application*: Before bidding on a major project with a developer you don't know, send a minor query (a question about the bid documents, a request for clarification on a spec). The developer's response tells you how they treat contractors, how fast their consultants respond, how hierarchical their decision process is. If they ignore the query, you have learned something important — do not bid.

### 14. Borrow a corpse to resurrect the soul (借尸还魂)

Use a defunct institution, brand, or relationship as the vehicle for a new initiative. The opponent does not see the new content because they recognize the old container.

*Ravages of Time example*: Liu Bei's entire strategy of legitimacy. He had no army, no territory, no money — but he had the imperial surname Liu. He used the corpse of Han legitimacy to resurrect the soul of his own claim, eventually building Shu-Han on the basis of being the "true" continuation of the Han.

*Contractor application*: When you want to enter a market segment that has high barriers to entry, acquire or partner with a small, established-but-struggling contractor in that segment. You are not buying his capability (which is exhausted); you are buying his license, his references, and his client list. The "corpse" is his business; the "soul" is your capital and execution. The market sees continuity; you get a head start.

### 15. Lure the tiger off the mountain (调虎离山)

Do not attack the opponent where he is strong. Induce him to leave his strong position, then attack him where he is weak.

*Ravages of Time example*: Yuan Fang's various maneuvers to draw enemy commanders out of fortified positions into the open. The tiger in his den is invincible; the tiger on the plain is just another large animal.

*Contractor application*: If a competitor dominates a particular client relationship and you cannot break it, do not try to break it directly. Instead, attract the client to a different kind of project — a project the competitor cannot execute. The client, on the new ground, has no loyalty to the competitor on that ground, and you win on neutral terrain.

### 16. When the snipe and clam grapple, the fisherman profits (鹬蚌相争，渔翁得利)

Two opponents in conflict exhaust each other; a third party takes the prize. Position yourself as the third party.

*Ravages of Time example*: Sun Quan and Liu Bei's territorial disputes over Jingzhou — both spent resources contesting the province, while Cao Cao consolidated the north. The third party (Cao Cao) profited from the two opponents' mutual exhaustion.

*Contractor application*: When two large contractors engage in a bid war for a major project (one that you cannot win), do not bid. Wait for the winner to discover the project is harder than they priced. Then approach the developer with an offer to take over the difficult scope at a premium. The bid war was the snipe-clam fight; the scope takeover is the fisherman's profit.

### 17. Cast a brick to attract jade (抛砖引玉)

Sacrifice something modest to gain something valuable. The small investment attracts the larger reward.

*Ravages of Time example*: Liu Bei's three visits to Zhuge Liang's cottage — each visit a small public gesture of humility. The "brick" was the public humility; the "jade" was Zhuge Liang's lifetime service. The investment of three visits bought a kingdom.

*Contractor application*: Take one small project at cost (no margin) for a high-profile client. The "brick" is the foregone profit; the "jade" is the reference, the photo on your portfolio, and the introduction to that client's network. The next project from the same client or his network is at full margin.

### 18. Defeat the enemy by capturing their chief (擒贼擒王)

Do not dissipate effort against the rank-and-file. Identify the decision-maker, the leader, the strategic node. Defeat (or win over) the chief, and the rest follows.

*Ravages of Time example*: Every successful campaign in the Ravages narrative focuses on the commander, not the army. Cao Cao at Guandu attacked Yuan Shao's supply depot at Wuchao (the chief's source of strength), not Yuan Shao's main army. When the depot burned, Yuan Shao's army collapsed without a battle.

*Contractor application*: In any deal, identify who actually makes the decision. The "decision-maker" is rarely the person you are negotiating with. It may be the developer's wife, the consultant's senior partner, the funder's risk officer. Spend your relationship effort on the chief, not on the envoy.

## Chapter 4 — Chaos Stratagems (Stratagems 19–24)

These stratagems create or exploit chaos in the opponent's structure.

### 19. Take away the fire from under the cauldron (釜底抽薪)

Do not fight the symptoms; remove the source. The cauldron stops boiling not when you stir the water but when you remove the fire.

*Ravages of Time example*: Jia Xu's advice to Zhang Xiu against Cao Cao: rather than fight Cao Cao's vanguard (the boiling water), attack his supply lines (the fire). Without supplies, the vanguard could not fight.

*Contractor application*: When a competitor is undercutting your bids across multiple projects, do not match his discounts on each project (stirring the water). Find the source of his cost advantage — a supplier who gives him exceptional terms, a labor source you can also access — and remove or match the source. The discounts will end on their own.

### 20. Catch fish in troubled waters (混水摸鱼)

When the situation is chaotic, the well-positioned party takes advantages that would be impossible in stable conditions.

*Ravages of Time example*: The general chaos after Dong Zhuo's death — Li Jue and Guo Si rampaging through Chang'an — created the conditions in which Cao Cao consolidated the emperor's person. In stable conditions, the emperor would have been protected by a strong coalition. In chaos, no one could prevent Cao Cao's move.

*Contractor application*: When the Egyptian market enters a phase of disruption (currency devaluation, regulatory change, a major contractor's collapse), move aggressively. The competitors who are paralyzed by the chaos cannot respond; the clients who need reliable contractors in the chaos will pay premiums. Stable-market discipline ("wait and see") is the wrong strategy in chaos; chaos rewards the decisive.

### 21. The cicada sheds its golden shell (金蝉脱壳)

Create the appearance of remaining while actually departing. The opponent wastes effort attacking your shell.

*Ravages of Time example*: Zhuge Liang's withdrawal from Wuzhang Plains after his death — the army was ordered to march out, display banners, and then quietly withdraw under cover of the appearance of presence. Sima Yi, who feared a trap, did not pursue.

*Contractor application*: When you are exiting a market segment (because it has become unprofitable), maintain a public posture of activity for one more quarter. The competitor who would attack your departing clients is delayed by the appearance of presence. By the time he realizes you have left, you have moved your clients and your team.

### 22. Shut the door to catch the thief (关门捉贼)

Allow the opponent to enter a position, then close the exit. The opponent, trapped, must surrender or be destroyed on your terms.

*Ravages of Time example*: Cao Cao's encirclement at Guandu — he allowed Yuan Shao's forces to extend their supply lines deep into Cao territory, then cut the supply lines at Wuchao. Yuan Shao's army, having advanced too far, was trapped between Cao Cao's main force and the burned depot.

*Contractor application*: In a negotiation, allow the other party to commit resources to the deal (legal review, financing, internal approvals) before you reveal your firm bottom line. Once they have invested in the deal, they will accept worse terms rather than walk away. The investment in the deal is the closed door.

### 23. Befriend a distant state and attack a neighboring one (远交近攻)

Alliance with far powers; war with near powers. The far power cannot directly threaten you; the near power can. Build the far alliance, then defeat the near.

*Ravages of Time example*: Qin's unification strategy — far alliances to isolate the target, then conquest of the near target. In the Ravages era, Cao Cao's diplomacy with the southern warlords (Sun Quan, Liu Biao) while he concentrated on the northern warlords (Yuan Shao) is the canonical case.

*Contractor application*: Do not attack the contractor in the next district; you share subcontractors, you share suppliers, your markets overlap. Attack (compete hard against) the contractor two regions away, with whom you do not share infrastructure. The contractor next door is a potential alliance partner; the contractor far away is a pure competitor.

### 24. Borrow a passage to attack Guo (假道伐虢)

Request a permission (a "passage") from a third party that you do not actually need, in order to position yourself to attack that third party. The third party, having granted the passage, is unprepared.

*Ravages of Time example*: The classic case from the *Zuo Zhuan* (referred to in the Ravages corpus) — Jin borrowed a passage through Yu to attack Guo; having defeated Guo, Jin turned around and attacked Yu on the return march. Both were destroyed by a single permission.

*Contractor application*: When a developer has multiple project sites and you want a particular one, request to be "considered" for a different site (the passage). The developer's attention and negotiation effort goes to the site you do not want. When the developer has invested in the negotiation for that site, switch to the site you actually want — the developer, having invested, prefers to give you a project rather than lose the whole relationship.

## Chapter 5 — Proximate Stratagems (Stratagems 25–30)

These stratagems manipulate the structure of alliances and identifications.

### 25. Replace the beams and pillars with rotten timber (偷梁换柱)

Substitute the essential support of the opponent's structure with weak material. The structure looks intact; it cannot bear weight.

*Ravages of Time example*: Yuan Shao's inner court — Yuan Shao believed he was supported by capable advisors (the beams). In reality, the rival factions (Shen Pei vs. Guo Tu) had replaced solid advice with factional manipulation (rotten timber). Yuan Shao's "structure" looked intact to him, but it could not bear the weight of the Guandu campaign.

*Contractor application*: When a competitor's firm depends on a key engineer or project manager, do not poach him. Instead, recruit the people around him (the assistants, the junior engineers). The key engineer stays, but his support structure is gone. His output drops; the competitor does not understand why. You have not taken the beam; you have taken the supports.

### 26. Point at the mulberry and curse the locust (指桑骂槐)

Punish or criticize one party as a warning to another. The party warned understands the message without being directly attacked.

*Ravages of Time example*: Cao Cao's execution of officials who failed at logistics during the campaign against Yuan Shao — the executions were nominally about specific failures, but the message to the rest of the administration was clear: do not fail in this campaign. The mulberry was the executed official; the locust was the rest of the court.

*Contractor application*: When one subcontractor is late, do not absorb the delay quietly. Document it, send a formal letter, and copy (transparently, not in secret) the other subcontractors on the project. The "punishment" of the late subcontractor is the warning to the others — they will not be late on the next milestone.

### 27. Feign madness but remain in control (假痴不癫)

Appear foolish, incompetent, or confused while maintaining full internal clarity. The opponent underestimates you; his underestimate creates openings.

*Ravages of Time example*: Sima Yi's decade-plus of appearing to be a sick, withdrawn scholar under Cao Cao, Cao Pi, and Cao Rui. He appeared to be politically inactive; in reality, he was building the network that would, in one decisive move at the Gaoling Tombs Incident (249 CE), seize the entire Cao Wei state. The "madness" was the cover for the most consequential strategic operation in Three Kingdoms history.

*Contractor application*: Do not advertise your strategic ambition. If you intend to dominate the New Capital market in 5 years, do not announce it. Behave as a regional contractor who is "happy with what we have." The competitors who watch you see no threat; the developers who would resist your rise see no rise. When the position is consolidated, the competitors learn about it too late to react.

### 28. Climb onto the roof and remove the ladder (上屋抽梯)

Place the opponent in a position from which he cannot retreat, then attack. The ladder is removed *after* the opponent has climbed.

*Ravages of Time example*: Zhuge Liang's various schemes that lured enemy commanders into positions where retreat was impossible. The commander who has crossed a river that is then destroying the bridge has no exit.

*Contractor application*: In a negotiation with a difficult client who has tried to renegotiate after signing, do not respond to the renegotiation. Instead, quietly begin to execute the original contract (mobilize, order materials). Once the client has accepted your mobilization, he cannot easily switch contractors — he has invested in your presence on site. The renegotiation collapses because the client has climbed the roof and you have removed the ladder.

### 29. Deck the tree with false blossoms (树上开花)

Create the appearance of strength you do not have. The illusion, if credible, deters attack.

*Ravages of Time example*: Liu Bei's early career — small army, no territory, but a retinue of famous warriors (Guan Yu, Zhang Fei) and a reputation (the imperial surname) that made every warlord hesitate before attacking him. The "tree" (Liu Bei's actual forces) was small; the "blossoms" (the reputation, the generals' fame) made it appear larger.

*Contractor application*: When your firm is small but you want to bid on large projects, create the appearance of capacity you do not yet have: a polished website, professional engineering drawings, references from past projects (even small ones), partnerships with named subcontractors. The "blossoms" are the appearance; the "tree" grows to match the appearance once you win a project. The appearance is not deception of the client about the work — it is presentation of capability you can deliver.

### 30. Make the guest the host (反客为主)

Start as the weaker party in a relationship; through patience and incremental leverage, become the dominant party. The guest becomes the host.

*Ravages of Time example*: Liu Bei's takeover of Yi province from Liu Zhang. Liu Bei entered as a guest (invited to defend against Zhang Lu); through incremental build-up of local support, he became the host, and Liu Zhang was expelled. The transition took years; the move, when it came, was one dinner.

*Contractor application*: When you join a developer's panel of contractors, you are the guest — the developer has the relationship, the power, the choice. Through steady delivery, dependable advice, and consistent pricing, you become the developer's preferred contractor. After three projects, you are the host — the developer comes to you first; other contractors are invited only if you decline. The transition is the strategy.

## Chapter 6 — Defeat Stratagems (Stratagems 31–36)

These are the most profound stratagems — they describe how to act when you are losing, how to convert defeat into future advantage, and how to disengage without final loss. The strategic mind must master these before the others, because every career contains defeats.

### 31. The beauty trap (honey trap) (美人计)

Distract the opponent with a temptation — not necessarily a literal beauty, but anything that corrupts his discipline and judgment. The opponent, distracted, makes errors.

*Ravages of Time example*: Dia Chan. Wang Yun offered Dia Chan to both Dong Zhuo and Lu Bu, knowing the resulting jealousy would destroy the partnership. The "beauty" was not just Dia Chan's person; it was the temptation of possession that drove Dong Zhuo and Lu Bu apart. The stratagem destroyed the most powerful warlord in the realm through his own bodyguard.

*Contractor application*: A competitor who is hard to beat on quality and price can sometimes be beaten by giving him an opportunity that he cannot resist — a large project at generous terms that he will over-commit to. The over-commitment stretches his cash and his team. You do not win by defeating him; you win by giving him an opportunity he defeats himself with.

### 32. The empty fort strategy (空城计)

When you are weakest, appear strongest. When you have nothing, appear to have an ambush. The opponent, believing the appearance, retreats.

*Ravages of Time example*: Zhuge Liang at Xicheng — described above under Stratagem 7. The deeper Ravages treatment: Sima Yi's decision to retreat was not foolish; he could not afford to risk that Zhuge Liang was bluffing. The expected cost of an ambush exceeded the expected value of a quick victory. Zhuge Liang exploited Sima Yi's rationality, not his foolishness.

*Contractor application*: When a project is going badly and you have no good options, present the appearance of full control. The subcontractor who suspects you are over-extended will demand payment up front; the subcontractor who believes you are in control will accept your standard terms. The appearance is not a lie about your finances (which would be fraud); it is the disciplined management of perception during a vulnerable moment.

### 33. Let the enemy's own spy sow discord in his camp (反间计)

Do not try to destroy the opponent's alliance from outside. Insert (or permit) a seed of discord that grows from inside.

*Ravages of Time example*: Cao Cao's interception of Yuan Shao's letter to his subordinates at Guandu — instead of exposing the letter, he destroyed it unread, allowing Yuan Shao's advisors to suspect each other. The paranoia did more damage than the discovery would have. Yuan Shao's court destroyed itself.

*Contractor application*: When a competitor's firm has internal tensions (between the founder and a senior engineer, between two departments), do not exploit the tension directly. Quietly hire one of the secondary parties — the resulting internal recrimination in the competitor's firm will do more damage than any direct competitive move.

### 34. Inflict pain on yourself to win the enemy's trust (苦肉计)

Demonstrate harm to yourself to gain the opponent's trust. The opponent, believing you are a victim, lowers his guard.

*Ravages of Time example*: Huang Gai's flogging at the order of Zhou Yu before the Battle of Red Cliffs. The flogging was staged (with Huang Gai's consent) to convince Cao Cao's spy that Huang Gai was disaffected. Cao Cao believed the apparent victim; Huang Gai's subsequent "defection" was the fire ship that destroyed Cao Cao's fleet.

*Contractor application*: This stratagem is dangerous in the contractor context because the user's Islamic ethics forbid self-harm as a manipulation. The permissible version: when a developer publicly mistreats you (a complaint, a late payment), do not retaliate publicly. The mistreatment, endured visibly, builds sympathy with the other developers and consultants in the market who have heard of you. The pain is real; the strategic value of the visible pain is the trust you gain from the wider market.

### 35. Chain stratagems (连环计)

Combine multiple stratagems into a sequence. The opponent, having defended against one, is exposed to the next; having defended against the second, is exposed to the third.

*Ravages of Time example*: Wang Yun's orchestration of Dia Chan's use — first to inflame Lu Bu against Dong Zhuo, then to enable Lu Bu's assassination of Dong Zhuo, then to position Lu Bu for Wang Yun's own subsequent maneuvering. Each stratagem set up the next. The chain was the strategy; the individual stratagems were links.

*Contractor application*: A market entry often requires a chain: first win a small reference project (Stratagem 17, brick to attract jade), then use the reference to win a medium project (Stratagem 30, guest to host), then use the medium project's revenue to fund a bid for a flagship project (Stratagem 29, deck the tree with false blossoms). Each link depends on the previous; the chain is the strategy.

### 36. If all else fails, retreat (走为上)

When the situation is hopeless, retreat is the best stratagem. Retreat preserves the army to fight another day. The general who refuses to retreat when retreat is necessary loses the army — and the war.

*Ravages of Time example*: Liu Bei's repeated retreats — from his early base in Xu province, from Jingzhou, from Changban. Each retreat looked like a defeat; each preserved Liu Bei's life and his small core of loyalists, who became the foundation of Shu-Han. The general who never retreats cannot survive a strategic surprise; the general who retreats well cannot be destroyed.

*Contractor application*: Some projects cannot be saved. Some clients cannot be satisfied. Some markets cannot be entered profitably. The strategic mind recognizes when retreat is the only winning move. The contractor who walks away from a bad project in month 2 loses EGP 500,000; the contractor who refuses to retreat and tries to "save" the project loses EGP 5,000,000 and a year of his career. The retreat is not a defeat — it is the stratagem that preserves the firm for the next opportunity.

## The meta-stratagem

The 36 Stratagems are not a menu. They are a vocabulary. The strategic mind does not ask "which stratagem should I use?" — it asks "what does the opponent believe, and which stratagem exploits that belief?" The stratagem follows from the opponent's model of the situation, not from your preference.

Two warnings:

**1. Stratagems without substance fail.** A contractor who relies on stratagems but has no execution capacity will be discovered. The stratagem buys time; execution wins the war. Sun Tzu's principle (file `sun_tzu_applied.md` Principle 1) and the 36 Stratagems operate together: preparation (substance) creates the conditions that make stratagems effective.

**2. Stratagems used against clients backfire.** The 36 Stratagems are written for war, where the opponent is an enemy. In business, the client is not an enemy — the client is a partner in a transaction. Stratagems against competitors are permissible; stratagems against clients erode the legitimacy that is your long-term advantage (Constant 7, `01_CORE/constants_of_power.md`).

## Applied to the Egyptian contractor

Three concrete applications of the 36 Stratagems framework in the next 90 days:

1. **Stratagem 36 (Retreat) audit**: List every active project, client, and supplier relationship. Identify any that should be retreated from in the next 90 days. Plan the retreat with the same care as a campaign — the worst retreat is an unplanned one.

2. **Stratagem 30 (Guest to host) campaign**: Identify one developer with whom you are currently the "guest" (a secondary contractor). Plan a 12-month sequence of three projects that ends with you as the preferred contractor.

3. **Stratagem 27 (Feign madness) posture**: For the next 6 months, do not announce any strategic ambition publicly. Quietly execute; quietly build. Let competitors underestimate you. The underestimation is the resource you spend later.
