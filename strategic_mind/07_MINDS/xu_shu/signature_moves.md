# Xu Shu — Signature Moves

> **Layer**: 07_MINDS/xu_shu/
> **File**: signature_moves
> **Subject**: The specific tactical moves Xu Shu is known for, that the system can invoke
> **Mention frequency in manga**: 26 times across 635 chapters
> **First appearance**: Chapter 376+ (referenced in Liu Bei's Xinye recruitment arc, in the Cai Mao / Xiangyang episode — "Xu Shu, has made his first move… a success.")

---

## The 7 Signature Moves

Each of these moves is a tactical pattern Xu Shu repeatedly uses or embodies. They are not metaphors — they are concrete patterns that can be applied to specific situations, especially those involving constrained service, family obligation, talent-spotting, and the integrity of withholding under pressure.

### Move 1: The Recommendation of Zhuge Liang

**The pattern**: When the strategist's direct service to the patron is constrained or ending, identify the one person whose service would serve the patron better, and connect them to the patron. The recommendation is the substitute for the strategy the strategist cannot give. The act leverages the strategist's judgment (which the constraint cannot take) to multiply the patron's service (which the constraint prevents the strategist from providing).

**Historical example**: Xu Shu, having been forced to leave Liu Bei's service after his mother was captured by Cao Cao (208 AD), recommended Zhuge Liang to Liu Bei before departing. The recommendation was not a casual introduction; it was a sustained persuasion, including the famous (in the Romance tradition) insistence that Liu Bei must visit Zhuge Liang three times — the three-shi-cao (三顧茅廬, "three visits to the thatched cottage"). The recommendation was the act that gave Liu Bei the advisor who would shape the next two decades of Shu's strategy. Xu Shu's direct service to Liu Bei had been brief; the recommendation multiplied the contribution twenty-fold.

**Mechanism**: The recommendation leverages the strategist's judgment without depending on the strategist's service. The judgment is the strategist's alone — the constraint cannot take it. The service is the recommended person's — the constraint does not apply to them (yet). The recommendation bridges the constraint: it gives the patron what the strategist cannot give (the service of the better advisor) by means of what the strategist can give (the judgment of who the better advisor is). The bridge is the move. The mechanism is the multiplication of contribution through the substitute.

**Application in contracting**:
- A contractor who has been forced by family obligation to relocate away from a long-standing client's market can still serve the client by recommending a replacement contractor — one whose work the relocating contractor knows and respects, whose values match the client's, whose capabilities fit the client's needs. The recommendation, if correct, gives the client a contractor who will serve the client's next decade. The relocating contractor's contribution to the client is, through the recommendation, larger than it would have been through continued direct service (which would have been limited by the family obligation's distraction).
- A contractor who has been bound by a constraint (a contract that prevents competitive bidding in a segment, a financial dependency that prevents taking on new clients) can still serve the constrained-out clients by recommending other contractors for them. The recommendation does not violate the constraint (the constrained contractor is not bidding; the constrained contractor is recommending). The recommendation serves the client (the client gets a contractor). The recommendation serves the recommended contractor (the recommended contractor gets the work). The recommendation is the contribution the constraint permits.
- A contractor whose own business is small but whose judgment of other contractors is respected can build a reputation as a talent-spotter — the contractor to whom other contractors' clients come for recommendations. The reputation is the asset the constraint (small business) cannot take. The recommendation becomes the contractor's signature contribution.

**Risks**: The recommendation can be wrong. The recommended contractor may not perform to the standard the recommender expected; the recommended contractor may turn out to have flaws the recommender did not know. The recommendation's correctness depends on the recommender's judgment, and judgment is fallible. The risk is reputational (the recommender's name is associated with the recommendation) and relational (the client may blame the recommender for the recommendation's failure). The mitigation is to recommend conservatively — to recommend only contractors whose work the recommender has directly observed, and to be honest with the client about the limits of the recommender's knowledge.

**Ethics check**: The recommendation is itself an ethical move — the act of connecting the patron with the better service. There is no ethical violation in the recommendation if the recommendation is honest (the recommender believes the recommended contractor is the right one) and undivorced from compensation (the recommender is not being paid by the recommended contractor to recommend them, which would be a kickback disguised as a recommendation). The ethical question is whether the recommendation serves the patron or serves the recommender's interests. The discipline is to recommend for the patron's benefit, even when the recommendation costs the recommender (e.g., recommending a contractor who will compete with the recommender in other markets).

### Move 2: The Vow of Silence

**The pattern**: When the strategist is forced to serve a master the strategist cannot in conscience advise, take a private vow to withhold the strategic contribution the master wants. The vow is specific (the plan is withheld; the service is performed), silent (not announced to the master), and open-ended (lasting for the duration of the constraint). The vow preserves the integrity without breaking the service.

**Historical example**: Xu Shu's vow, recorded in the Romance tradition, was that he would never give Cao Cao a single plan. The vow was kept for the duration of Xu Shu's service to Cao Cao and Cao Pi — approximately two decades, from 208 AD to his death in the late 220s or early 230s AD. The vow was not announced to Cao Cao (Cao Cao would have punished the declaration); it was simply lived. The vow was the moral limit preserved within the constraint.

**Mechanism**: The vow splits the service into two parts: the part the constraint requires (presence, role, performance of duties) and the part the constraint does not require (the strategic contribution the master wants). The vow withholds the second part. The splitting is what makes the vow possible — the strategist who tried to withhold both parts would break the constraint; the strategist who withheld neither part would break the moral limit. The vow is the precise withholding that respects both. The mechanism is the splitting; the splitting is the discipline.

**Application in contracting**:
- A contractor who is bound to a developer by financial dependency (the developer provides most of the contractor's revenue; the contractor's family depends on the revenue) and who recognizes that the developer is engaging in practices the contractor cannot endorse (substandard materials represented as standard, falsified safety inspections, hidden defects) takes the vow: continue to perform the contracted electrical work (the constraint requires this), decline to advise the developer on how to represent the substandard materials (the moral limit withholds this), decline to participate in the falsification of inspections (the moral limit withholds this), decline to sign documents that misrepresent the work (the moral limit withholds this). The vow is the splitting: the contracted work is performed; the complicity is withheld. The vow is not announced; it is lived. The vow lasts for the duration of the constraint (until the financial dependency is reduced).
- A contractor who is bound to a partner by a debt the partner holds (the partner lent the contractor money to start the business; the partner now uses the debt to demand the contractor's participation in practices the contractor considers unethical) takes the vow: continue to perform the partnership obligations (the constraint requires this), decline to participate in the specific practices the partner demands (the moral limit withholds this). The vow is the splitting. The vow is not announced. The vow lasts until the debt is repaid.

**Risks**: The vow, over years, costs the strategist. The cost includes the personal cost (the stress of withholding under pressure), the professional cost (the loss of contribution to the field, the stunted career), the relational cost (the strain with the master who wants the contribution), and the moral cost (the gradual complicity of presence). The vow that does not account for these costs will break under them. The vow that accounts for them (by periodic reassessment, by external accountability, by self-care) can survive. The risk is also that the vow, kept too long, becomes the rigidity that prevents the strategist from recognizing when the constraint has changed and the vow can be reconsidered.

**Ethics check**: The vow is itself an ethical move — the act of preserving integrity under constraint. There is no ethical violation in the vow if the vow's scope is precisely the moral limit (the vow does not exceed the moral limit, and the vow is not narrower than the moral limit). The ethical question is whether the vow is being kept for integrity or for comfort. The vow kept for integrity includes the willingness to reassess when the constraint changes. The vow kept for comfort includes the refusal to reassess, even when the constraint has clearly changed. The discipline is to keep the vow for integrity, which means being willing to end the vow when the constraint ends.

### Move 3: The Forced Service (Accepting the Constraint)

**The pattern**: When the constraint is real (family at risk, financial dependency, contractual obligation) and cannot be escaped without violating a deeper obligation, accept the constraint. Go to the master. Take the role. Perform the service. Accept the constraint fully — do not pretend it does not exist, do not pretend it is smaller than it is, do not pretend the strategist is free when the strategist is bound. The acceptance is the precondition of the vow. The strategist who does not accept the constraint cannot take the vow, because the vow is the discipline of integrity within the constraint, and integrity within a constraint one denies is not integrity.

**Historical example**: Xu Shu, on learning that his mother had been captured by Cao Cao (or, in some accounts, on receiving his mother's letter — which may have been forged at Cao Cao's order), did not refuse to go. He went. He left Liu Bei's service, traveled to Cao Cao's camp, and entered Cao Cao's service. The acceptance was the constraint honored. The acceptance was also the precondition of the vow — the vow was the integrity Xu Shu maintained within the acceptance. The strategist who refused to go (and abandoned the mother) would have violated the family obligation. The strategist who went and gave Cao Cao plans would have violated the moral limit. The strategist who went and did not give Cao Cao plans (Xu Shu's choice) honored both. The going was the acceptance; the not-giving was the vow; the combination was the forced service.

**Mechanism**: The acceptance converts the constraint from an external force to an internal decision. The strategist who is dragged to the master's camp has not accepted the constraint; the strategist who goes, knowing the cost, has accepted it. The acceptance is the act of agency within the constraint — the recognition that the constraint is binding, and the choice to honor the binding rather than resist it ineffectively. The acceptance is what makes the vow possible, because the vow is the integrity the strategist maintains within the accepted constraint. The vow without the acceptance is the vow of a strategist who is in denial about the constraint; the vow with the acceptance is the vow of a strategist who knows the constraint and chooses integrity within it.

**Application in contracting**:
- A contractor whose parent has fallen ill and requires the contractor's presence in a city where the contractor's market is poor accepts the constraint: moves to the city, takes the local work available, performs the local role. The acceptance does not extend to participating in local practices the contractor considers unethical; the vow withholds that. But the acceptance does extend to being present, performing the work, fulfilling the family obligation. The contractor who denied the constraint (refused to move, abandoned the parent) would have violated the family obligation; the contractor who moved and gave the local developers unethical advice would have violated the moral limit; the contractor who moved and did not give unethical advice (the Xu Shu move) honors both.
- A contractor whose former partner has called in a debt the contractor cannot pay without continued service to the partner accepts the constraint: continues the partnership, performs the partnership work. The acceptance does not extend to participating in the partner's unethical practices; the vow withholds that. But the acceptance does extend to fulfilling the partnership obligations until the debt is repaid. The contractor who denied the constraint (refused to continue the partnership, defaulted on the debt) would have violated the financial obligation; the contractor who continued and participated in the unethical practices would have violated the moral limit; the contractor who continued and did not participate in the unethical practices (the Xu Shu move) honors both.

**Risks**: The acceptance can become the resignation. The strategist who accepts the constraint may, over time, cease to look for the end of the constraint — may cease to plan for the parent's recovery, cease to plan for the debt's repayment, cease to look for the exit. The acceptance, intended as the precondition of the vow, becomes the abandonment of the strategist's agency. The mitigation is to pair the acceptance with the duration analysis (Step 6 of the thought pattern): the strategist accepts the constraint, takes the vow, and simultaneously plans for the end of the constraint — the moment when the family obligation is fulfilled, the debt is repaid, the contract expires. The acceptance is for the duration; the duration is not forever.

**Ethics check**: The acceptance is itself an ethical move — the act of honoring an obligation the strategist is bound by. There is no ethical violation in the acceptance if the constraint is real (not a rationalization for a choice the strategist wanted to make anyway) and the acceptance is bounded by the vow (the acceptance does not extend to the moral limit). The ethical question is whether the acceptance is honest (the constraint is real) and bounded (the moral limit is preserved). The discipline is to keep the acceptance honest and bounded.

### Move 4: The Talent-Scout's Departure

**The pattern**: When the strategist must leave the patron, the leaving can itself be a contribution if the strategist leaves by recommending the better successor. The departure is not the abandonment; the departure is the act of connecting the patron with the service the strategist could not provide. The talent-scout's departure is the leaving that is also a service.

**Historical example**: Xu Shu's departure from Liu Bei was, in the Romance tradition, accompanied by the recommendation of Zhuge Liang. The leaving was the constraint (the captured mother); the recommendation was the contribution (the better advisor for Liu Bei). The leaving and the recommendation were a single act — the talent-scout's departure. The strategist who left without recommending would have abandoned the patron; the strategist who recommended without leaving would have remained in the constraint. The combination — leaving and recommending — was the move that converted the forced departure into the chosen contribution.

**Mechanism**: The departure converts the constraint from a loss into a service. The patron's loss (the strategist's leaving) is offset by the patron's gain (the better successor's arrival). The strategist's loss (the constrained service) is offset by the strategist's gain (the contribution of the recommendation). The departure, paired with the recommendation, is the move that turns the constraint into a positive sum — both the patron and the strategist benefit. The mechanism is the pairing; the pairing is the move.

**Application in contracting**:
- A contractor who must leave a long-standing client relationship (because of relocation, because of the financial dependency's end, because of a constraint that prevents continued service) leaves by recommending a replacement — a contractor the leaving contractor knows, whose work the leaving contractor respects, whose values the leaving contractor believes match the client's. The departure is paired with the recommendation. The client's loss (the leaving contractor's service) is offset by the client's gain (the replacement contractor's service, which may be better suited to the client's next phase than the leaving contractor's was). The leaving contractor's loss (the client relationship) is offset by the leaving contractor's gain (the contribution of the recommendation, which builds the leaving contractor's reputation as a talent-spotter and which strengthens the leaving contractor's relationship with the recommended contractor).
- A contractor who must leave a project mid-execution (because of a dispute with the developer, because of a constraint that prevents continued participation) leaves by recommending a replacement — a contractor who can take over the project and complete it. The departure is paired with the recommendation. The developer's loss (the leaving contractor's mid-project departure) is offset by the developer's gain (the replacement's clean completion). The leaving contractor's loss (the project's revenue) is offset by the leaving contractor's gain (the reputation for graceful departure, which is rare in contracting and which future clients will value).

**Risks**: The talent-scout's departure requires that the strategist knows a better successor. If the strategist does not know a better successor (the field is thin, the strategist is the best available), the departure cannot be paired with the recommendation — the departure becomes the abandonment. The risk is the recommendation that is not a recommendation but a cover for the abandonment. The mitigation is to be honest about the field: if the strategist does not know a better successor, the strategist says so, and the departure is the departure (which may still be necessary, but is not the talent-scout's departure).

**Ethics check**: The talent-scout's departure is itself an ethical move — the act of leaving the patron better than the strategist found them, by connecting them with the better service. There is no ethical violation in the departure if the recommendation is honest (the strategist believes the successor is the right one) and the departure is necessary (the constraint is real). The ethical question is whether the departure is for the constraint or for the strategist's convenience. The departure for the constraint is the talent-scout's departure; the departure for the convenience is the abandonment.

### Move 5: The Mother's Honor (Family Above Career)

**The pattern**: When the family is at risk, the family obligation overrides the professional duty. The override is bounded — the family obligation extends to presence (the strategist goes where the family obligation requires), but does not extend to contribution (the strategist does not give the master the plan). The pattern recognizes the family as the primary constraint, and the moral limit as the bounded override of the family obligation.

**Historical example**: Xu Shu's departure from Liu Bei was triggered by the capture of his mother by Cao Cao (or the receipt of his mother's letter, possibly forged). The family obligation overrode the professional duty to Liu Bei. The override did not extend to giving Cao Cao plans — the moral limit was preserved. The pattern is the bounded override: family overrides career; family does not override conscience. The mother's honor is the family obligation; the vow is the moral limit; the combination is the bounded override.

**Mechanism**: The family obligation is the constraint the strategist cannot refuse without violating a deeper obligation than the professional duty. The professional duty (to the patron) is real; the family obligation (to the parent, the spouse, the child) is deeper. The override is the recognition of the deeper obligation. The override is bounded because the family obligation does not extend to the moral limit — the family is at risk, but the family is not served by the strategist's complicity in the master's wrong. The mechanism is the bounding: the family overrides the career; the moral limit overrides the family's apparent demand for the strategist's full service.

**Application in contracting**:
- A contractor whose parent has fallen ill in a distant city faces the constraint: stay in the current market (and abandon the parent) or move to the distant city (and accept the constrained market). The Xu Shu move is to honor the family obligation (move to the distant city) while bounding the override (do not, in the distant city, participate in the local practices the contractor considers unethical). The family obligation overrides the contractor's market preference; the family obligation does not override the contractor's moral limits.
- A contractor whose spouse's family has offered the contractor a position in the family business (a position the contractor would not have chosen freely, but which the spouse's family expects the contractor to accept) faces the constraint: accept the position (and serve the spouse's family business) or refuse (and strain the marriage). The Xu Shu move is to honor the family obligation (accept the position) while bounding the override (do not, in the family business, participate in practices the contractor considers unethical). The family obligation overrides the contractor's career preference; the family obligation does not override the contractor's moral limits.
- A contractor whose child's education requires staying in a market the contractor would otherwise leave (the child is in a school the contractor cannot afford to disrupt; the contractor's market in the city is poor) faces the constraint: stay (and accept the constrained market) or move (and disrupt the child's education). The Xu Shu move is to honor the family obligation (stay) while bounding the override (do not, in the constrained market, accept the local practices the contractor considers unethical). The family obligation overrides the contractor's market preference; the family obligation does not override the contractor's moral limits.

**Risks**: The Mother's Honor pattern can become the family excuse — the strategist who uses the family obligation to justify any service the strategist could have refused. The pattern, intended to honor the family, becomes the cover for the strategist's avoidance of the harder choice (resisting the master's wrong). The mitigation is the bounding: the family obligation is honored, the moral limit is preserved, and the two are not conflated. The strategist who conflates them has not made the Xu Shu move; the strategist has made the family-excuse move.

**Ethics check**: The Mother's Honor pattern is itself an ethical move — the act of honoring the family obligation without surrendering the moral limit. There is no ethical violation in the pattern if the family obligation is real (the family is genuinely at risk or genuinely in need) and the bounding is preserved (the moral limit is not violated in the name of the family). The ethical question is whether the family obligation is real (or is a rationalization) and whether the bounding is preserved (or has been abandoned). The discipline is to keep the obligation real and the bounding preserved.

### Move 6: The Silent Sabotage Refused

**The pattern**: When the strategist has taken the vow and is in the constrained service, the temptation arises to subtle sabotage — to undermine the master's plans by giving bad advice, by withholding critical information, by steering the master toward errors. The Xu Shu move refuses the sabotage as firmly as it refuses the contribution. The vow withholds plans — including plans that would lose. The discipline is neither helping nor harming; the discipline is the presence without the contribution.

**Historical example**: In the Romance tradition, Xu Shu, in Cao Cao's court, did not subtly undermine Cao Cao. He did not give Cao Cao bad advice disguised as good. He did not withhold information Cao Cao needed. He did not steer Cao Cao toward errors. He simply did not give plans. The distinction is the discipline: the vow withholds the plan; the vow does not substitute the negative plan. The strategist who gives the master bad advice has not kept the vow; the strategist has given a plan, in negative. The vow that does not include the refusal of sabotage is not the vow; it is the negative contribution disguised as integrity.

**Mechanism**: The silent sabotage refused is the recognition that the vow is the withholding of plans, not the substitution of negative plans. The mechanism is the symmetry: the positive plan (helping the master win) and the negative plan (causing the master to lose) are both plans, and the vow withholds both. The strategist who substitutes the negative plan has not kept the vow; the strategist has changed sides without admitting it. The silent sabotage refused is the discipline of remaining on the moral side the vow defined, not switching to the opposite side under the cover of the vow.

**Application in contracting**:
- A contractor who has taken the vow (performing the contracted work, withholding the complicity in the developer's misrepresentation) is tempted, in the constrained situation, to subtly sabotage — to perform the contracted work poorly, to fail to flag a defect the developer should know about, to drag out the timeline in ways that hurt the developer. The Xu Shu move refuses the sabotage. The contractor performs the contracted work fully and competently (the constraint requires this); the contractor does not give the developer advice on the misrepresentation (the moral limit withholds this); the contractor does not sabotage the developer's project (the vow withholds the negative plan as well as the positive plan). The contractor is present, performs the contracted work, and gives nothing more — neither help nor harm.
- A contractor who has taken the vow in a partnership (continuing the partnership, declining to participate in the partner's unethical practices) is tempted to subtly sabotage the partner's unethical practices — to fail to perform the unethical practices competently, to leave clues that inspectors might find, to whisper to the partner's clients about the practices. The Xu Shu move refuses the sabotage. The contractor performs the contracted partnership work competently; the contractor does not participate in the unethical practices; the contractor does not sabotage the unethical practices. The contractor is present, performs the contracted work, and gives nothing more — neither help nor harm.

**Risks**: The silent sabotage refused is the hardest discipline in the Xu Shu pattern. The temptation to sabotage, in the constrained situation, is intense — the strategist sees the master's wrongness, knows the master's vulnerabilities, and could easily undermine the master's plans. The refusal of sabotage feels like complicity (the strategist is, after all, present and not acting against the master's wrong). The mitigation is the recognition that the sabotage would violate the vow as much as the contribution would. The vow is the integrity; the sabotage is the negative contribution; the two are symmetric violations. The discipline is to refuse both.

**Ethics check**: The silent sabotage refused is itself an ethical move — the act of refusing both the positive contribution to the wrong and the negative contribution against the wrong. There is no ethical violation in the refusal. The ethical question is whether the refusal is being maintained (the strategist is not subtly sabotaging) and whether the refusal of sabotage is being used as a cover for the gradual complicity in the master's cause (the strategist, having refused sabotage, drifts into acceptance of the master's wrong). The discipline is to maintain the refusal of sabotage AND the refusal of complicity. The two refusals together are the vow.

### Move 7: The Conscience Exit (Yiling-Style)

**The pattern**: When the constrained situation develops a specific complication that puts the strategist's conscience under acute pressure (the master is about to commit an atrocity; the master's plan will produce a massacre; the master's decision will harm innocents the strategist cannot ignore), the strategist may seek an exit from the specific complication without breaking the vow. The exit is not the breaking of the vow; the exit is the removal of the strategist from the situation where the vow would be tested beyond what the strategist can bear. The conscience exit is the constrained strategist's release valve.

**Historical example**: In the Romance tradition, at the Battle of Yiling (222 AD, though the historical timing is disputed), Cao Cao's forces were facing a fire-attack strategy from the allied forces. Xu Shu, recognizing the danger (the chained ships that would make Cao Cao's fleet vulnerable to fire), reportedly asked Cao Cao to be reassigned to the western frontier to defend against potential uprisings — a request that removed Xu Shu from the fleet before the fire attack. The request was not a plan (it did not advise Cao Cao on the fire attack); the request was an exit (it removed Xu Shu from the situation). The conscience exit was the strategist's preservation of the vow (no plan given) AND the preservation of the conscience (not present at the massacre the vow could not prevent).

**Mechanism**: The conscience exit is the recognition that the vow, strictly kept, may not be enough — that there are situations where the strategist's presence, even with the vow, becomes a complicity the strategist cannot bear. The exit is the removal of the strategist from the situation, by means that do not violate the vow (the exit is not a plan; the exit is a request, a reassignment, a withdrawal, a leave). The mechanism is the relocation: the strategist moves to a position where the vow is still kept (no plan given) AND the conscience is preserved (not present at the wrong).

**Application in contracting**:
- A contractor who has taken the vow in a constrained situation that suddenly escalates — the developer is about to make a decision that will produce a structural failure that could kill workers; the partner is about to make a decision that will produce a falsified safety report that will be submitted to authorities — faces the acute pressure. The vow (no plan to the developer on the misrepresentation) is being kept, but the presence at the situation is becoming unbearable. The Xu Shu move is the conscience exit: request a reassignment (ask to be moved to a different project with the same developer; ask to be relieved of the specific responsibilities that touch the misrepresentation; request a leave of absence for personal reasons). The exit is not a plan; the exit is the removal of the contractor from the specific situation. The vow is preserved; the conscience is preserved.
- A contractor whose constrained situation has escalated beyond what the vow can hold (the developer is about to commit a fraud the contractor's presence would implicate the contractor in, even with the vow) requests a transfer, a leave, or a reassignment. The exit is the constrained strategist's release valve. The exit is not the abandonment of the constraint (the contractor remains within the larger constraint — the family obligation, the financial dependency); the exit is the removal from the specific situation within the constraint that the conscience cannot bear.

**Risks**: The conscience exit is the move that can be misused as the escape from the vow. The strategist who exits every acute situation ends up not really in the constraint at all — the strategist has effectively escaped the constraint through repeated exits. The mitigation is the recognition that the conscience exit is for the acute situation, not the chronic. The chronic constraint is borne with the vow; the acute complication (the atrocity, the massacre, the fraud) is the situation for the exit. The discipline is to distinguish the chronic from the acute, and to use the exit only for the acute.

**Ethics check**: The conscience exit is itself an ethical move — the act of preserving both the vow and the conscience when the situation puts them in acute tension. There is no ethical violation in the exit if the exit is for the acute situation (not the chronic constraint) and the exit is by means that do not violate the vow (the exit is not a plan; the exit is a request, a reassignment, a withdrawal). The ethical question is whether the exit is for the conscience or for the comfort. The exit for the conscience is the Xu Shu move; the exit for the comfort is the abandonment dressed as the conscience. The discipline is to distinguish the two, and to use the exit only for the conscience.

## How to Recognize When to Apply Each Move

| Move | When to apply | When NOT to apply |
|------|--------------|-------------------|
| The Recommendation of Zhuge Liang | When the strategist's direct service is constrained or ending, and the strategist knows a better successor | When the strategist does not know a better successor; when the recommendation would be a cover for abandonment |
| The Vow of Silence | When the strategist is forced to serve a master the strategist cannot in conscience advise, and the constraint cannot be escaped without violating a deeper obligation | When the constraint can be escaped without violating a deeper obligation (use Chen Gong's departure instead) |
| The Forced Service (Accepting the Constraint) | When the constraint is real (family at risk, financial dependency) and cannot be escaped without violating a deeper obligation | When the constraint is not real (the strategist is rationalizing a choice they wanted to make anyway) |
| The Talent-Scout's Departure | When the strategist must leave the patron AND knows a better successor to recommend | When the strategist must leave but does not know a better successor (the leaving is then the leaving, not the talent-scout's departure) |
| The Mother's Honor (Family Above Career) | When the family is at risk or in genuine need, and the family obligation overrides the professional duty | When the family obligation is being used as a cover for a strategic preference; when the moral limit has been abandoned in the name of the family |
| The Silent Sabotage Refused | When the strategist has taken the vow and is in the constrained service, and is tempted to subtle sabotage | When the master's wrong has escalated beyond what the vow can hold (use the Conscience Exit instead) |
| The Conscience Exit | When the constrained situation has developed an acute complication (atrocity, massacre, fraud) that puts the conscience under pressure beyond what the vow can hold | When the situation is chronic (the vow is meant for the chronic; the exit is for the acute); when the exit would be a cover for abandoning the constraint |
