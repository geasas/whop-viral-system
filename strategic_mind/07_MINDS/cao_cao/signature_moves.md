## The 7 Signature Moves

### Move 1: Holding the Emperor to Command the Princes (挾天子以令諸侯)

**The pattern**: Don't seize power directly. Seize the symbol of power. Rule through the symbol.

**Historical example**: Cao Cao seized Emperor Xian in 196 AD and ruled in his name for 24 years. Cao Cao's military actions were "in the Emperor's service." His political appointments were "by the Emperor's decree." His opponents were "rebels against the Emperor." The substance was Cao Cao's power; the form was the Han dynasty's legitimacy.

**Mechanism**: Legitimacy is a multiplier on power. Power without legitimacy invites resistance. Power with legitimacy reduces resistance. By attaching your power to existing legitimacy, you reduce the friction of exercising it.

**Application in contracting**:
- Get certified by the Egyptian Federation of Contractors. Your work is the same; the certification multiplies your legitimacy.
- Partner with an established firm on a project. Your work is the same; the partnership multiplies your legitimacy.
- Get a recommendation from a respected figure in the industry (an engineer, a developer, a government official). Your work is the same; the recommendation multiplies your legitimacy.

**Risks**: The legitimizing authority has its own agenda. The Emperor tried to escape Cao Cao multiple times. The certification requires you to follow the certifying body's rules. The partnership requires you to share profits. The recommendation requires you to maintain the recommender's reputation.

### Move 2: Recruiting Enemy Talent

**The pattern**: When you defeat an enemy, recruit their best people. Don't punish talent for being on the wrong side; reward it for switching sides.

**Historical example**: After defeating Lu Bu, Cao Cao recruited Zhang Liao (who had been Lu Bu's general). Zhang Liao became one of Cao Cao's most loyal and capable generals. After defeating Yuan Shao, Cao Cao recruited many of Yuan Shao's former advisors — including, eventually, the Sima family.

**Mechanism**: Talent is rare and transferable. Loyalty is common and contextual. A talented person who was your enemy can become a talented ally if the conditions are right. Punishing talent for past loyalty to your enemy reduces your talent pool.

**Application in contracting**:
- A competitor's project manager leaves after a falling out. Recruit him. He knows the competitor's methods, weaknesses, and clients.
- A subcontractor used to work for a competitor. Use him anyway. His experience with the competitor is valuable information.

**Risks**: Talent recruited from enemies may be disloyal — they switched sides once, they may switch again. The mitigation: build the conditions for loyalty (treat them well, give them responsibility, reward them for staying). Talent that was recruited because of mercenary motives needs to be retained through generous motives.

### Move 3: The Punishment of Self (割髮代首)

**The pattern**: When you violate your own rule, punish yourself publicly. The punishment can be symbolic (cutting hair instead of head), but it must be visible.

**Historical example**: Cao Cao's army marched through grain fields. He ordered: anyone who tramples grain dies. His own horse was startled and trampled grain. Cao Cao ordered his own execution. His advisors protested — you cannot execute the commander. Cao Cao cut his hair instead, a symbolic punishment. The army saw that the rule applied even to the commander.

**Mechanism**: A rule that applies to everyone but the leader is not a rule. By punishing yourself (even symbolically), you make the rule credible. The credibility of the rule is more valuable than the specific punishment.

**Application in contracting**:
- If you set a quality standard, hold yourself to it publicly. When you make a mistake on a project, acknowledge it to your team and fix it visibly. The team sees that the standard is real.
- If you set a payment standard (pay subcontractors on time), never pay yourself before you pay them. If you do, apologize publicly and correct it.

**Risks**: Symbolic punishment can be empty if it's seen as theater. The punishment must cost something (status, comfort, pride) to be credible.

### Move 4: The Decisive Raid (Wuchao)

**The pattern**: When the situation is stalemated, find the enemy's decisive point and strike it directly. Don't try to win the whole war at once — win the one point that decides the war.

**Historical example**: At Guandu, Cao Cao was losing the war of attrition. He was outnumbered, low on supplies, considering retreat. When Xu You defected and revealed the location of Yuan Shao's supply depot at Wuchao, Cao Cao acted decisively. He led a small force on a night raid, burned the supplies, and broke Yuan Shao's army.

**Mechanism**: Most situations have a "decisive point" — the one thing that, if changed, changes everything. Finding that point requires analysis. Striking it requires decisiveness. Doing both requires both analytical skill and courage.

**Application in contracting**:
- In a stalled negotiation, find the one term that matters most to the client and concede on everything else. Concede the term that doesn't matter to you but matters to them.
- In a struggling project, find the one task that's blocking everything else. Solve it first, even at a loss on that specific task.
- In a competitive market, find the one client relationship that opens the door to others. Win that one even at a discount.

**Risks**: Striking the wrong point wastes the decisive force. The analysis must be correct. This is where the multi-level thinking from `thinking_patterns.md` pays off — it identifies the decisive point.

### Move 5: Public Mercy, Private Calculation

**The pattern**: When you defeat an enemy who has surrendered, treat them well publicly. The private calculation may be different, but the public mercy serves the larger game.

**Historical example**: After defeating Yuan Shao, Cao Cao found letters from his own subordinates to Yuan Shao, offering to switch sides. He burned the letters without reading them. The public message: I trust my people; I do not hold past treachery against them. The private calculation: persecuting the traitors would alienate the rest; pardoning them kept them loyal.

**Mechanism**: Public mercy signals that defection is reversible. People who would otherwise resist to the death (because they have already betrayed you once) become willing to surrender. This reduces the cost of future victories.

**Application in contracting**:
- A subcontractor who worked with a competitor approaches you. Hire him publicly. Other subcontractors see that you welcome former competitors' people. They become willing to switch.
- A client who chose a competitor over you comes back after the competitor failed. Welcome them. Other clients see that you don't punish them for choosing the competitor.

**Risks**: Public mercy can attract mercenaries — people who switch sides for opportunity, not commitment. The mitigation: distinguish between reversible switches (a subcontractor who chose a competitor for a single project) and pattern switches (a subcontractor who has switched sides 5 times in 10 years). The first is forgivable; the second is a warning.

### Move 6: The Strategic Retreat to Recover

**The pattern**: After a defeat, retreat to a defensible position, recover, and reorganize. Don't try to win immediately after a loss. Take the time to understand what went wrong.

**Historical example**: After Red Cliffs (208 AD), Cao Cao retreated north. He didn't try to immediately re-invade the south. He spent the next years consolidating the north, building up his forces, addressing the lessons of the defeat. When he finally campaigned again, it was on different terms.

**Mechanism**: Defeat is information. The information takes time to absorb. Acting immediately after defeat, in the heat of emotion, leads to compounding losses. Retreating to recover allows the information to be processed and the next move to be made on better terms.

**Application in contracting**:
- A major project fails or loses money. Don't immediately bid on the next similar project. Take 3 months to analyze what went wrong. Fix the underlying issue. Then bid on similar projects with the new knowledge.
- A client relationship breaks down. Don't immediately try to win them back. Take time to understand what happened. Approach them again only after you've internalized the lesson.

**Risks**: Recovery time can become procrastination. Set a recovery deadline (3 months, 6 months) and act on it.

---

### Move 7: Composing the Story

**The pattern**: After every major event, compose the story of it — in writing, in art, in poetry. The story becomes the official version, which becomes the history.

**Historical example**: Cao Cao was a poet. His poems — including "Short Song Style," "Viewing the Sea," and others — were composed during and after campaigns. They shaped how his actions were understood. He was not just a warlord; he was a man of culture, a thinker, a poet-warrior. The poetry humanized him and shaped his legacy.

**Mechanism**: Events without narrative are ambiguous. The narrative is what gives the event meaning. By composing the narrative, the strategist shapes how the event is remembered and how the next event is interpreted.

**Application in contracting**:
- After completing a major project, write a case study. Document what was done, why, and what was learned. The case study becomes your portfolio entry and shapes how potential clients understand your work.
- After a difficult project, write a "lessons learned" memo. The memo isn't for the client; it's for you and your team. It shapes how the next project is approached.

**Risks**: The narrative can become propaganda. If the narrative diverges too far from reality, people stop believing it. The narrative must be true, even if it's selective.

## How to Recognize When to Apply Each Move

| Move | When to apply | When NOT to apply |
|------|--------------|------------------|
| Holding the Emperor | When you can attach to existing legitimacy | When you have already established your own legitimacy |
| Recruiting Enemy Talent | When you find genuine talent regardless of origin | When the talent has a pattern of disloyalty |
| Punishment of Self | When your rule has been violated and credibility is at stake | When the violation is minor and visibility would make it worse |
| Decisive Raid | When you've identified the single decisive point | When the situation is fluid and the decisive point is unclear |
| Public Mercy | When mercy serves the larger game (signals to others) | When the offender is a pattern betrayer who will betray again |
| Strategic Retreat | When defeat has happened and recovery time is needed | When momentum is on your side and retreat wastes it |
| Composing the Story | After major events to shape understanding | When the events are minor and don't need narrative |
