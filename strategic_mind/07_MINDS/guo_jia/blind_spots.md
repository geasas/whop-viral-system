## The 5 Blind Spots

### Blind Spot 1: Burning Out from Sustained Intensity

**The pattern**: Guo Jia's style of rapid reading and decisive action is unsustainable. He died at 38. The intensity that produces brilliant insights also drains the body and mind.

**Historical example**: Guo Jia died on the return march from the Wuhuan campaign (207 AD). He was 38. The campaign had been grueling — long marches through difficult terrain, in summer heat. His body could not sustain the intensity his mind demanded.

**Symptom in your practice**: A contractor who works 14-hour days for months to capitalize on opportunities eventually crashes. The crash costs more than the opportunities gained.

**Counter-pattern**: Apply Sima Yi's conservation. The long game requires sustainability. Build periods of recovery into the schedule. The opportunity you miss today may be replaced by a better one tomorrow; the burnout you suffer today cannot be replaced.

---

### Blind Spot 2: Acting Before Reading Correctly

**The pattern**: Speed without accuracy is recklessness. Guo Jia's reads were mostly right; the cost of being wrong would have been severe. Most people who try to apply Guo Jia's speed fail because they haven't done the years of preparation that made Guo Jia's instant reads accurate.

**Historical example**: Guo Jia's recommendation to attack Liu Bei immediately (rather than letting him go) was right. Cao Cao's hesitation to act on the recommendation was wrong. But had Guo Jia recommended attacking and been wrong (Liu Bei was actually stronger than he appeared), the cost would have been high.

**Symptom in your practice**: A contractor who acts on "gut instinct" without the underlying analysis often makes the wrong call. The "gut" of an experienced contractor is the result of years of pattern recognition; the "gut" of an inexperienced contractor is just feeling.

**Counter-pattern**: Don't apply Guo Jia's speed until you've done the years of preparation. The speed is the visible part; the preparation is the invisible part. Without the preparation, the speed is just impulsiveness.

---

### Blind Spot 3: Failing in Patient Situations

**The pattern**: Guo Jia's instinct is to act. In situations where waiting is the right move, his instinct fails. He would have been a poor choice to advise Cao Cao during the years of consolidation after Guandu. He was a war strategist, not a peace strategist.

**Historical example**: If Guo Jia had lived, would he have been able to advise Cao Cao during the patient years of preparing for the southern campaign? Probably not well. His speed would have driven premature action.

**Symptom in your practice**: A contractor who always wants to act may rush into projects that should be declined. The impulse to "do something" can override the wisdom to "do nothing."

**Counter-pattern**: When the situation calls for waiting, switch to Sima Yi. Guo Jia is not the universal mind; he is the mind for the closing window.

---

### Blind Spot 4: Vulnerability to Faster Opponents

**The pattern**: When both sides are playing the speed game, the side that is faster wins. If the opponent is faster than you, your speed is a liability — you commit before they do, and they exploit your commitment.

**Historical example**: Had Guo Jia faced an opponent who was equally fast (say, another Guo Jia), the outcome would have been unpredictable. He never faced such an opponent. Most of his opponents were slower (Yuan Shao was notoriously indecisive).

**Symptom in your practice**: A contractor who tries to win a bidding war by acting fast may face a competitor who is faster. The competitor commits first; the contractor must then choose to match (escalating the war) or yield.

**Counter-pattern**: Don't engage in speed wars with faster opponents. Switch to a different game — patience (Sima Yi) or comprehensive building (Cao Cao).

---

### Blind Spot 5: Candor Without Tact Can Cost Relationships

**The pattern**: Guo Jia's honesty was effective because Cao Cao valued candor. With a different patron — one who valued flattery — Guo Jia's candor would have cost him his position.

**Historical example**: Cao Cao mourned Guo Jia's death deeply and credited him with much of his success. But Cao Cao was unusual in his tolerance for candor. Most rulers would have dismissed Guo Jia.

**Symptom in your practice**: A contractor who tells every client the unvarnished truth will lose some clients. Not every client wants the truth; some want validation. Knowing which client is which is a skill.

**Counter-pattern**: Match the honesty to the client. With clients who value candor, be candid. With clients who value flattery, deliver truth gently or remain silent. The principle is honesty in your own conduct, not honesty in every utterance.
