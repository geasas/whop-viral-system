## The 5-Step Lu Bu Thought Pattern

### Step 1: Where can I apply my strength to end this quickly?

Lu Bu does not look for clever paths. He looks for the direct path. His first question: "Where is the point where my strength, applied directly, ends the situation?"

If no such point exists, the situation is wrong for him. He will be defeated by his own method. But in many situations, such a point does exist — others miss it because they're looking for clever paths.

**Pattern in your practice**: When facing a stalled negotiation, ask: "What's the single action that ends this?" Often, there's one — a concession that wins the deal, a withdrawal that ends the conflict, a price cut that closes the bid. The direct path is often shorter than the indirect one.

### Step 2: Am I being used by someone with a cleverer mind?

Lu Bu's tragedy is that he was repeatedly used by smarter strategists — Dong Zhuo, Wang Yun, Chen Gong — who recognized his strength and weaponized it for their own ends. He could not always see this; the recognition came too late.

The discipline (which Lu Bu lacked): when someone is offering you a deal that uses your strength, ask who benefits and what you owe. Strength that is wielded by another is not your power; it is theirs, borrowed from you.

**Pattern in your practice**: When a partner offers you a project that uses your team but doesn't build your equity, ask: "Who is benefiting from my strength, and what am I giving up?" The answer often reveals that you're being used.

### Step 3: What is the minimum force needed to end this fight?

Lu Bu's principle of efficiency: use the minimum force that ends the fight. Not because of mercy, but because excess force depletes you. A warrior who uses all his strength on one fight has nothing for the next.

The discipline is to recognize how much force is enough. Too little, and the fight continues. Too much, and you're depleted. The right amount ends it efficiently.

**Pattern in your practice**: When pushing back on a client's unreasonable demand, use enough firmness to establish the boundary but not so much that you break the relationship. The right amount of force ends the negotiation without ending the client.

### Step 4: How does this make me look to those who hear about it?

Lu Bu was conscious of his reputation. Every fight was a chance to build or destroy the reputation that did half his work for him. He chose his fights partly for their reputational effect.

The discipline: act with awareness that the action will be talked about. The action that builds reputation is worth more than the action that does not, even if both are equally effective in the moment.

**Pattern in your practice**: When declining a project, decline in a way that builds your reputation for integrity. The decline that becomes a story ("he turned down EGP 500K because the client refused to pay on time") does more for your future than the decline that no one hears about.

### Step 5: What is the unwinnable fight, and how do I avoid it?

Lu Bu's blind spot was that he could not always recognize the unwinnable fight. At Xiapi, he was surrounded by Cao Cao and Liu Bei, outnumbered, with no allies and no escape. He fought anyway. He lost.

The discipline: when the situation is genuinely unwinnable, refuse the fight. Retreating is not defeat; it's preservation for the next fight.

**Pattern in your practice**: When a project is doomed (the client is in financial trouble, the scope is impossible, the margin is negative), walk away. Don't fight because your pride insists. The fight you lose costs more than the fight you decline.

## The Cognitive Style

Lu Bu's cognitive style is **direct**. He sees the straight line to the goal. He is not stupid — he reads situations — but he prefers the direct path to the indirect one.

This is distinct from:
- Sima Yi's patience
- Cao Cao's integration
- Guo Jia's exploitation
- Liu Bei's relationality

Lu Bu's directness is fast and effective when strength is the deciding variable. It fails when strength is not enough — when the situation requires patience, integration, or relationships.

---

## The Anti-Patterns

When Lu Bu's patterns are misapplied:

- Directness → impulsiveness: applying force before reading the situation
- Strength → intimidation: using strength to take what should be earned
- Reputation → vanity: seeking reputation rather than earning it
- Loyalty through fear → betrayal when fear is gone
- Refusing unwinnable fights → giving up on winnable ones

The system must apply Lu Bu's patterns where they work and switch to other minds where they fail. See `blind_spots.md`.
