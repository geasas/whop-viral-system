## The 6 Blind Spots

### Blind Spot 1: Ruthlessness Turns Allies Into Assassins

**The pattern**: Dong Zhuo's ruthlessness turned even his closest allies against him. He was assassinated by Lu Bu, his own bodyguard and adopted son. The pattern: ruthless treatment of allies creates allies who become enemies when they have the chance.

**Historical example**: Lu Bu assassinated Dong Zhuo in 192 AD. The assassination was orchestrated by Wang Yun, but it was executed by Lu Bu — the person Dong Zhuo trusted most. Dong Zhuo's earlier abuse of Lu Bu (taking Lu Bu's intended wife, Diao Chan, for himself) created the personal motivation. The political context provided the opportunity.

**Symptom in your practice**: A contractor who treats subordinates ruthlessly — firing them for minor errors, humiliating them publicly, taking credit for their work — creates subordinates who become competitors or saboteurs when they have the chance.

**Counter-pattern**: Apply Liu Bei's mind. The treatment of allies determines whether they remain allies. The ruthless treatment creates enemies; the fair treatment creates loyalty.

---

### Blind Spot 2: Cannot Build, Only Destroy

**The pattern**: Dong Zhuo could destroy the existing order but could not build a new one. His regime was unstable from the beginning because it had no constructive foundation. The destruction that brought him to power also made his power untenable.

**Historical example**: After taking power, Dong Zhuo had no plan for governing. He alienated the scholars, he alienated the people, he alienated his own generals. He could not build a stable administration because his method of taking power (destruction) had destroyed the very institutions he needed to govern.

**Symptom in your practice**: A contractor who disrupts an existing market but has no plan for what comes next. The disruption brings attention; the inability to deliver on the new model destroys the reputation.

**Counter-pattern**: Apply Cao Cao's building mind. The destruction of the old must be paired with the design of the new. The new must be built before the destruction begins, not after.

---

### Blind Spot 3: Symbol Without Substance Is Fragile

**The pattern**: Dong Zhuo held the Emperor (the symbol) but could not govern (the substance). The symbol bought time but could not sustain the regime. Without substance, the symbol's legitimacy eroded.

**Historical example**: The Emperor remained with Dong Zhuo but the Emperor's edicts became less credible over time. The scholars who had initially accepted Dong Zhuo's legitimacy (because of the Emperor) eventually rejected it (because Dong Zhuo was clearly governing for himself, not the Emperor).

**Symptom in your practice**: A contractor who has a high certification (the symbol) but cannot deliver quality (the substance) finds that the certification becomes less valuable over time. Clients discover that the certification does not predict the work; they stop trusting the certification.

**Counter-pattern**: Pair the symbol with the substance. Pursue the certification AND the capability. The certification opens doors; the capability keeps them open.

---

### Blind Spot 4: Cannot Stop the Destruction Once Started

**The pattern**: The destruction that reshapes the macro-board cannot be contained. Once started, it spreads. The spreading destroys things you didn't intend to destroy — including your own assets.

**Historical example**: Dong Zhuo's burning of Luoyang destroyed the capital but also destroyed the credibility of his regime. The act that was supposed to consolidate power became the symbol of his tyranny. The destruction could not be controlled.

**Symptom in your practice**: A contractor who initiates a price war to disrupt the market finds that the price war destroys his own margins. The destruction that was supposed to hurt competitors also hurts the initiator.

**Counter-pattern**: Apply Sima Yi's careful assessment. Before initiating destruction, calculate the full second-order effects. If the destruction will hurt you as much as the opponent, don't initiate it.

---

### Blind Spot 5: Reputation Becomes a Trap

**The pattern**: Dong Zhuo's reputation for ruthlessness preceded him. The reputation made it impossible to negotiate, to form alliances, to be trusted. The reputation that protected him in the short term isolated him in the long term.

**Historical example**: When Dong Zhuo wanted to negotiate with the coalition, the coalition refused — they could not trust him. When Dong Zhuo wanted to make alliances with other warlords, the warlords refused — they feared betrayal. The reputation made every diplomatic move impossible.

**Symptom in your practice**: A contractor with a reputation for ruthless negotiation finds that clients refuse to engage with him. The reputation that protected him in early negotiations (when clients were afraid of being out-negotiated) became the obstacle in later negotiations (when clients preferred to deal with someone easier).

**Counter-pattern**: Apply Liu Bei's reputation-building. The reputation for fair dealing opens doors that the reputation for ruthlessness closes. The long-term reputation is more valuable than the short-term advantage.

---

### Blind Spot 6: The Long Time Horizon Requires Survival

**The pattern**: Dong Zhuo's long-term thinking was correct (the warlords did fight each other for decades), but he did not survive to benefit from it. He was assassinated before his long-term plan paid off. The long time horizon requires the strategist to survive until the payoff.

**Historical example**: Dong Zhuo was assassinated in 192 AD, only 2 years after taking power. The Three Kingdoms period he had helped create continued for 60+ years. His strategic analysis was right; his survival was insufficient.

**Symptom in your practice**: A contractor with a 10-year plan who doesn't survive the first 2 years cannot benefit from the plan. The plan was correct; the survival was inadequate.

**Counter-pattern**: Apply Sima Yi's survival discipline. The long game requires the player to be alive at the end. The discipline includes health, financial reserves, and risk management. The plan must include the strategist's survival, not just the strategy's success.
