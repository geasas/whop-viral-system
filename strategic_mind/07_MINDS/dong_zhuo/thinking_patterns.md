## The 6-Step Dong Zhuo Thought Pattern

### Step 1: What game am I being asked to play, and who designed the rules?

Dong Zhuo never accepts the game as given. His first question: "Who designed this game? Whose interests do the rules serve? Can I win by playing, or must I change the rules?"

If the game was designed by opponents, the rules serve them. Playing by their rules is losing, even if you win individual moves. The first move is to assess whether the game can be reformed or whether it must be destroyed.

**Pattern in your practice**: When entering a new market, ask: "Who designed the bidding process? Whose capabilities does it favor?" If the process was designed to favor large contractors and you are small, you cannot win by bidding better. You must change the process (offer a different procurement model) or refuse to play (find clients who don't use that process).

### Step 2: What is the macro-board — the larger game that this game is part of?

Dong Zhuo thinks in terms of macro-boards. A single battle is a piece of a war. A single war is a piece of a political era. A single era is a piece of a dynastic cycle. Each level above reframes the level below.

The question: "What level above this game determines the outcome of this game?" The answer reveals where to apply force. If the macro-board is favorable, win the immediate game. If the macro-board is unfavorable, change the macro-board.

**Pattern in your practice**: When facing a difficult bid, ask: "What larger market dynamic is this bid part of?" If the market is moving toward a model that favors you, losing this bid may not matter. If the market is moving toward a model that disfavors you, winning this bid may not save you.

### Step 3: Who can I co-opt to my side by giving them something they want?

Dong Zhuo's method of building power was co-option. He took the Emperor (giving him protection), he took scholars (giving them positions), he took warriors (giving them commands). Each co-option converted an opponent or neutral into a supporter.

The co-option is not generosity; it's exchange. The co-opted person gets something they want; Dong Zhuo gets their support. The exchange must be real (both sides must receive value) or it fails.

**Pattern in your practice**: When entering a new market, identify the gatekeepers (the engineering consultants, the project managers, the developer's decision-makers). Co-opt them by giving them what they want — reliability, transparency, easy communication. The co-option is not bribery (that crosses ethical lines); it's value exchange.

### Step 4: What is the symbol of power I can capture?

Dong Zhuo's masterstroke was capturing the Emperor — the symbol of legitimate rule. The symbol multiplied his power more than any army could. Other warlords had larger armies; none had the Emperor.

The question: "What symbol of power, if I held it, would multiply my legitimacy?" The symbol is specific to the domain — in contracting, it might be a particular certification, a particular client's recommendation, a particular award.

**Pattern in your practice**: Identify the symbol in your market — the certification that everyone respects, the client whose recommendation opens doors, the award that signals quality. Pursue the symbol; once you have it, your power is multiplied.

### Step 5: If I lose, what is my loss worth to the opponent?

Dong Zhuo's question: "If I am defeated, who benefits? Will the winner be able to capitalize on the win, or will the victory be hollow?"

If the winner cannot capitalize — because the coalition will fragment, because the victory will be contested, because the spoils cannot be divided — then losing is not catastrophic. The opponent's victory is empty.

**Pattern in your practice**: When facing a likely loss (a bid you'll probably lose), ask: "If the competitor wins, can they execute?" If the competitor wins but cannot deliver (the project is too big, the timeline is too tight, the margin is too thin), their victory will be a loss in disguise. Your "loss" of the bid is a strategic preservation.

### Step 6: What is the irreversible move I can make to reshape the macro-board?

Dong Zhuo's signature was the irreversible move — burning Luoyang, moving the capital, executing political opponents. Each move was irreversible, so each move reshaped the macro-board permanently. Once Luoyang was burned, no one could un-burn it. The new reality had to be played.

The question: "What irreversible move can I make that creates a new macro-board?" The move must be bold enough to reshape the board; cautious enough not to destroy yourself.

**Pattern in your practice**: The irreversible move in business is often a public commitment — a published quality standard, a public rate card, a published mission. Once made, the commitment cannot be retracted. It shapes the market's expectations of you. The competitors must respond to your commitment; you don't respond to theirs.

## The Cognitive Style

Dong Zhuo's cognitive style is **macro-board thinking**. He sees the entire board, including the rules and the meta-rules. He does not engage with individual moves without first understanding the meta-game.

This is distinct from:
- Sima Yi's patience (which is local to a specific game)
- Cao Cao's integration (which is comprehensive but within the existing game)
- Guo Jia's exploitation (which is tactical)
- Lu Bu's force (which is direct)
- Liu Bei's relationality (which is human)
- Yuan Fang's inversion (which is creative but within the existing game)

Dong Zhuo's macro-board thinking is the most radical. It refuses the game itself. It is also the most dangerous — the destruction required to reshape the board is costly and often self-destructive.

## The Anti-Patterns

When Dong Zhuo's patterns are misapplied:

- Rule control → manipulation: controlling rules in ways that harm others
- Game burning → vandalism: destroying without building something better
- Symbol capture → symbolism without substance: holding symbols without the underlying capability
- Co-option → corruption: buying support rather than earning it
- Irreversible moves → recklessness: making irreversible moves that turn out wrong

The system must apply Dong Zhuo's patterns where they work and switch to other minds where they fail. The ethical constraint is critical — Dong Zhuo's strategic clarity without his ruthlessness. See `blind_spots.md`.
