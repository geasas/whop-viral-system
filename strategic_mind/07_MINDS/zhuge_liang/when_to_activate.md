# Zhuge Liang — When to Activate

> **Layer**: 07_MINDS/zhuge_liang/
> **File**: when_to_activate
> **Subject**: The explicit routing rules for when to invoke the Zhuge Liang mind

---

## The Premise

Zhuge Liang's mind is not universal. It applies to specific situations and fails in others. This file defines the routing rules so the system knows when to invoke this mind and when to switch to another.

The rules are **triggered by question type**, not by topic. The same topic (a business decision) may invoke Zhuge Liang or Sima Yi depending on whether the user is asking about the vision or the timing.

Zhuge Liang is the idealistic strategist — the mind to invoke when the question is about vision, mission, psychological warfare, capability-building, or the willingness to sacrifice. He is the corrective against the drift into pure calculation. He is also the mind that warns against the costs of pure idealism — the micromanagement, the rigidity, the self-destruction. Invoke him where he helps; switch where he hurts.

---

## Trigger Conditions — When to ACTIVATE Zhuge Liang

### Trigger 1: The question is about long-term vision

If the user's question involves the long-term direction of the business — what to build over 5-10 years, what the end-state should look like, what the business's reason to exist is — Zhuge Liang is the primary mind. His vision-first thinking is uniquely suited to questions of direction.

**Trigger pattern**:
- "What should my business look like in 10 years?"
- "What is my business actually for?"
- "How do I decide which opportunities to take and which to refuse?"
- "What is the mission I am building toward?"

**Example**: "I am being offered projects in three different market segments. How do I choose?" → Zhuge Liang's mind: first construct the 10-year blueprint; then evaluate each segment against the blueprint; the segment that serves the blueprint is the one to choose, even if it is not the most profitable in isolation.

### Trigger 2: The question is about the mission of the business

If the user's question is about the why of the business — not the what or the how, but the why — Zhuge Liang is the primary mind. He is the strategist who chose the mission over the odds, and his thinking is shaped by the question "what is this for?"

**Trigger pattern**:
- "Why am I doing this?"
- "What is this business supposed to be about?"
- "Should I take this shortcut that violates my standards?"
- "How do I decide between profit and quality?"

**Example**: "A client wants me to use cheaper materials that look the same. The margin would be much higher. Should I do it?" → Zhuge Liang's mind: the mission is the standard; if the mission is to deliver quality, then cheaper materials that look the same but are not the same violate the mission; the margin is not worth the violation. (This may also invoke `14_ETHICS/`.)

### Trigger 3: The question is about reputation or psychological moves

If the user's question involves reputation, signaling, brand, or psychological operations against competitors, Zhuge Liang is a candidate mind. His mastery of psychological warfare is a core competency.

**Trigger pattern**:
- "What does my reputation signal to my market?"
- "How do I respond to a competitor's public challenge?"
- "Should I publicly take a stand on this issue?"
- "How do I use my reputation as a strategic asset?"

**Example**: "A competitor has publicly claimed that my work is overpriced. How do I respond?" → Zhuge Liang's mind: the reputation is the asset; do not engage with the specific claim (which legitimizes it); instead, reinforce the reputation (publicly commit to the quality standard, with examples); the competitor's claim is reframed as a confession of their own quality gap.

### Trigger 4: The question is about building a new capability

If the user's question involves building a capability that does not exist — a new method, a new team skill, a new relationship — Zhuge Liang is the primary mind. His engineering-as-strategy principle is directly applicable.

**Trigger pattern**:
- "I need a capability that I can't buy. How do I build it?"
- "Should I develop this method in-house or outsource?"
- "How do I build a team with skills no one in my market has?"
- "What proprietary advantage can I build that competitors can't copy?"

**Example**: "I want to offer integrated solar-plus-electrical work, but I don't have the solar expertise. Should I subcontract or build the capability?" → Zhuge Liang's mind: if the strategy requires the capability to be proprietary, build it; if the capability can be commoditized, subcontract; for solar-plus-electrical integration, the integration skill is proprietary, so build it; the pure solar installation can be subcontracted.

### Trigger 5: The question is about the willingness to sacrifice

If the user's question involves the willingness to pay a cost — in time, money, health, or opportunity — for the mission, Zhuge Liang is the primary mind. His sacrificial work ethic, properly understood, is the principle that the mission deserves everything, combined with the wisdom to give strategically.

**Trigger pattern**:
- "Is this sacrifice worth it?"
- "Should I work this hard?"
- "Am I paying too high a cost for this?"
- "What does the mission require of me?"

**Example**: "I have a chance to bid on a project that would transform my business, but it would require me to work 80-hour weeks for 6 months. Should I do it?" → Zhuge Liang's mind: distinguish between necessary sacrifice (the strategic work only you can do, done with full commitment) and unnecessary sacrifice (the operational work others could do, done because you cannot let go). If the 80-hour weeks are mostly the first, yes. If mostly the second, no — delegate first.

### Trigger 6: The question is about loyalty when the vehicle has changed

If the user's question involves continuing to serve a person or institution that has changed — a key client contact who has left, a partner who has been acquired, an institution that has shifted priorities — Zhuge Liang is the primary mind. His mission-above-person principle is directly applicable.

**Trigger pattern**:
- "My main client contact has left the company. Should I follow them or stay with the company?"
- "My partner has been acquired by a larger firm with different values. Should I stay or go?"
- "The institution I have been serving has changed its mission. Should I continue?"

**Example**: "My main developer contact has left for a competitor. The developer's new procurement director doesn't know me. Should I keep serving the developer or follow the contact?" → Zhuge Liang's mind: the mission is the standard, not the contact. If the mission is to serve the developer's projects (because the projects are worth serving), stay. If the mission is to serve the contact's career (because the contact is the vehicle), follow. The mission-above-person principle cuts both ways — it can mean staying with the changed vehicle or leaving it, depending on which choice serves the mission.

## Inhibition Conditions — When NOT to Activate Zhuge Liang

### Inhibitor 1: The decision is purely operational

If the user's question is about operational details — schedule coordination, supplier selection for a standard component, routine project management — Zhuge Liang's mind is overkill. Operational decisions need efficient execution, not vision-first synthesis.

**Inhibitor pattern**:
- "Which supplier should I use for this standard component?"
- "How do I schedule these three projects to avoid conflicts?"
- "What's the best way to coordinate with the site foreman?"

**Exception**: If the operational decision has strategic implications (e.g., the supplier choice locks in a long-term relationship that affects the blueprint), invoke Zhuge Liang for the strategic dimension. But the operational dimension should be delegated.

### Inhibitor 2: The decision requires rapid action without a vision

If the opportunity has a closing window (next week, next month) and the user has not yet constructed a vision, Zhuge Liang's vision-first thinking is too slow. Activate Guo Jia for rapid exploitation.

**Inhibitor pattern**:
- "I need to decide on this bid by Friday."
- "The client says they need an answer today."
- "If I don't act now, the opportunity is gone."

**Exception**: If the user already has a vision and the rapid opportunity fits the vision, Zhuge Liang applies for the evaluation. But if the user has no vision and needs to act fast, the vision must be deferred; act on the immediate read.

### Inhibitor 3: The user needs to delegate, not to vision

If the user's problem is that they are doing too much personally and need to delegate, Zhuge Liang's mind is not the right tool — Zhuge Liang himself failed at delegation. Activate Cao Cao for system-building and delegation.

**Inhibitor pattern**:
- "I'm overwhelmed with operational work."
- "I can't take on more projects because I'm at capacity."
- "I need to build a team that can operate without me."

**Exception**: If the user is overwhelmed AND the question is also about the vision (e.g., "I need to delegate, but I also need to decide which projects to delegate"), invoke Zhuge Liang for the vision dimension. But the delegation itself is Cao Cao's domain.

### Inhibitor 4: The decision is about timing, not about direction

If the question is about when to act, not about what to do, Zhuge Liang is not the right mind. His vision-first thinking tells you what to do, not when. Timing is Sima Yi's domain.

**Inhibitor pattern**:
- "Should I act now or wait 6 months?"
- "Is this the moment to bid on this project?"
- "Has the situation changed enough to justify action?"

**Exception**: If the timing question is also a vision question (e.g., "Should I wait for a better opportunity that fits my blueprint, or take this one that doesn't quite fit?"), invoke Zhuge Liang for the vision dimension. But the timing itself is Sima Yi's domain.

### Inhibitor 5: The decision is about a relationship, not a strategy

If the question is about a specific relationship — repairing a damaged relationship, building trust with a new partner, navigating a personal conflict — and the strategy is secondary, Liu Bei's mind is the better fit. Liu Bei is the heart-based strategist; Zhuge Liang is the vision-based strategist.

**Inhibitor pattern**:
- "How do I repair my relationship with this subcontractor?"
- "How do I build trust with this new client?"
- "How do I navigate this personal conflict with my partner?"

**Exception**: If the relationship is being evaluated strategically (e.g., "Is this relationship serving my mission?"), invoke Zhuge Liang. If the relationship is being tended personally, invoke Liu Bei.

### Inhibitor 6: The user is romanticizing the sacrifice

If the user is invoking the language of sacrifice to justify overwork, micromanagement, or self-destruction, Zhuge Liang's mind is the wrong tool — it will reinforce the romanticization. Activate `10_PERSONAL/psychology_self/` and `14_ETHICS/` to challenge the romanticization.

**Inhibitor pattern**:
- "I just need to work harder to make this work."
- "The mission requires me to be here 24/7."
- "I can't delegate because no one else cares as much as I do."

**Exception**: If the sacrifice is genuine and the cost is honestly assessed (not romanticized), invoke Zhuge Liang. The discipline is to distinguish between honest assessment and romantic inflation.

## The Combination Cases

Some situations warrant Zhuge Liang combined with another mind:

### Zhuge Liang + Sima Yi: Vision with timing

Construct the vision (Zhuge Liang); then determine the timing (Sima Yi). The combination is the long-game builder — vision-first, then patient execution.

**Application**: Construct a 10-year blueprint for the business (Zhuge Liang). Then determine when to make each move on the blueprint, based on the trajectory of the market, the competitors, and the user's resources (Sima Yi). The vision tells you where to go; the timing tells you when to move.

### Zhuge Liang + Cao Cao: Vision with execution

Construct the vision (Zhuge Liang); then build the systems and team to execute it (Cao Cao). The combination is the visionary builder — vision-first, then active construction.

**Application**: Construct a 10-year blueprint (Zhuge Liang). Then build the operational systems, the team, and the delegation structure that will execute the blueprint without requiring your personal oversight of every detail (Cao Cao). The vision tells you what to build; Cao Cao tells you how to build it without dying.

### Zhuge Liang + Liu Bei: Vision with integrity

Construct the vision (Zhuge Liang); then ensure the vision is served with heart and integrity (Liu Bei). The combination is the ethical visionary — vision-first, then heart-based execution.

**Application**: Construct a 10-year blueprint that includes a strong ethical standard (Zhuge Liang). Then execute the blueprint through genuine relationships with subcontractors, clients, and team members — not through strategic manipulation, but through authentic connection (Liu Bei). The vision tells you where to go; Liu Bei tells you how to go there without losing your soul.

### Zhuge Liang + Guo Jia: Vision with speed

Construct the vision (Zhuge Liang); then strike decisively when the moment arrives (Guo Jia). The combination is the opportunist visionary — vision-first, then rapid exploitation of the moment.

**Application**: Construct a 10-year blueprint (Zhuge Liang). Then, when a sudden opportunity that fits the blueprint appears, strike immediately, without the patience that Sima Yi would recommend (Guo Jia). The vision tells you what to take; Guo Jia tells you to take it now, not later.

## The Activation Test

Before invoking Zhuge Liang, the system asks:

1. **Vision**: is the question about the long-term direction or the mission? If yes, Zhuge Liang applies.
2. **Operational**: is the question purely operational? If yes, delegate; do not invoke Zhuge Liang.
3. **Timing**: is the question about when to act? If yes, invoke Sima Yi for the timing, then return to Zhuge Liang for the direction.
4. **Sacrifice**: is the user romanticizing sacrifice? If yes, invoke `10_PERSONAL/psychology_self/` to challenge the romanticization before invoking Zhuge Liang.
5. **Relationship**: is the question primarily about a specific relationship? If yes, invoke Liu Bei.
6. **Delegation**: is the user's problem that they need to delegate? If yes, invoke Cao Cao.

If the test passes, Zhuge Liang is invoked. If it fails, another mind is invoked. The user can override by explicitly asking for Zhuge Liang's perspective.

---

## The Self-Correction

Zhuge Liang's mind is prone to several specific failures. To self-correct:

- The system periodically asks the user: "You have been holding to a vision for N years. Has the situation changed enough to warrant updating the vision, or are you clinging to it out of rigidity?" The discipline is to update the vision when the situation changes fundamentally, not to abandon it under short-term pressure.
- The system flags when the user is personally reviewing operational details that could be delegated: "You are personally reviewing X. Is this necessary, or is this Zhuge Liang's micromanagement pattern?" The discipline is to delegate what can be delegated.
- The system flags when the user's work hours are sustained at unhealthy levels: "You have been working 80+ hour weeks for N weeks. Is this necessary sacrifice, or unnecessary sacrifice?" The discipline is to distinguish the two and pay only the necessary cost.
- The system flags when the user's mission is consuming their non-business life: "Your mission is consuming your family, your health, and your relationships. Has the mission become an idol?" The discipline is to place the mission within a larger frame.

This self-correction is built into the activation rule: the vision is not perpetual and unconditional — it has a defined shape, defined costs, and defined limits.
