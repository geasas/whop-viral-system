## The 7 Signature Moves

Each of these moves is a tactical pattern Sima Yi repeatedly uses. They are not metaphors — they are concrete patterns that can be applied to specific situations.

### Move 1: The "Sick Strategist" — Feigned Weakness

**The pattern**: When under pressure, retreat into apparent incapacity. Claim illness. Withdraw from public view. Make the opponent believe you are no longer a threat.

**Historical example**: Sima Yi claimed illness for years during Cao Shuang's regency. He appeared bedridden, drooling, unable to hold a cup. Cao Shuang believed him harmless. Then, when Cao Shuang left the capital for a sacrifice at the imperial tombs, Sima Yi rose from his bed, seized the capital in a day, and eliminated the Cao clan's political control.

**Mechanism**: The deception allows the opponent to relax their vigilance. They stop monitoring you. They stop preparing for your moves. When you finally act, they have no defense.

**Application in contracting**:
- A contractor who feels threatened by a more established competitor might publicly "scale back" — give up some small contracts, appear to focus on a single project. The competitor, sensing weakness, stops watching. The contractor then quietly acquires a major project while the competitor is distracted.
- A contractor in financial trouble might present to suppliers as struggling, delaying payment requests — which paradoxically makes suppliers extend credit more readily because they don't want to lose the relationship when the contractor recovers.

**Risks**: The deception can become real if you deceive yourself. Claim illness too long and you stop being able to act. The move must have a definite end point — the moment to "rise from the bed" must be planned in advance.

**Ethics check**: Feigned weakness in commercial competition is generally acceptable (the opponent is also strategizing). Feigned weakness to extract sympathy-based favors (donations, mercy) crosses ethical lines.

### Move 2: Refusing Battle to Win the War

**The pattern**: When facing an opponent who needs to win quickly, refuse to engage. Force them into a war of attrition. Let their need for progress consume them.

**Historical example**: Zhuge Liang's northern expeditions (228-234 AD). Zhuge Liang needed quick victories to demonstrate Shu's strength before his own health failed. Sima Yi, recognizing this, refused every pitched battle. He fortified. He waited. He let Zhuge Liang's logistics stretch and his health deteriorate. After 6 years of this, Zhuge Liang died at Wuzhang Plains. Shu's northern threat ended.

**Mechanism**: The opponent's need for speed becomes their weakness. Every day they don't make progress, they lose. Every day you don't engage, you conserve. The asymmetry compounds in your favor.

**Application in contracting**:
- A larger competitor enters your market aggressively, undercutting prices. They need quick wins to justify their entry to their board. Refuse to compete on price. Maintain your rates, your quality, your relationships. Let them bleed on the projects they win at low margins. After 12-18 months, their board pulls back. You've lost some short-term revenue but maintained your pricing power.
- A client is pressuring you to commit to a project quickly. They likely have a competing offer they need to compare against, or a budget deadline. Wait. If they really want you, the deadline will extend. If they go with the competitor, you've avoided a project that was being rushed for reasons you don't understand.

**Risks**: If the opponent is more patient than expected, you may wait forever. This move requires an accurate read of the opponent's time horizon. Apply only when you have evidence the opponent needs speed.

### Move 3: Cultivating the Next Generation

**The pattern**: Build relationships with the people who will be in power next, not just those in power now. The young officer today is the general tomorrow. The junior bureaucrat today is the minister in 10 years.

**Historical example**: Sima Yi built relationships with younger Cao family members and with his own sons (Sima Shi, Sima Zhao). When he moved, he had a network of younger allies who would carry the Sima agenda forward for another generation.

**Mechanism**: Power shifts through generations. Those who bet on the next generation accumulate allies at a discount. When the next generation takes power, those allies become the inner circle.

**Application in contracting**:
- The young project manager at a developer's office today will be the procurement director in 5 years. Cultivate the relationship now. The investment pays later.
- The junior engineer at a government agency today will be the head of the contracting department in 10 years. Build the relationship before they're promoted, when they have time and remember who befriended them.

**Risks**: Investing in the next generation is slow — they may not rise as expected, or may leave the sector. Spread your bets across multiple prospects rather than concentrating on one.

---

### Move 4: The "Illness Withdrawal" — Strategic Disengagement

**The pattern**: When the political situation is too dangerous to remain in, withdraw entirely. Claim illness. Return home. Wait for the situation to clarify. Re-emerge when conditions favor you.

**Historical example**: Sima Yi refused to serve Cao Cao initially, claiming illness. Cao Cao tested him — sent an agent to verify the illness. Sima Yi maintained the deception. Eventually, Cao Cao forced him into service, but only after Sima Yi had bought time to assess the situation.

**Mechanism**: When you cannot win and cannot afford to lose, the third option is to not play. Disengagement preserves your forces and your reputation. You can return when conditions change.

**Application in contracting**:
- A client is making unreasonable demands that you cannot meet profitably. Rather than fighting or capitulating, withdraw gracefully. "I'm not the right contractor for this project." The client may return with better terms; or you've preserved the relationship for the next opportunity.
- A market segment is becoming dominated by unethical players. Withdraw from that segment. Focus on a segment where ethics and quality still command a premium. Wait for the segment to mature or for the unethical players to fail.

**Risks**: Withdrawal can become permanent. Some opportunities do not return. The move must be made with a plan for re-engagement, not as a permanent exit.

---

### Move 5: Watching Two Enemies Fight

**The pattern**: When two opponents are about to clash, position yourself to benefit regardless of who wins. Make yourself useful to the winner; make yourself indispensable to the loser (so that the loser's fall does not drag you down).

**Historical example**: Sima Yi watched Cao Cao's wars with Yuan Shao, Liu Bei, Sun Quan, without ever being the primary commander. He was always present, always learning, never the one whose head would roll if a battle was lost. By the time Cao Cao died, Sima Yi was the most experienced strategist in the Cao camp without any major defeats to his name.

**Mechanism**: Direct involvement exposes you to direct consequences. Indirect involvement keeps you informed without exposing you to loss. When the dust settles, you emerge as the experienced, uncontaminated choice.

**Application in contracting**:
- Two large developers are competing for the same market. Don't commit to one exclusively. Work with both at arm's length. The winner will still need you. The loser will need you to recover.
- Two factions are competing within a client's organization. Don't pick a side. Serve both professionally. The winner will trust you (you served them); the loser will not blame you (you didn't betray them).

**Risks**: True neutrality is hard to maintain. Each side may pressure you to commit. The move requires diplomatic skill to execute without appearing to waver.

---

### Move 6: Documenting the Past, Preparing the Future

**The pattern**: Maintain detailed knowledge of what has happened, so you can recognize patterns when they recur. The future rarely brings truly novel situations — it brings variations on past patterns. Knowing the past lets you anticipate the future.

**Historical example**: Sima Yi studied past campaigns obsessively. He knew the patterns of how coalitions form and break. He knew which strategies worked in which terrains. This knowledge let him read situations faster than opponents who were less prepared.

**Mechanism**: Knowledge compounds. Every past campaign analyzed becomes a template for the next. Patterns that took others months to recognize, you recognize in days.

**Application in contracting**:
- Maintain a database of every project: what worked, what failed, what surprised you. Review quarterly. The patterns you'll see over 5 years are more valuable than any consultant's advice.
- Track competitors: who won which bid, at what price, with what outcome. Patterns emerge. Bids that were 20% above the next competitor's price failed 80% of the time. Bids that were 5-10% above succeeded 60% of the time. The market has a price elasticity; you can find it.

**Risks**: Documentation without analysis is just records. The analysis is what creates value. Schedule periodic reviews of the data.

---

### Move 7: The Decisive Stroke After Years of Patience

**The pattern**: After years of waiting, when the moment finally arrives, strike with maximum force. No hesitation. No half-measures. The patience was preparation; this moment is what it was for.

**Historical example**: The Gaoping Tombs Incident (249 AD). After years of apparent illness and retirement, Sima Yi mobilized his private forces, took the capital, and executed Cao Shuang's faction in a single day. Decisive. Brutal. Final. The patience of 10 years culminated in 24 hours.

**Mechanism**: Years of patience build credibility and resources. The decisive stroke converts them into power. Hesitation at this moment is fatal — the opponent has the same opening you do, and will use it if you don't.

**Application in contracting**:
- After years of building relationships with a developer, an opportunity arises to bid on a major project that would transform your business. Bid aggressively. Bring your full relationship network to bear. Don't hold back to "preserve the relationship" — the relationship was for THIS.
- After years of accumulating capital and reputation, an opportunity arises to acquire a struggling competitor at a discount. Execute the acquisition. Don't wait for "a better deal" — the moment is the deal.

**Risks**: The decisive stroke is irreversible. If it fails, you've lost everything. The discipline is to recognize the difference between "the moment has arrived" and "I'm tired of waiting." The first warrants the stroke; the second does not.

## How to Recognize When to Apply Each Move

| Move | When to apply | When NOT to apply |
|------|--------------|------------------|
| Sick Strategist | When opponent is overconfident and you can deceive them for a defined period | When the opponent is monitoring you closely; when you need the deception to last more than 18 months |
| Refusing Battle | When opponent needs speed and you can afford to wait | When the opponent is more patient than you |
| Cultivating Next Gen | When young allies are accessible and likely to rise | When the current generation is entrenched for another 10+ years |
| Illness Withdrawal | When the situation is dangerous and disengagement preserves you | When the situation is dangerous AND requires action to prevent worse outcomes |
| Watching Enemies Fight | When two opponents are genuinely in conflict and you can serve both neutrally | When neutrality is impossible (one side will punish you for not committing) |
| Documenting the Past | Always — this is foundational, not situational | Never — this is a discipline, not a tactic |
| Decisive Stroke | When the moment truly arrives, with sufficient preparation | When you're tired of waiting; the moment hasn't arrived |
