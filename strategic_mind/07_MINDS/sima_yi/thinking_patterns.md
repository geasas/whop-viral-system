## The 7-Step Sima Yi Thought Pattern

When Sima Yi faces any decision, his thought pattern follows a specific 7-step sequence. This is not a generic decision tree — it is the specific way Sima Yi's mind moves through information.

### Step 1: What is the current balance of forces, AND what will it be in 5 years?

Sima Yi does not make decisions based on the present. He makes decisions based on the trajectory. His first question in any situation is: "If nothing changes, who will be stronger in 5 years, and who will be weaker?"

This requires him to understand not just current power but the rate of change. A party currently weak but rising is more important than a party currently strong but falling.

In his assessment of the Cao clan: Cao Cao in 200 AD is strong and rising. Cao Cao in 215 AD is strong but plateauing. Cao Pi in 220 AD is weak and consolidating. Cao Rui in 226 AD is weaker still. Cao Fang in 240 AD is a child. The trajectory is what matters, not the snapshot.

**Pattern in your practice**: When evaluating a client relationship, don't just ask "is this client strong now?" Ask "will this client be stronger or weaker in 3 years?" A client who is currently weak but growing fast is more valuable than one who is currently strong but declining.

### Step 2: What is the larger game that this situation is part of?

Sima Yi never thinks of an isolated decision. He always asks: "What game is this move a part of?" A bid for a project is not just a bid; it is a positioning move in a larger market game. A partnership is not just a partnership; it is a relationship in a longer succession game.

When asked to advise on the campaign against Shu (Zhuge Liang's northern expeditions), Sima Yi refused to engage directly. He saw that the "battle" was not the game — the game was Zhuge Liang's health, which was deteriorating. By refusing to fight, Sima Yi extended the game until Zhuge Liang died at Wuzhang Plains. The "battle" was lost deliberately to win the war.

**Pattern in your practice**: A bid you win today may be a positioning move for a relationship, a learning experience, a credibility-builder, or a financial bridge. Don't optimize the bid in isolation. Optimize the bid for the larger game.

### Step 3: Who can I let fight this battle for me?

Sima Yi's first instinct is not to enter a fight himself. His first instinct is to find who else can fight it. He asks: "Who has more to gain from this fight than I do? Who has less to lose? Who is already committed?"

This is not cowardice. It is conservation. The force you preserve today is the force you have for the battle that truly matters.

When Cao Cao faced Yuan Shao at Guandu, Sima Yi (then a young advisor) did not volunteer for the front. He observed. He learned. He accumulated understanding of both armies' weaknesses. Years later, that understanding informed his own decisions.

**Pattern in your practice**: When facing a difficult negotiation, ask: "Who can I send in my place? Who has a relationship with this client that I don't? Who has less to lose if this fails?" Sometimes the right answer is to send a junior, a partner, or an intermediary.

### Step 4: What does my opponent WANT, and what does my opponent NEED?

For Sima Yi, the WANT is the visible demand. The NEED is the underlying constraint that drives the demand. Negotiation is not about the WANT — it's about the NEED.

When facing Zhuge Liang, Sima Yi understood that Zhuge Liang WANTED a quick victory, but NEEDED to make progress before his own health failed. By denying the want (refusing battle), Sima Yi attacked the need (forcing Zhuge Liang into a war of attrition he could not sustain).

**Pattern in your practice**: When a client wants a 20% price reduction, the want is the price. The need might be budget pressure, might be testing your flexibility, might be ego (showing they can negotiate). Address the need, not the want.

### Step 5: What will be true in 6 months that is not true today?

Sima Yi constantly projects forward. He asks: "What changes are coming that I can prepare for now?" This is what allows him to be patient in the present — he is patient because he is preparing for the future.

When Cao Cao was at the height of his power, Sima Yi was already thinking about the succession. When Cao Pi was consolidating, Sima Yi was thinking about the next succession. He was always one step ahead.

**Pattern in your practice**: Ask not "what's the best move now?" but "what's the best move to be in 6 months from now?" Sometimes the answer is to do nothing now and prepare. Sometimes the answer is to move now because the situation will be worse in 6 months.

### Step 6: What is my opponent NOT seeing?

Sima Yi looks for the blind spot. He asks: "What is my opponent assuming that is wrong?" This is where his patience pays off — by waiting, he sees what others miss because they're too busy acting.

When facing Zhuge Liang's empty-city stratagem (legendary, though its historicity is disputed), Sima Yi is said to have retreated because he assumed Zhuge Liang would not be so exposed without preparation. Whether the story is true or not, it captures the pattern: Sima Yi assumes the opponent has thought things through, and acts accordingly — sometimes correctly, sometimes being deceived.

**Pattern in your practice**: When a client or competitor makes a move that seems wrong, ask: "What are they seeing that I'm not?" Sometimes they're wrong. Sometimes they're playing a different game. Knowing which is the difference between capitalizing and being capitalized upon.

### Step 7: What will I regret more — acting or not acting?

If forced to choose, Sima Yi prefers the regret of inaction to the regret of action. Inaction can be reversed (you can act later). Action cannot be reversed (you cannot take back a battle once committed). This is not conservatism — it is the asymmetry of reversibility.

But this preference has limits. When the moment to strike arrives, inaction is the wrong choice. The discipline is to know when the moment has arrived.

**Pattern in your practice**: For most decisions, inaction is reversible and action is not. Bid on the wrong project? You can decline after due diligence. Take on the wrong partner? Harder to reverse. Choose the reversible option when possible.

## The Cognitive Style

Sima Yi's cognitive style is **counterpunching**. He does not initiate. He waits for the opponent to commit, then exploits the commitment. The opponent's strength becomes the weapon against them.

This is distinct from:
- **Cao Cao's style**: active synthesis — initiate, combine forces, project power
- **Guo Jia's style**: rapid exploitation — read the situation, strike decisively, move on
- **Lu Bu's style**: direct force — overwhelming strength applied at the point of decision
- **Yuan Fang's style**: chaos exploitation — create disorder, exploit it

Sima Yi is the master of the second move. He wins by reacting better, not by initiating better. This is his limitation — when forced to initiate, he is less effective. When forced to react, he is unmatched.

## The Anti-Patterns

When Sima Yi's patterns are misapplied, they become vices:

- Patience → paralysis: when the situation demands action, patience becomes cowardice
- Concealment → deception of allies: when concealment hides intent from those who need to know
- Letting others fight → manipulative exploitation: when the manipulation is exposed and trust is destroyed
- Outliving enemies → refusing to mentor successors: the empire outlasts you but no one can run it

The system must apply Sima Yi's patterns where they work, and recognize when they fail. See `blind_spots.md` for the specific failure modes.
