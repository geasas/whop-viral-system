## The 6-Step Yuan Fang Thought Pattern

### Step 1: What is the opponent's strength, and in what context does it become a weakness?

Yuan Fang's first question is always the inversion question. He doesn't ask "how do I match the opponent's strength?" He asks "in what context does the strength become a weakness?"

The context change is the strategy. If the opponent is fast, change the terrain so that speed is a disadvantage (narrow, crowded, obstacle-rich). If the opponent is large, change the game so that size is a disadvantage (small, fragmented, fast-changing). If the opponent is confident, change the situation so that confidence leads them into a trap.

**Pattern in your practice**: When facing a larger competitor, don't try to compete on their terms (where they are strong). Change the terms to where their size is a disadvantage — small projects, fast turnaround, personal client relationships, custom solutions. Their size is your advantage in their disadvantage.

### Step 2: Where can I introduce controlled chaos?

Yuan Fang doesn't wait for chaos to arrive. He creates it. The chaos can be in the opponent's supply chain, in their team dynamics, in their client relationships, in their decision-making. The chaos doesn't have to defeat the opponent directly — it just has to create uncertainty, which the strategist can exploit.

**Pattern in your practice**: When facing a competitor with a stable client relationship, introduce controlled uncertainty — a better offer, a rumor of dissatisfaction, a problem with their current work. The uncertainty may not win the client, but it weakens the competitor's confidence and creates the opening for your move.

### Step 3: What does the opponent expect me to do, and what is the opposite?

Yuan Fang's default move is the opposite of what the opponent expects. The opponent who expects aggression gets passivity. The opponent who expects passivity gets aggression. The opponent who expects a direct attack gets a flanking maneuver. The opponent who expects a flanking maneuver gets a direct attack.

The discipline is to know what the opponent expects, which requires reading their model of you. If you don't know what they expect, you can't invert it.

**Pattern in your practice**: When a competitor expects you to bid low (because they think you're hungry), bid high. The high bid signals confidence, which the competitor didn't expect. The client may take the bid because the high price signals quality. The competitor's model of you was wrong; the inversion worked.

### Step 4: What is the trap I can build, and what is the trigger?

Yuan Fang thinks in terms of traps and triggers. The trap is the situation that channels the opponent. The trigger is the event that springs the trap.

The trap may take weeks to build. The trigger may be a single moment. The discipline is to build the trap patiently while waiting for the trigger.

**Pattern in your practice**: To win a major client from a competitor, build the trap (build a relationship with the client over months, demonstrate quality, develop trust). The trigger is the moment the competitor makes a mistake — a delivery delay, a quality issue, a price increase. The trigger springs the trap; the client switches.

### Step 5: What can I sacrifice that the opponent thinks I value?

Yuan Fang's willingness to sacrifice is his distinctive feature. The sacrifice can be tactical — a decoy, a false opening, a deliberately exposed weakness. The sacrifice makes the opponent think they have the advantage, when the advantage is what they're being led into.

**Pattern in your practice**: In a negotiation, deliberately concede a point you don't care about, to make the opponent think they're winning. The opponent's confidence makes them more flexible on the points you actually care about. The sacrificed point was the trap; the opponent's confidence is the trigger.

### Step 6: What will the second-order effects of this be?

Yuan Fang thinks about second-order effects more than first-order. He doesn't just ask "what happens if I do X?" He asks "what happens after X happens? What does the opponent do then? What do other actors do?"

The first-order effect of a move is visible. The second-order effect is where the strategy lives. Most strategists win on first-order effects; Yuan Fang wins on second-order.

**Pattern in your practice**: When you lower your price to win a bid, the first-order effect is winning the bid. The second-order effects are: (1) the client expects lower prices in future bids, (2) competitors may match your lower price, (3) your margin is reduced, limiting your ability to deliver quality. The second-order effects may make the first-order win a long-term loss.

## The Cognitive Style

Yuan Fang's cognitive style is **inversion**. He sees the world upside down. Strengths become weaknesses. Confidence becomes vulnerability. Order becomes chaos. His mind is constantly looking for the inversion that others miss.

This is distinct from:
- Sima Yi's patience
- Cao Cao's integration
- Guo Jia's exploitation
- Lu Bu's directness
- Liu Bei's relationality

Yuan Fang's inversion is creative but unstable. It works in chaotic situations and fails in stable ones. It works against confident opponents and fails against humble ones. It works with traps and fails without them.

## The Anti-Patterns

When Yuan Fang's patterns are misapplied:

- Inversion → contrarianism: doing the opposite of what's expected even when the expected is right
- Chaos → destruction: creating chaos you cannot control
- Deception → dishonesty: lying when honesty would have worked
- Sacrifice → cruelty: sacrificing people as well as things
- Trap → paranoia: seeing traps where none exist

The system must apply Yuan Fang's patterns where they work and switch to other minds where they fail. The ethical dimension is critical — Yuan Fang's strategic clarity without his willingness to sacrifice people. See `blind_spots.md`.
