## The 6 Signature Moves

### Move 1: The Four Types of Strategy (四類計謀)

**The pattern**: Yuan Fang categorizes strategies into four types, escalating in sophistication:
1. **Type 1**: Use what already exists (resources, terrain, alliances).
2. **Type 2**: Create from the enemy's strength (turn their force against them).
3. **Type 3**: Make the enemy's victory their defeat (lure them into a winning position that destroys them).
4. **Type 4**: Design the entire reality before the battle begins (the opponent enters a world you've already shaped).

The four types are a hierarchy. Type 1 is the easiest. Type 4 is the hardest. Yuan Fang aims for Type 4.

**Historical example**: Yuan Fang's "Fourth Type" strategy is the burning of Luoyang by Dong Zhuo, which Yuan Fang analyzed as the masterpiece — Dong Zhuo designed the entire reality (the city burning, the coalition fracturing, the empire collapsing) before the battle began.

**Mechanism**: Higher types of strategy are more decisive because the opponent doesn't even realize they're in a strategic situation until it's too late. Type 1 the opponent sees. Type 4 the opponent doesn't see.

**Application in contracting**:
- Type 1: Use existing relationships to win a bid.
- Type 2: Use a competitor's reputation against them (their reputation makes them expensive; you offer quality at lower cost).
- Type 3: Lure a competitor into winning a bid that will destroy them (the project is unwinnable; the competitor wins it and loses money).
- Type 4: Shape the market (become the recommended contractor for an entire segment) so that competitors enter a market you've already designed.

**Risks**: Higher types of strategy are harder to execute. Type 4 requires the most preparation and the most patience. Don't attempt Type 4 when Type 1 or 2 would suffice.

### Move 2: Turning Speed into Weakness

**The pattern**: When the opponent is fast, change the situation so that speed is a disadvantage. The fast opponent, expecting speed to win, runs into a situation where speed causes errors.

**Historical example**: In the manga, Yuan Fang defeats the Red Hare (Lu Bu's fast cavalry) by leading them into terrain where their speed cannot be controlled — narrow streets, oil-slicked surfaces, obstacles. The speed that was their advantage becomes the cause of their destruction.

**Mechanism**: Speed is a strength in flat, open terrain. In terrain that requires careful navigation, speed causes errors. The strategist's job is to change the terrain.

**Application in contracting**:
- A competitor known for fast bidding wins by bidding quickly. If you make the bid process slower and more detailed (requiring more documentation, more reference checks, more site visits), the competitor's speed becomes a disadvantage. Their fast bid lacks the detail the client wants; your slow bid is comprehensive.

**Risks**: The terrain change must be legitimate. If the change is purely to disadvantage a competitor (without serving the client), it's manipulative and may be unethical.

### Move 3: Burning the Map (燒圖)

**The pattern**: When the strategic situation is unfavorable, destroy the map entirely. Force everyone to play a new game where you have advantages.

**Historical example**: Dong Zhuo's burning of Luoyang is the example Yuan Fang analyzes. The city, the map, the existing political structure — all burned. A new map was drawn where Dong Zhuo's enemies started from scratch.

**Mechanism**: Sometimes the existing game is rigged against you. The only way to win is to destroy the game and create a new one. The new game must be one where you have advantages the old game denied you.

**Application in contracting**:
- If the existing market segment is dominated by an entrenched competitor, don't compete in that segment. Create a new segment (a new service category, a new client type, a new value proposition) where the competitor's advantages are irrelevant.
- If the existing bidding system favors large contractors, don't bid through that system. Build direct relationships with clients who bypass the bidding system.

**Risks**: Burning the map is risky because you're betting that the new game will be more favorable. If the new game turns out to be worse, you've burned the old game for nothing.

### Move 4: The Decoy Sacrifice

**The pattern**: Sacrifice something the opponent thinks you value to make them believe they're winning. Their confidence is the trap.

**Historical example**: Yuan Fang sacrifices Sun Shu (his beloved) to delay pursuers. The pursuers think Yuan Fang is desperate; they slow down to rescue her. The delay gives Yuan Fang time to escape. The sacrifice was the trap; the opponent's reaction was the trigger.

**Mechanism**: A sacrifice that the opponent thinks you'd never make unless you were desperate signals desperation. The opponent, sensing weakness, lowers their guard. The lowered guard is the opening.

**Application in contracting**:
- In a negotiation, walk away from a deal you actually want. The client thinks you've given up; they may chase you with better terms. The walk-away was the trap; the better terms are the trigger.
- In a competitive bid, bid higher than you expect to win. The competitor thinks you're not serious; they may bid conservatively. The high bid was the trap; the competitor's conservatism is the trigger.

**Risks**: The sacrifice may be real. If you walk away and the client doesn't chase, you've lost the deal. If you bid high and the competitor outbids you, you've lost the project.

### Move 5: The Pre-Built Trap

**The pattern**: Build the trap months before the trigger. The opponent enters the trap without knowing; the trigger springs it.

**Historical example**: Yuan Fang's strategic masterpiece (the burning of Luoyang analysis) is the pre-built trap. Dong Zhuo built the trap over months — planting seeds of doubt in the coalition, positioning forces, preparing the evacuation. The trigger was the moment the coalition attacked. The attack was expected; the response was prepared.

**Mechanism**: Most strategies react to events. Yuan Fang's strategies create the events. The pre-built trap shapes the situation so that the opponent's actions, even if they appear to be their own choice, are actually channeled into the trap.

**Application in contracting**:
- To win a major client from a competitor, build the trap (develop the relationship, demonstrate quality, become the natural alternative). The trigger is the competitor's mistake. The trap springs; the client switches. The competitor thinks the client chose freely; the trap was set months before.

**Risks**: Pre-built traps require patience. The trap must be built before the trigger; if you trigger too early, the trap is incomplete. If you wait too long, the opportunity passes.

---

### Move 6: Information as Weapon

**The pattern**: Information released at the right time, in the right way, is more powerful than military force.

**Historical example**: Yuan Fang uses leaked information to fracture the coalition — a rumor here, a planted letter there, a "leaked" plan elsewhere. The coalition, full of internal tensions, fractures from the information before any battle. The information was the weapon; the coalition's fragmentation was the result.

**Mechanism**: Information that the opponent thinks they discovered (but was actually planted) is more persuasive than information they're told. The planted information creates decisions that the opponent believes are their own.

**Application in contracting**:
- Let a potential client "discover" that you've been recommended by a respected figure. The discovery is more persuasive than a direct statement. (The recommendation must be real, of course. The planting is just the timing and channel.)
- Let a competitor "discover" that you're considering a project they wanted. They may bid higher than they should, losing money even if they win.

**Risks**: Planted information crosses into manipulation. The discipline is to plant true information (real recommendations, real considerations) and let it be discovered, not to plant false information. False information is deception; true information is just timing.

## How to Recognize When to Apply Each Move

| Move | When to apply | When NOT to apply |
|------|--------------|------------------|
| Four Types | When the situation allows for strategic escalation | When Type 1 is sufficient (don't over-engineer) |
| Speed into Weakness | When the opponent's strength is speed | When the opponent's strength is not speed |
| Burning the Map | When the existing game is rigged against you | When the existing game is favorable |
| Decoy Sacrifice | When the opponent is overconfident | When the sacrifice may be real |
| Pre-Built Trap | When you have months to prepare | When the situation requires immediate action |
| Information as Weapon | When true information can be planted for discovery | When the only available information is false |
