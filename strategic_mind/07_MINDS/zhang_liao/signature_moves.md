## The 7 Signature Moves

Each of these moves is a tactical pattern Zhang Liao repeatedly uses. They are not metaphors — they are concrete patterns that can be applied to specific situations. Each move has a mechanism (why it works), a historical anchor (where it appears in the record), an application to the contractor's domain, a risk profile (when it backfires), and an ethics check.

---

### Move 1: The Hefei Defense — Holding the Small Force Against the Many

**The pattern**: When defending with a small force against a much larger attacker, do not passively wait behind the walls. Read the larger force's specific weaknesses (slow coordination, fragile morale, borrowed authority, deployment constraints), identify the moment when those weaknesses are most exposed (usually dawn, before the larger force has organized), and strike at that moment with a picked small force that achieves disproportionate psychological impact. The tactical casualties are secondary; the psychological break is primary. The larger force that has been psychologically broken cannot sustain the siege even if the tactical numbers still favor it.

**Historical example**: The Battle of Hefei (215 AD). Sun Quan invaded with a hundred thousand men. Zhang Liao had seven thousand defenders in Hefei. Cao Cao, campaigning in the west, had left sealed instructions: when Sun Quan attacked, Zhang Liao and Li Dian were to sally out; Yue Jin was to hold the city. Zhang Liao opened the instructions, recruited eight hundred picked men, and at dawn led a charge into the hundred-thousand-man camp. The raid reached Sun Quan's command post. Sun Quan's officers could not organize a defense in time. The raid killed several Wu officers, broke the camp's morale, and withdrew in good order back to Hefei. The hundred-thousand-man army, demoralized and unable to take the city, retreated within days. The retreat turned into a rout at Xiaoyao Ford, where Zhang Liao nearly captured Sun Quan himself. The hundred thousand never invaded Hefei again.

**Mechanism**: The large force's strength (numbers) is also its weakness (slow coordination, dependence on morale). The small force's weakness (numbers) is also its strength (speed, cohesion, single command). The dawn raid exploits the asymmetry at the moment when the large force's weaknesses are most exposed and the small force's strengths are at their peak. The psychological break is the goal — once broken, the large force cannot rebuild the morale faster than the siege would take the city. The city does not need to be defended forever; it needs to be defended until the larger force's will breaks.

**Application in contracting**:
- A larger competitor enters your market segment with a massive campaign — aggressive pricing, marketing, sales staff. The conventional defense is to match the campaign and bleed cash. The Hefei defense is to identify the competitor's specific weaknesses (the sales staff does not know the local market, the pricing cannot sustain the project portfolio, the marketing promises cannot be kept by the operations team) and strike at the moment of maximum exposure (the first major project the competitor wins, when the operations team is overwhelmed and the promises are unmet). Your eight hundred: a hand-picked team that delivers the project the competitor just promised, for the client the competitor just took. The client, comparing your eight hundred's delivery with the competitor's hundred thousand's promises, learns what the market learns. The competitor's morale (their willingness to invest in your segment) breaks.
- A large procurement is being run by a state agency that has assembled a complicated bid process designed to favor a large prime contractor. The Hefei defense is to identify the process's specific weaknesses (the agency's procurement officers have no time to evaluate the bids in detail; the large prime contractor's bid will be a generic template; the agency's actual technical concerns are not the concerns the bid asks about) and strike at the moment of maximum exposure (the bid clarification round, when the agency asks questions and the prime contractor answers generically). Your eight hundred: a targeted clarification response that addresses the agency's actual technical concerns in specific, technical, demonstrated detail. The agency's perception of who can deliver shifts; the bid is yours.

**Risks**: The Hefei defense fails when the small force misreads the large force's weaknesses. The dawn raid against a large force whose coordination is fast, whose morale is durable, and whose commander's authority is earned will be a sacrifice, not a victory. Apply this move only when you have evidence that the large force's weaknesses are real and exploitable at the moment you have chosen.

**Ethics check**: The Hefei defense is ethical when the psychological operation breaks the opponent's will to engage without inflicting unnecessary harm (the tactical casualties are the minimum required; the psychological break is the goal). It is unethical when the operation is designed to humiliate the opponent personally, to harm the opponent's reputation beyond the strategic necessity, or to delight in the opponent's distress. The goal is the duty; the duty is the secured frontier, not the destroyed opponent.

### Move 2: The Night Raid — Striking at the Opponent's Maximum Disorientation

**The pattern**: When the opponent has set up for a sustained engagement (a siege, a long negotiation, a multi-month procurement), the moment of maximum disorientation is the moment before the opponent has organized for the day. The dawn raid, executed at the right hour with a picked force, can produce a result that the entire day's worth of conventional engagement cannot. The raid is not the whole strategy; it is the single act that breaks the opponent's plan.

**Historical example**: At Hefei, the night raid was the dawn attack by eight hundred men into Sun Quan's hundred-thousand-man camp. The camp had set up for the siege; the troops were sleeping; the officers had not yet convened for the day's commands. The raid caught the camp in the moment between sleep and organization — the moment when a hundred thousand men are not yet a hundred-thousand-man army but are a hundred thousand individuals, each disoriented, each looking for direction, each vulnerable to the charge that has direction. The charge broke the camp's coherence; the coherence did not recover.

**Mechanism**: The dawn raid works because the large force has organized for the campaign (which lasts days, weeks, months), not for the moment (which lasts minutes, hours). The small force that organizes for the moment — that picks its eight hundred, drills them, briefs them, and attacks at the exact hour of maximum disorientation — has an asymmetry the large force cannot match without reorganizing for the moment (which the large force cannot do, because it has organized for the campaign). The asymmetry is what produces the disproportionate result.

**Application in contracting**:
- A long competitive bid process has been running for months. The competitors have settled into a routine: submissions, clarifications, revisions. The conventional approach is to follow the routine and hope your bid is the best. The night raid is to identify the single moment in the bid process when the client's evaluation is most disoriented (often the moment after the formal submissions, when the evaluators have read the bids and are forming initial opinions) and to deliver, at that moment, a single additional piece of value that the competitors have not prepared (a site visit the client did not request, a technical memo on a question the client did not ask, a reference call the client did not expect). The single act, delivered at the disoriented moment, shifts the client's perception more than the months of routine bid work.
- A long client relationship has gone into routine drift — the client is still giving you work, but the work is becoming commodity work, the relationship is no longer strategic. The conventional approach is to accept the drift and the gradual decline. The night raid is to identify the moment when the client is most disoriented (a recent project failure with another contractor, an internal reorganization, a new strategic priority) and to deliver, at that moment, a single proposal that reconnects the relationship to the strategic level (a proposal for a project the client has not yet articulated but that you can see is coming, with a value the competitor has not anticipated). The single proposal, delivered at the disoriented moment, restores the relationship to the strategic level.

**Risks**: The night raid fails when the timing is wrong. A raid at the wrong hour (too early, before the disorientation; too late, after the organization) is a waste. The discipline is to know the opponent's cycle and to strike at the exact moment of disorientation. Apply this move only when you have studied the opponent's cycle and know the hour.

**Ethics check**: The night raid is ethical when it is a strategic move within the legitimate engagement (a competitive bid, a client relationship). It is unethical when it is an ambush outside the legitimate engagement (a surprise attack on a relationship the competitor believes is settled, a disclosure timed to inflict maximum personal damage on a competitor's employee). The test: is the move within the engagement the opponent has agreed to, or is it an ambush outside the engagement? The first is strategy; the second is sabotage.

### Move 3: The Switched Allegiance — Reassigning the Duty When the Channel Fails

**The pattern**: When the principal through whom you have been serving the duty can no longer carry the duty (because the principal is dead, because the principal's judgment has failed, because the principal's organization has been dissolved), the duty must be reassigned to a new principal who can carry it. The reassignment is not betrayal — it is the continuation of the duty through a new channel. The discipline is to make the reassignment cleanly, with the previous duty fully discharged, and the new duty fully accepted.

**Historical example**: After Lu Bu's execution at Xiapi (198 AD), Zhang Liao's duty to Lu Bu was discharged — Zhang Liao had served Lu Bu to the very end, even as Lu Bu's decisions destroyed them. The duty that remained (to serve as a military professional, to use his capacity to defend a frontier or win a campaign) needed a new channel. Cao Cao, who had defeated Lu Bu, was the obvious new principal — Cao Cao's strategic vision was large enough to use Zhang Liao's capacity, and Cao Cao's judgment was worthy of the duty. Zhang Liao accepted Cao Cao's commission. The switch was clean: the previous duty discharged, the new duty accepted, no residual disloyalty, no hedge. From the day of the switch to the day of his death, Zhang Liao served one lord.

The contrast with Lu Bu's own switches (killing Ding Yuan to join Dong Zhuo, killing Dong Zhuo to join Wang Yun) is the contrast between clean reassignment and betrayal. Zhang Liao's switches were responses to the channel failing (Ding Yuan killed, Dong Zhuo killed, Lu Bu killed); Lu Bu's switches were the cause of the channel failing (Lu Bu killed each of his principals). The two patterns look similar from outside — "served multiple masters" — but are morally opposite.

**Mechanism**: The clean reassignment works because the duty, not the person, is the substance. The principal who understands the duty (rather than the person) recognizes that the subordinate who switched cleanly is more trustworthy, not less — because the subordinate who discharges the duty fully before reassigning it is the subordinate who will discharge the new duty fully. The principal who confuses person and duty reads the switch as disloyalty and refuses to trust the switcher — and loses access to the very subordinates who would have served the duty most completely.

**Application in contracting**:
- A long-time client's business has been acquired by a larger firm with different procurement processes, different standards, and a different strategic direction. The original relationship is gone; the duty (deliver excellent electrical work, build a reliable client relationship) remains, but it must now be served through the new channel. The switched allegiance is to learn the new procurement process, build the new relationships, and serve the new organization with the same discipline the old one received. The switch is clean: the old relationship honored, the new relationship entered with full commitment.
- A long-time subcontractor relationship has broken down — the subcontractor's quality has declined, their pricing has become unreliable, their team has been disrupted. The duty (deliver the project on spec, on time, on budget) remains, but it must be served through a new subcontractor. The switched allegiance is to identify the new subcontractor, transition the work cleanly, complete the existing commitments with the old subcontractor, and serve the new subcontractor with the same discipline. The switch is not punishment of the old; it is the continuation of the duty through the new.

**Risks**: The switched allegiance fails when the reassessment is wrong — when the channel could have carried the duty but the subordinate switched for comfort or opportunity. The discipline is to ask: "Is the channel truly failing, or am I switching because the channel has become uncomfortable?" If the channel is failing, switch cleanly. If the channel is uncomfortable, do not switch — push through the discomfort, because the channel may recover and the duty will continue.

**Ethics check**: The switched allegiance is ethical when the previous duty has been fully discharged before the switch is made. It is unethical when the switch is made while the previous duty is still active (abandoning a project mid-execution for a better offer, leaving a client without the work they paid for). The test: is the previous duty complete or, if not complete, has the transition been structured so the duty is still served? If yes, the switch is ethical. If no, the switch is breach.

### Move 4: The Sealed Orders — Executing the Intent When the Principal Is Absent

**The pattern**: When the principal cannot be present to make the decision, the principal leaves sealed instructions to be opened at the right moment. The subordinate's discipline is to open the instructions at the right moment, read the intent behind the instructions, and execute the intent — even when the instructions are incomplete or the situation has changed. The execution of the intent is the trust the long service has built; the trust is the autonomy; the autonomy is the victory.

**Historical example**: Cao Cao, leaving for the western campaign against Liu Bei, left sealed instructions with the Hefei garrison: "When Sun Quan attacks, open these. Zhang Liao and Li Dian sally out; Yue Jin defends the city." The instructions were brief, written before Cao Cao knew the exact situation Sun Quan's invasion would create. Zhang Liao's discipline was to read the intent behind the instructions — "the principal wants the garrison to take the initiative, not to be sieged passively; the principal has chosen me to lead the sally; the principal has paired me with Li Dian to force us to set aside our enmity for the duty" — and to execute the intent. The execution required an act the instructions did not specify: Zhang Liao personally went to Li Dian, asked him to set aside their enmity for the duty, and Li Dian agreed. The act of reconciliation was the service of the intent; the sealed orders were the permission; the long-service trust was the autonomy that produced the victory.

**Mechanism**: The sealed orders work because the long service has built the trust that allows the principal to give the permission in advance, and because the subordinate's discipline allows the intent to be served even when the instructions are incomplete. The principal's absence is not a constraint; it is the opportunity for the subordinate to demonstrate the trust. The subordinate who executes the intent wins the autonomy; the autonomy produces the victory; the victory produces more trust; the trust produces more autonomy. The compounding is the long-service asset.

**Application in contracting**:
- A client has hired you for a project but the client's project manager will be absent for the first two weeks (travel, family emergency, reorganization). The client leaves you with the project specifications and the contract, but no day-to-day supervision. The sealed orders are the contract; the intent is the project's success. Your discipline is to execute the intent — start the project on time, make the decisions the absent PM would have made (within the contract's scope), document every decision, report regularly, and have the project ready for the PM's return. The execution produces the trust that the next project will be awarded with the same autonomy.
- A developer has hired you for a multi-phase project but the developer's procurement director, who championed your hire, has left the company. The new procurement director does not know you. The sealed orders are the original contract; the intent is the multi-phase project's success. Your discipline is to execute the intent — deliver Phase 1 to a standard that makes Phase 2 an obvious decision for the new director, document the value of the relationship in the new director's terms, and have the relationship ready for the new director's review. The execution produces the trust that the multi-phase project continues with you, not with a competitor the new director might have preferred.

**Risks**: The sealed orders fail when the subordinate misreads the intent. The subordinate who executes the instruction literally, without reading the intent, produces a result that the principal did not want. The subordinate who improvises beyond the intent, claiming to serve it, produces a result the principal did not authorize. The discipline is to read the intent accurately and to execute it precisely — neither narrowing it to the instruction nor expanding it beyond the intent.

**Ethics check**: The sealed orders are ethical when the subordinate executes the intent in good faith and reports the execution honestly. It is unethical when the subordinate uses the principal's absence to act beyond the intent and then claims the principal's authorization after the fact. The test: would the principal, knowing what you did, recognize it as the intent they wanted? If yes, the execution is ethical. If no, the execution is a usurpation.

### Move 5: The Personal Charge — Leading the Critical Moment from the Front

**The pattern**: When the critical moment of an operation has been identified, and the moment requires the personal presence of the strategist to produce the result no substitute can produce, lead the moment from the front. The personal charge is not bravery for its own sake; it is the strategic choice to be at the point where the marginal gain of your presence is highest. The discipline is to charge only when the moment needs you, and to delegate every moment that does not.

**Historical example**: At Hefei, Zhang Liao led the dawn raid in person. He was at the front of the eight hundred. He killed officers with his own weapon. He reached Sun Quan's command post. He was wounded in the action. He did this because the dawn raid required his personal presence — the eight hundred followed him because he was there, the officers he killed were killed by him, the psychological impact on Sun Quan's command was the impact of seeing Zhang Liao in person. The strategy and the personal charge were the same act. The alternative — Zhang Liao planning the raid and a subordinate leading it — would have produced a smaller result, because the eight hundred would have followed a subordinate less willingly, and the psychological impact would have been less.

The contrast with the moments Zhang Liao did not personally lead is the discipline. He did not personally defend every wall section during the siege. He did not personally command every subsequent operation. He charged at the critical moment and delegated the rest. The combination produced the victory and preserved him for the next critical moment.

**Mechanism**: The personal charge works because the strategist's presence at the critical moment produces three results no substitute can produce: (1) the team's effort intensifies when the principal is there; (2) the principal sees, in person, what the reports would have hidden; (3) the principal's authority is most credible when it is exercised in person, at the moment, with the team. The three together produce a result that no delegation can replicate. The cost is the exposure; the benefit is the result. The discipline is to charge when the benefit exceeds the cost.

**Application in contracting**:
- A project is at its critical moment — the first week of installation, when the team's tone is set, when the first unexpected complication is met, when the client's confidence is being tested. The personal charge is to be on site for the first week — walking the site at 6 AM, handling the first complication in person, setting the tone with the foreman and the team, demonstrating to the client that the principal is present. The team works harder; the client's confidence is set; the project's tone is established. The cost (the principal's time, the principal's exposure) is justified by the benefit (the project's success for the next 18 months).
- A client relationship is at its critical moment — the first major scope change, the first payment dispute, the first unexpected delay. The personal charge is to handle the moment in person — meet the client, walk the issue, propose the resolution, document the agreement. The client's confidence in the relationship is set by how the principal handles the critical moment. The project manager's handling would have been competent; the principal's handling is decisive.

**Risks**: The personal charge fails when it becomes the habit rather than the exception. The principal who charges every moment burns out; the team that never gets to lead never develops; the principal's presence becomes routine rather than decisive. The discipline is to charge the moments that need you, and to delegate the moments that do not. The default is delegation; the exception is the charge.

**Ethics check**: The personal charge is ethical when it serves the work. It is unethical when it serves the principal's need for visibility or the principal's discomfort with delegation. The test: am I charging because the moment needs me, or because I am uncomfortable not being in charge? The first is the duty; the second is vanity.

### Move 6: The Psychological Dominance — Making the Name a Deterrent Before the Engagement

**The pattern**: The opponent who has been psychologically broken once does not need to be broken again; the memory of the previous break deters the next engagement. Build the psychological dominance deliberately, through the strategic use of demonstrated victory, until the name itself becomes a deterrent. The dominance is the cheapest defense — it is won once and pays dividends for years.

**Historical example**: After the Battle of Hefei, Zhang Liao's name became a deterrent in Wu. The idiom "Zhang Liao stops babies from crying" (張遼止啼) was used in Wu for generations — mothers calmed frightened children at night by invoking Zhang Liao's name, the way other cultures invoked bogeymen. Sun Quan never again invaded Hefei with a comparable force. The southern frontier of the Cao regime was secured not by the seven-thousand-man garrison (which was rotated and replaced) but by the memory of the eight-hundred-man raid, which outlasted every garrison. The dominance was won once, at Hefei; the dividends paid for decades.

The dominance is not intimidation through cruelty. The dominance is the demonstrated capacity to win at the moment of maximum disorientation. The opponent who has been broken once knows the capacity is real; the opponent who has heard of the break believes the capacity is real. The belief is the deterrent. The demonstrated capacity is the foundation; the belief is the multiplier.

**Mechanism**: The psychological dominance works because the opponent's mind, once broken, fills in the strategist's capacity beyond what the strategist has actually demonstrated. The opponent who has been broken once assumes the strategist can break him again, in any situation, by any method. The assumption over-credits the strategist, which is the strategist's advantage. The dominance is the asymmetric asset: it costs the strategist nothing to maintain (it is a memory, not a force), and it costs the opponent everything to overcome (he must rebuilt the morale, the cohesion, the willingness to engage).

**Application in contracting**:
- A contractor's reputation for finishing on time, every time, becomes a psychological asset in the market. The client who has heard "they finished the hospital project two weeks early" assumes the contractor will finish every project early. The assumption over-credits the contractor; the contractor's actual capacity is "we finish on time, reliably, with occasional early finishes." The reputation is the dominance; the dominance is the asymmetric asset. Every bid the contractor enters is informed by the dominance; every competitor the contractor faces is informed by it.
- A contractor's reputation for not negotiating price down after the contract is signed becomes a psychological asset. The competitor who has been beaten once by the contractor's pricing discipline assumes the contractor will beat him again, in any bid. The assumption over-credits the contractor; the contractor's actual capacity is "we hold our price because our costs are real." The dominance deters the competitor from entering the bids the contractor enters; the bids the contractor enters are easier to win because the dominance has already deterred the strongest competitors.

**Risks**: The psychological dominance fails when it becomes the strategist's only asset. The dominance that is not backed by actual capacity is a bluff; the opponent who tests the bluff discovers it; the dominance is broken; the next engagement is harder than the first. The discipline is to maintain the actual capacity (the team, the training, the relationships) that the dominance advertises. The dominance is the multiplier; the capacity is the multiplicand. The dominance without the capacity is zero; the capacity without the dominance is the unmultiplied number.

**Ethics check**: The psychological dominance is ethical when it is built on demonstrated capacity and used to deter opponents from engagements that would waste their resources. It is unethical when it is built on inflated claims and used to extract concessions from clients who over-credit the strategist. The test: would the dominance survive a direct test? If yes, the dominance is ethical. If no, the dominance is a fraud that will be exposed by the first serious test.

### Move 7: The Long Service — Compounding the Trust Until the Autonomy Becomes the Asset

**The pattern**: Serve the principal for years, through the principal's campaigns and sicknesses and successions and eventual death, with the discipline of executing the duty at every moment. The accumulated trust produces the autonomy that produces the victories that no centrally commanded strategy can produce. The long service is the strategy; the autonomy is the asset; the victories are the dividends.

**Historical example**: Zhang Liao served Cao Cao for roughly twenty years (198-220 AD), through the campaigns against Yuan Shao's remnants, the campaign against the Wuhuan in the north, the Hefei defense, and the long southern frontier. He served Cao Pi (Cao Cao's successor) for two more years, until his own death from illness in 222 AD. The twenty years of faithful service produced the trust that produced the sealed-orders autonomy at Hefei. A subordinate with two years of trust could not have been left with sealed instructions to defend the southern frontier against a hundred-thousand-man invasion. The trust was the asset; the autonomy was the dividend; the victory was the proof that the strategy works.

The long service is not a moral pose. It is the empirical discipline of compounding. The trust that builds in year one produces the autonomy in year three; the autonomy in year three produces the assignments in year five; the assignments in year five produce the victories in year ten; the victories in year ten produce the sealed-orders autonomy in year twenty. Each year's service compounds on the previous year's service. The compounding is the asset that no short-service subordinate can match.

**Mechanism**: The long service works because trust is built one action at a time, and the actions accumulate. The principal who has been served well for one year trusts the subordinate for the next year; the principal who has been served well for ten years trusts the subordinate for the next decade. The trust is not granted; it is built. The building is slow; the asset is durable. The short-service subordinate must be watched; the long-service subordinate is trusted. The watched subordinate must ask permission; the trusted subordinate acts and reports. The permission-asking subordinate misses the moment; the acting subordinate catches it.

**Application in contracting**:
- The contractor who serves the same three or four clients for ten years accumulates the trust that produces the work without bids. The clients, having been served well across multiple projects, across multiple budget cycles, across multiple internal reorganizations, trust the contractor with the next project before it is bid. The bid is the formality; the relationship has already won. The long service is the asset; the work-without-bids is the dividend.
- The contractor who serves the same key team for ten years accumulates the trust that produces the autonomy. The team, having been served well (paid fairly, treated with respect, given meaningful work, retained through slow periods), trusts the contractor with the next critical moment. The team that has been with the contractor for ten years executes the dawn raid; the team that has been with the contractor for ten weeks needs supervision. The long service with the team is the asset; the autonomy is the dividend; the victories are the proof.

**Risks**: The long service fails when the calcification sets in — when the trust becomes the assumption that the duty does not need to be reassessed. The long-service subordinate who does not reassess the duty, when the principal's judgment has failed, is the subordinate who serves the failing principal into the disaster. The discipline is to maintain the long service while continuing to reassess the duty. The two are not in conflict — the long service is the asset; the reassessment is the discipline that keeps the asset honest.

**Ethics check**: The long service is ethical when the duty is served at every moment. It is unethical when the long service becomes the cover for serving the principal's worst impulses (the subordinate who has been with the principal for twenty years and who helps the principal execute an unethical project because "the principal has been my lord for twenty years"). The test: would I do this if I had just been hired, with no relationship to the principal? If yes, the action is ethical. If no, the long service is the cover for an unethical action.

## How to Recognize When to Apply Each Move

| Move | When to apply | When NOT to apply |
|------|--------------|------------------|
| Hefei Defense | Defending against a much larger attacker whose weaknesses can be read and exploited at a specific moment | When the larger attacker's weaknesses are not real or not exploitable; when the small force's cohesion is fragile |
| Night Raid | When the opponent has set up for a sustained engagement and the moment of maximum disorientation can be identified | When the opponent has organized for the moment (the disorientation does not exist); when the small force does not have the cohesion to execute the raid |
| Switched Allegiance | When the channel through which the duty is being served can no longer carry the duty | When the channel is uncomfortable but functional; when the switch is for opportunity rather than duty |
| Sealed Orders | When the principal is absent and has left instructions whose intent must be executed at the right moment | When the principal is present and can make the decision; when the instructions are clear and the situation has not changed |
| Personal Charge | At the critical moment of an operation, when the principal's presence produces a result no substitute can produce | When the moment is routine and the principal's presence is unnecessary exposure; when the charge has become a habit |
| Psychological Dominance | When the demonstrated victory can be used to deter future engagements | When the dominance is not backed by actual capacity; when the dominance becomes the strategist's only asset |
| Long Service | Always — this is the foundational discipline, not a tactical move | Never — this is a discipline, not a tactic, but it must be paired with reassessment of the duty |
