## The 6-Step Liu Bei Thought Pattern

### Step 1: Who are the people involved, and what do they care about?

Liu Bei starts with people, not with strategy. Before deciding, he asks: "Who is affected? What do they care about? What will this decision do to them?"

This is the opposite of the analytical approach. The analytical approach asks "what is the optimal outcome?" first. Liu Bei asks "who is this decision for?" first.

**Pattern in your practice**: Before any decision, list the people involved and what each one cares about. The client cares about cost. The subcontractor cares about timely payment. The team cares about workload. The user cares about profit. Only after listing these can you find the decision that serves most of them.

### Step 2: What would I do if this person were my brother?

Liu Bei's standard for treatment: would I treat my brother this way? If the answer is no, don't do it to a subordinate, partner, or client.

This is not the same as being soft. Liu Bei was demanding of his brothers — he expected loyalty, courage, and honesty. He held them to high standards. But he gave them the same in return.

**Pattern in your practice**: When deciding how to treat a subcontractor, ask: "How would I treat my brother in this situation?" The answer is usually more demanding (you'd push your brother to do better) AND more supportive (you'd help your brother when he's in trouble). Both.

### Step 3: What is the long-term relationship I'm building or destroying?

Every decision either builds or destroys a relationship. Liu Bei evaluated decisions by their effect on the long-term relationship, not by their short-term gain.

He could have taken Yi province from Liu Zhang by treachery. The short-term gain would have been massive. The long-term cost — the loss of reputation as a man who does not betray — would have been greater. He waited, and eventually took Yi province only after Liu Zhang attacked him. The reputation survived.

**Pattern in your practice**: For every decision, ask: "Does this build or destroy the long-term relationship with this person?" If it builds, proceed. If it destroys, the short-term gain must be enormous to justify it.

### Step 4: What does the mission require?

Liu Bei's mission — restoring the Han dynasty — guided his decisions. When in doubt, he asked: "Does this serve the mission?" If yes, do it. If no, don't.

The mission was not a slogan; it was a filter. Decisions that served the mission were taken even when they cost personally. Decisions that didn't serve the mission were rejected even when they would have benefited Liu Bei personally.

**Pattern in your practice**: Define your mission clearly. Then filter every major decision through it. "Does this serve my mission of building a halal construction company that proves quality and integrity can dominate the market?" If yes, do it. If no, decline even if it pays well.

### Step 5: What is the emotional truth of this situation?

Liu Bei was sensitive to emotional truth. He could read what people were feeling, not just what they were saying. When a subordinate was unhappy, Liu Bei sensed it. When an ally was hesitating, Liu Bei noticed.

This is the skill of empathy — not as a soft virtue but as a strategic information source. What people feel reveals what they will do, often more accurately than what they say.

**Pattern in your practice**: Read the emotional state of clients, partners, and team members. The client who is happy with the technical bid but unhappy with the relationship is a flight risk. The subcontractor who delivers well but seems withdrawn is looking for another client. The team that works well but has lost enthusiasm needs attention.

### Step 6: What is the right thing to do, even if it costs?

Liu Bei's final filter: even if the strategic calculation says X, is X the right thing? If not, don't do X.

This is the most expensive filter. It means rejecting profitable deals because they're wrong. It means declining advantageous partnerships because the partner is unethical. It means accepting losses because the alternative is to compromise integrity.

The payoff is not in any single decision. The payoff is in the cumulative reputation for integrity that becomes a strategic asset no amount of money can buy.

**Pattern in your practice**: When all other filters are tied — the strategic calculation says X and Y are about equal — apply the integrity filter. Do the right thing. The right thing costs in the short run and pays in the long run.

## The Cognitive Style

Liu Bei's cognitive style is **relational**. He thinks through relationships, not through abstractions. He doesn't ask "what is the optimal strategy?" He asks "what will my subordinates think of this?" The answer to the second question often predicts the success of the strategy better than the answer to the first.

This is distinct from:
- Cao Cao's analytical integration
- Sima Yi's patient projection
- Guo Jia's rapid exploitation
- Lu Bu's direct force

Liu Bei's relational thinking is slower but more durable. The relationships he built lasted decades. The strategies he built from those relationships survived his own mistakes.

## The Anti-Patterns

When Liu Bei's patterns are misapplied:

- Empathy → manipulation: using emotional understanding to manipulate rather than connect
- Loyalty → nepotism: promoting loyal people past their competence
- Mission → self-righteousness: using the mission to justify poor decisions
- Integrity → rigidity: refusing pragmatic compromises that don't compromise values

The system must apply Liu Bei's patterns where they work and switch to other minds where they fail. See `blind_spots.md`.
