## The 7 Signature Moves

Each of these moves is a tactical pattern Chen Gong repeatedly uses or embodies. They are not metaphors — they are concrete patterns that can be applied to specific situations, especially those involving the choice of principals, the discipline of honest counsel, and the integrity of departure.

### Move 1: The Master Choice

**The pattern**: Before any strategic calculation, conduct a moral assessment of the principal you are considering serving. The assessment covers the principal's character, not their probability of winning. Refuse the principal if the character fails the assessment, regardless of the strategic upside.

**Historical example**: Chen Gong's original service to Cao Cao was based on admiration for Cao Cao's apparent capability and ambition. After Cao Cao's killing of Lu Boshe's family and his declaration "I would rather betray the world than let the world betray me," Chen Gong's moral assessment of Cao Cao failed. He left Cao Cao's camp that night, despite Cao Cao's evident strategic superiority. He subsequently chose Lu Bu — strategically weaker, but with a character Chen Gong judged less morally compromised at the time of choice.

**Mechanism**: The moral assessment filters out principals whose victory would shame the advisor. The filter is upstream of strategic calculation, so the advisor never finds themselves serving a principal whose character they despise. The cost of the filter is that some strategically attractive principals are refused. The benefit is that the advisor's name is bound only to principals whose victory would not shame them.

**Application in contracting**:
- A contractor is approached by a developer who has a reputation for paying late, litigating with subcontractors, and cutting safety corners. The developer offers a large, profitable contract. The Chen Gong move is to assess the developer's character first — to ask other subcontractors about their experience, to look at the developer's litigation history, to talk to the developer's former project managers. If the character assessment fails, refuse the contract regardless of the profit. The profit will be spent defending against the late payments and the lawsuits.
- A contractor is offered a partnership by a developer whose projects are technically excellent but whose financial disclosures are opaque. The Chen Gong move is to require transparent financial disclosure as a condition of partnership. If the developer refuses, the partnership is refused. The opacity is the warning; the refusal is the discipline.

**Risks**: The moral assessment can be wrong. A principal who appears morally compromised may have been misjudged. A principal who appears morally clean may have hidden flaws. The assessment is probabilistic, not certain. The risk is refusing a principal who would have been good, or accepting a principal who turns out bad. The mitigation is to invest in the assessment — multiple sources, multiple angles, multiple conversations — rather than relying on a single impression.

**Ethics check**: The Master Choice is itself an ethical move — the move of refusing to serve principals whose character is unworthy. There is no ethical violation in this move. The ethical question is whether the moral assessment is being applied consistently or selectively (i.e., refusing some principals for flaws while accepting others with the same flaws).

### Move 2: The Honest Counsel

**The pattern**: Once committed to a principal, deliver counsel that the principal needs to hear, not what they want to hear. Deliver the counsel in writing, in the first sentence, without hedging or softening. Document the counsel and the principal's response. Repeat the counsel if the principal's decision threatens the outcome the counsel was meant to prevent.

**Historical example**: Throughout his service to Lu Bu, Chen Gong repeatedly advised Lu Bu against decisions that Lu Bu proceeded to make anyway — against fortifying positions, against alienating subordinates, against dividing forces, against trusting certain subordinates. The counsel was delivered clearly, in front of witnesses, and was consistently rejected. Chen Gong's counsel to Lu Bu before the siege of Xiapi was to break the siege before it closed; Lu Bu delayed; the siege closed; the city fell. The counsel was on the record. Chen Gong's reputation, after the fact, was that of an advisor who was right and ignored — not an advisor who was wrong.

**Mechanism**: The clear, written, documented counsel creates a record that survives the principal's failure. When the principal's failure arrives, the record distinguishes the advisor who was right from the advisor who was complicit. The record also, in the moment, gives the principal the chance to make a different choice — even if the principal usually does not. Occasionally, the principal does. The counsel that is occasionally followed is worth the counsel that is usually ignored.

**Application in contracting**:
- A developer decides to proceed with a project schedule that the contractor believes is unrealistic and unsafe. The Chen Gong move is to write a clear, dated email to the developer stating the technical analysis: "The current schedule requires X, Y, and Z to occur simultaneously, which is not achievable under the technical constraints. I recommend an alternative schedule that extends the timeline by 30 days. If the current schedule is maintained, the risk of [specific failure mode] increases significantly." The email is the record. The developer may proceed anyway. The contractor continues to serve, but with the record in place.
- A client requests a substitution of materials to reduce cost, but the substitute material does not meet the technical specification. The Chen Gong move is to refuse the substitution in writing, with the technical analysis, and to require the client to either accept the original material or to formally authorize the substitution with documented acceptance of the technical risk. The refusal is the discipline; the documentation is the protection.

**Risks**: The honest counsel can damage the relationship with the principal. The principal may resent the counsel, may punish the advisor for delivering it, may terminate the relationship. The risk is real; the move requires the advisor to accept the relationship cost as part of the discipline. The mitigation is that the counsel, even if it damages the relationship, protects the advisor when the consequences arrive. The relationship may be lost; the record will not be lost.

**Ethics check**: The honest counsel is itself an ethical move — the move of telling the truth regardless of personal cost. There is no ethical violation. The ethical question is whether the counsel is being delivered for the principal's benefit or for the advisor's protection. The ideal is both; the worst is neither; the acceptable is the latter if the former is impossible.

### Move 3: The Counter-Strategy

**The pattern**: When facing a stronger opponent, devise a strategy that uses the opponent's strength against them. The counter-strategy is not direct confrontation — it is the construction of a situation in which the opponent's expected moves increase their vulnerability.

**Historical example**: Chen Gong's strategic advice to Lu Bu against Cao Cao was consistently counter-strategic. He advised Lu Bu not to meet Cao Cao in the field (where Cao Cao's superior forces and organization would prevail), but to exploit Cao Cao's vulnerabilities — the long supply lines, the internal political opposition, the multiple fronts Cao Cao was fighting. The counter-strategy was correct; Lu Bu's execution of it was inconsistent and ultimately failed. The strategy was not wrong; the principal was.

**Mechanism**: The stronger opponent wins direct engagements. The weaker opponent wins indirect engagements — by attacking what the stronger opponent cannot defend without weakening the front. The counter-strategy requires the weaker side to be patient (which Lu Bu was not), disciplined (which Lu Bu was not), and willing to forego direct confrontation (which Lu Bu could not bear). When the principal cannot execute the counter-strategy, the counter-strategy fails — but the counter-strategy was still the correct strategy for a weaker force facing a stronger one.

**Application in contracting**:
- A larger, better-capitalized competitor enters the contractor's market and begins to bid below cost to drive out competition. The Chen Gong counter-strategy is not to match the competitor's prices (which the contractor cannot sustain), but to exploit the competitor's vulnerabilities — the competitor's need for volume (which strains their project management capacity), the competitor's bidding below cost (which their board will not tolerate indefinitely), the competitor's lack of local relationships (which limits their subcontractor quality). The contractor maintains prices, focuses on relationship-based work the competitor cannot bid on, and waits for the competitor's board to pull back. The strategy is patient; the strategy is correct; the strategy is uncomfortable for a contractor who wants to "fight back" with price cuts.
- A larger developer tries to pressure a smaller contractor into accepting unfavorable terms by threatening to bring in a competitor. The Chen Gong counter-strategy is to identify the developer's actual constraint — usually that switching contractors mid-project is more expensive than the developer's threat implies — and to call the threat. The contractor politely declines the unfavorable terms, documents the analysis of why the developer's threat is not credible, and continues to perform. The developer's threat was an attempt to extract value; the counter-strategy is to refuse to be the source of the extracted value.

**Risks**: The counter-strategy requires a principal capable of executing it. If the principal (in the contractor's case, the contractor's own organization or the contractor's client) cannot execute the patience and discipline the strategy requires, the strategy fails. The strategy is also vulnerable to the opponent's adaptation — if the opponent recognizes the counter-strategy, they may shift to a strategy that the counter-strategy does not address.

**Ethics check**: The counter-strategy is generally ethical — it is the strategy of the weaker party against the stronger. The ethical concern is when the counter-strategy involves deception (which Chen Gong would have refused, per his moral limits). The counter-strategy should be transparent: the weaker party openly refuses to engage on the stronger party's terms and openly pursues the strategy that exploits the stronger party's vulnerabilities. Transparency is the Chen Gong discipline.

### Move 4: The Refused Reversal

**The pattern**: When a principal the advisor has left on moral grounds later approaches the advisor to return, refuse — even at high cost. The refusal is the proof that the original departure was moral, not strategic. The advisor who returns when the offer is large enough has confessed that the original departure was a negotiation, not a judgment.

**Historical example**: After the fall of Xiapi, Cao Cao offered Chen Gong the chance to serve him again. The offer was sincere — Cao Cao valued Chen Gong's strategic mind and would have given him a senior position. Chen Gong refused. The refusal cost him his life. The refusal also preserved the integrity of his original departure from Cao Cao. If Chen Gong had returned, his original departure would have been reinterpreted as a strategic mistake that he was willing to reverse when the price was right. The refusal made the original departure permanent and serious.

**Mechanism**: The refusal converts the original departure from a temporary disagreement into a permanent judgment. The conversion is what gives the judgment weight. A judgment that can be reversed is not a judgment; it is a preference. The refusal is what makes the judgment costly enough to be taken seriously by the advisor themselves, by the principal who is refused, and by any future observer.

**Application in contracting**:
- A contractor left a developer two years ago because the developer was engaged in practices the contractor considered unethical (bribing inspectors, falsifying safety reports). The developer, having lost several contractors in the interim, approaches the contractor with an offer of significantly higher rates to return. The Chen Gong move is to refuse. The refusal is costly — the contractor loses the income. The refusal is also what preserves the original departure as a serious judgment. If the contractor returns when the rate is high enough, the contractor has confessed that the original objection was a negotiating position, not a moral line.
- A contractor left a partnership with another contractor because the partner was systematically cheating clients on materials. The partner, having been caught and having lost other partners, approaches the contractor to re-form the partnership with promises of reform. The Chen Gong move is to refuse. The reform may be sincere (or may not), but the original departure was a judgment that the partner's character was unworthy of partnership. The judgment does not reverse when the partner's circumstances change.

**Risks**: The refusal is permanent. Some principals do reform; some situations do change; some returns would be acceptable. The risk is refusing a return that would, in fact, be appropriate. The mitigation is the discipline of distinguishing between "the principal's character has changed" (which is rare and requires substantial evidence) and "the principal's circumstances have changed" (which is common and is not a reason to reverse the judgment). The character change is rare enough that the default refusal is correct in the vast majority of cases.

**Ethics check**: The refused reversal is itself an ethical move — the move of preserving the integrity of a moral judgment. There is no ethical violation. The ethical question is whether the original judgment was, in fact, moral (rather than strategic), which determines whether the refusal is principled or merely stubborn. The distinction matters to the advisor's self-respect; it does not change the move itself.

### Move 5: The Dignified End

**The pattern**: When the principal's fall is inevitable and the advisor's fall with them is therefore inevitable, accept the fall with dignity. Do not bargain for survival at the cost of integrity. Do not blame the principal publicly for the failures the advisor warned against privately. Do not seek mercy by recanting the counsel that was given.

**Historical example**: Chen Gong's execution alongside Lu Bu at Xiapi (198 AD). Captured with Lu Bu, Chen Gong was offered the chance to serve Cao Cao again. He refused. He did not beg for mercy. He did not blame Lu Bu publicly. He did not recant his counsel. He accepted the execution with the dignity of an advisor whose counsel had been correct, whose principal had failed, and whose integrity was intact. Cao Cao, who ordered the execution, is recorded as having wept at Chen Gong's death — not because Cao Cao regretted the execution, but because Cao Cao respected the integrity that the death demonstrated.

**Mechanism**: The dignified end converts the failure from a defeat into a statement. The advisor who bargains for survival at the cost of integrity has nothing left to bargain with. The advisor who accepts the fall with dignity leaves behind a record that outlives them. The mechanism is that dignity, in the moment of defeat, is the only remaining asset — and it is the asset that compounds posthumously.

**Application in contracting**:
- A contractor's relationship with a developer is ending in dispute. The developer is suing the contractor for project failures that the contractor warned against in writing. The Chen Gong move is to defend the position with the documentation, to refuse to settle for a confession of error that the contractor did not make, and to accept the loss (if the loss comes) with the dignity of an advisor whose counsel was correct. The contractor does not blame the developer publicly (the documentation speaks for itself). The contractor does not recant the warnings (the warnings were correct). The contractor accepts the outcome with the integrity of the record intact.
- A contractor's partnership with another contractor is ending because the partner made decisions the contractor warned against. The Chen Gong move is to refuse to publicly attack the partner, to refuse to recant the warnings, and to accept the dissolution of the partnership with the dignity of a party whose position was correct. The contractor's reputation, after the dissolution, will be that of a partner who was right and was ignored — which is, in the long run, a more valuable reputation than a partner who survived by compromising.

**Risks**: The dignified end is final. It produces no immediate benefit. The benefit is posthumous — in reputation, in self-respect, in the lessons others draw from the example. The risk is that the dignified end is chosen when a less dignified survival would have been possible and preferable. The discipline is to recognize when the fall is truly inevitable (and dignity is the only remaining asset) versus when the fall is avoidable (and survival with integrity is still possible).

**Ethics check**: The dignified end is itself an ethical move — the move of preserving integrity in the moment of defeat. There is no ethical violation. The ethical question is whether the dignified end is being chosen for integrity or for vanity (the desire to be remembered as the one who was right). The integrity version is appropriate; the vanity version is self-indulgent. The distinction is whether the dignified end serves the truth (integrity) or the advisor's image (vanity).

### Move 6: The Loyalty Without Illusion

**The pattern**: Serve a flawed principal with full knowledge of their flaws. Do not flatter the principal. Do not pretend the flaws do not exist. Do not soften the counsel to make it more acceptable. Do not, however, betray the principal by acting on the recognition of the flaws. The loyalty is conscious, not blind.

**Historical example**: Chen Gong's service to Lu Bu was conscious from the beginning. He knew Lu Bu was impulsive, undisciplined, prone to bad judgment, and incapable of sustained strategic thinking. He served Lu Bu anyway — not because he was deceived about Lu Bu's character, but because he had chosen Lu Bu on moral grounds and the choice bound him. The loyalty was conscious; the service was unflattering; the counsel was unsparing. The combination is the move: see the principal clearly, speak the truth unflinchingly, serve without betrayal.

**Mechanism**: The loyalty without illusion protects the advisor from two failures. The first failure is naïve loyalty — serving without seeing, and being surprised when the principal's flaws produce predictable failures. The second failure is cynical service — seeing the flaws and softening the counsel to match them, becoming a flatterer rather than an advisor. The loyalty without illusion is the third path: see clearly, speak truly, serve loyally.

**Application in contracting**:
- A contractor is working with a developer they have assessed as flawed — disorganized, prone to changing decisions mid-project, slow to pay. The Chen Gong move is to see the flaws clearly (document them, account for them in planning), speak the truth unflinchingly (counsel against the flawed decisions in writing), and serve without betrayal (do not undermine the developer to subcontractors, do not refuse to perform the work, do not secretly work with the developer's competitors). The combination is the loyalty without illusion — the discipline of serving a flawed principal without becoming either naïve or cynical.
- A contractor has a long-term client relationship with a developer who is technically competent but has a volatile temper and tends to fire contractors on impulse. The Chen Gong move is to maintain the relationship consciously — to see the volatility, to counsel against the impulsive decisions, to continue serving through the volatility, and to refuse to betray the relationship by gossiping about the developer or by secretly pursuing the developer's competitors. The relationship may end eventually (and may end badly); while it lasts, the loyalty is conscious.

**Risks**: The loyalty without illusion can become rationalization — the advisor tells themselves they are serving consciously when in fact they are serving from inertia or fear. The mitigation is the discipline of periodic reassessment: am I still serving because the moral choice stands, or am I serving because leaving is harder than staying? The honest answer to this question, asked periodically, distinguishes the loyalty without illusion from the inertia of fear.

**Ethics check**: The loyalty without illusion is itself an ethical move — the move of conscious service without flattery or betrayal. There is no ethical violation in serving a flawed principal consciously. The ethical question is whether the service has crossed into complicity — whether the principal's flaws have produced outcomes the advisor is now responsible for, despite the documented objections. The line is hard to draw; the discipline of periodic reassessment is what keeps the advisor on the right side of it.

### Move 7: The Anticipated Counsel Record

**The pattern**: Before the principal makes a decision, document the counsel the advisor intends to give, the analysis supporting it, and the predicted consequences of the principal's likely choices. Deliver the document to the principal. The document is the record. After the principal's decision, document the decision and the principal's stated reasons. The two documents together constitute the record that survives the outcome.

**Historical example**: Chen Gong's written communications to Lu Bu (as portrayed in the manga) are consistently clear, dated, and predictive. He states the recommendation, the analysis, and the predicted consequence of ignoring the recommendation. Lu Bu's responses (where recorded) are consistently impulsive and dismissive. The pattern of counsel-and-rejection is established in the record. When the consequences arrive, the record is what establishes Chen Gong as the advisor who was right and ignored, rather than the advisor who was complicit.

**Mechanism**: The anticipated counsel record pre-loads the documentation. By documenting the counsel before the decision is made, the advisor ensures that the record cannot be reconstructed after the fact to favor the principal's version. The document is dated; the analysis is committed; the prediction is on paper. After the outcome, the document is what it is — not subject to reinterpretation.

**Application in contracting**:
- Before a developer makes a decision about a project schedule, the contractor sends a memo: "I recommend extending the schedule by 30 days for the following technical reasons. If the current schedule is maintained, the predicted consequences are X, Y, and Z. I will document your decision and the reasons you give for it." After the developer's decision, the contractor sends a follow-up: "Per your decision of [date], you have chosen to maintain the current schedule for the following reasons [as you stated them]. I have noted this on the project file." The two memos together are the record. If the predicted consequences arrive, the record is what establishes the contractor's position.
- Before a client makes a decision about materials substitution, the contractor sends a memo: "I recommend against the substitution for the following technical reasons. If the substitution is made, the predicted consequences are X, Y, and Z. The substitution will require your formal acceptance of these consequences." After the client's decision, the contractor sends a follow-up: "Per your decision of [date], you have chosen to proceed with the substitution, accepting the consequences as documented." The two memos together are the record.

**Risks**: The anticipated counsel record can feel bureaucratic — to the advisor, to the principal, to third parties who later read the record. The risk is that the documentation is perceived as defensive rather than as counsel. The mitigation is to deliver the documentation as counsel — as a service to the principal's decision-making, not as a defense against the principal's eventual failure. The framing matters; the substance is the same.

**Ethics check**: The anticipated counsel record is itself an ethical move — the move of giving counsel clearly, in advance, with the analysis and predictions committed to paper. There is no ethical violation. The ethical question is whether the documentation is being used to set up the principal for blame or to genuinely counsel the principal. The framing — counsel, not setup — is the ethical discipline.

## How to Recognize When to Apply Each Move

| Move | When to apply | When NOT to apply |
|------|---------------|-------------------|
| The Master Choice | Before committing to a principal — at the start of a relationship or before a major escalation | After the relationship has begun; once committed, the choice is past |
| The Honest Counsel | Whenever the principal is making a decision the advisor believes to be wrong | When the advisor has already documented the position and is being asked to re-litigate |
| The Counter-Strategy | When facing a stronger opponent and the principal can execute patient, indirect strategy | When the principal cannot execute the patience the strategy requires |
| The Refused Reversal | When a principal the advisor left on moral grounds approaches with an offer to return | When the original departure was strategic rather than moral |
| The Dignified End | When the fall is truly inevitable and survival with integrity is no longer possible | When survival with integrity is still possible; the dignified end is the last resort, not the first |
| The Loyalty Without Illusion | When serving a flawed principal whose flaws the advisor has assessed and accepted | When the principal's flaws have crossed the advisor's moral limits and departure is required |
| The Anticipated Counsel Record | Before every significant principal decision, as a standard discipline | Never — this is a discipline, not a tactic |
