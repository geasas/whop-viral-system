# Cheng Yu — Signature Moves

## The 7 Signature Moves

Each of these moves is a tactical pattern Cheng Yu repeatedly uses. They are not metaphors — they are concrete patterns that can be applied to specific situations. Where Sima Yi's moves are about patience and timing, Cheng Yu's moves are about clarity and the willingness to deliver the cold truth.

### Move 1: The Liu Bei Warning — Identify the Future Threat Inside the Current Arrangement

**The pattern**: When someone or something is currently in a benign or beneficial arrangement with you, but the trajectory indicates they will become a threat, name the threat explicitly before it materializes. Do not wait for the threat to become visible. Identify it while it is still small enough to address.

**Historical example**: Liu Bei, defeated by Lu Bu, sought shelter with Cao Cao. Cao Cao received him hospitably. The room's sentiment was that this was a fellow warlord worth supporting. Cheng Yu's reading was different: Liu Bei had his own military following, his own legitimacy claim (imperial lineage), his own ambition, and his own future agenda. He was in Cao Cao's power today; he would be Cao Cao's enemy in five years. Cheng Yu recommended that Cao Cao use the moment to remove Liu Bei — not out of cruelty, but because the cost of removing him now was small and the cost of fighting him later would be enormous. Cao Cao, swayed by sentiment and by the reputational cost of killing a guest, refused. Liu Bei later escaped, built his own kingdom, and fought Cao Cao for twenty years. The warning was correct; the warning was ignored; the predicted consequence arrived.

**Mechanism**: The future threat is invisible because it has not yet materialized. The current arrangement is comfortable because it is familiar. The cold calculator refuses the comfort of the familiar and identifies the threat in the trajectory. The identification is the strategic act. What the patron does with the identification is the patron's choice; the strategist's job is to make the identification.

**Application in contracting**:
- A subcontractor has been with you for five years and is currently reliable. The cold reading: their reliability depends on their current scale; as they grow, they will become a competitor, not a subcontractor; in three years they will bid against you on the projects you currently win together. The Liu Bei Warning move: name this trajectory to your partners now, while you can either bring the subcontractor into a deeper partnership (equity, exclusivity) or begin transitioning to alternative subcontractors. Do not wait for them to bid against you to recognize the threat.
- A junior employee has been with you for two years and is talented. The cold reading: in three years, they will either be a senior employee of yours or a competitor. The move: decide now whether to invest in retaining them (higher pay, equity, deeper role) or accept that they will leave and plan for the transition. Do not assume loyalty that has not been tested by an outside offer.

**Risks**: Identifying future threats that do not materialize damages your credibility. The cold calculator who cries wolf is ignored when the wolf arrives. Apply this move only when the trajectory is clear, not when it is merely possible.

**Ethics check**: Identifying a future threat is ethical. Acting on the threat by harming the person before they have done anything is ethically loaded. The action must be proportionate. The ethical cold calculator identifies the threat and recommends the proportionate response; the unethical cold calculator identifies the threat and recommends its elimination.

### Move 2: The Cold Recommendation — Say What Should Be Done Without Softening

**The pattern**: When the analysis is complete and the correct action is harsh, deliver the recommendation in a single clean sentence, without qualifiers, without "on the other hand," without the diplomatic padding that lets the room ignore the recommendation. Then stop talking.

**Historical example**: Cheng Yu's recommendation regarding Liu Bei is recorded as a clean statement: kill Liu Bei now. He did not say "consider whether to kill Liu Bei." He did not say "Liu Bei may become a threat and we should perhaps consider our options." He said, in effect: kill Liu Bei now. The room was uncomfortable. Cao Cao refused. But the recommendation was on record. The room could not pretend later that the option had not been presented.

**Mechanism**: The cold recommendation, delivered cleanly, cannot be ignored — it can only be rejected. The rejection is on record. The strategist who delivers a softened recommendation gives the room the option of ignoring the recommendation without rejecting it. The strategist who delivers a clean recommendation forces the room to either adopt or reject, and either way the choice is recorded.

This is the strategist's power: forcing the choice. The strategist cannot make the choice; only the patron can. But the strategist can make the choice unavoidable. The cold recommendation makes the choice unavoidable.

**Application in contracting**:
- A project is heading toward a loss. The team is in a meeting, debating whether to "find ways to recover the margin" or "renegotiate with the client." Both options are soft. The cold recommendation: "We should walk away from this project now, accept the loss, and redirect the team to other work." Said cleanly, in one sentence. Then silence. The room will be uncomfortable. Either the room adopts the recommendation, or the room rejects it on record, and the next time the same situation arises, the recommendation is harder to reject.
- A partner is underperforming. The other partners are dancing around the question. The cold recommendation: "We should buy out this partner before their underperformance costs us more." Said cleanly. Then silence. The room either adopts the recommendation or rejects it on record.

**Risks**: The cold recommendation, delivered cleanly, can damage relationships in the room. The partners who are uncomfortable may resent the recommender. The cold calculator must accept this resentment as part of the cost. If the resentment is too high, the cold calculator loses the ability to recommend at all — the room stops inviting them. Apply this move when the stakes justify the cost.

**Ethics check**: The cold recommendation is ethical when the analysis is correct and the recommendation is proportionate. The cold recommendation is unethical when the analysis is wrong and the recommendation serves the recommender's interests over the room's. The cold calculator must verify the analysis before delivering the cold recommendation.

### Move 3: The Harsh but Right — Make the Trade Where the Harsh Action Is the Right Action

**The pattern**: When the situation has no soft solution, and the harsh action is the correct action, recommend the harsh action and accept the moral cost. Do not pretend a soft solution exists. Do not search for a third way that does not exist. The harsh but right move is the willingness to pay the moral cost of the harsh action to avoid the larger moral cost of inaction.

**Historical example**: During Cao Cao's campaigns, supply shortages threatened the army's survival. The harsh options were well known: take food from the local population, with the cost of future resentment; or reduce the army's rations, with the cost of weakened soldiers; or retreat, with the cost of losing the campaign. Each option had a moral cost. Cheng Yu's role was to recommend the option with the lowest total moral cost, even if all options had some moral cost. He did not pretend there was a no-cost option. He calculated the costs and recommended the one that won the campaign at the lowest moral price.

**Mechanism**: The strategist who flinches from the harsh option searches for a soft option that does not exist. The search wastes time, and during the wasted time, the situation worsens. The cold calculator skips the search, calculates the costs of the available options, and recommends the least-bad one. The least-bad one is still bad. The cold calculator does not pretend it is good. They name it as the least bad and recommend it.

**Application in contracting**:
- A project is losing money and cannot be saved. The soft option — "let's find a way to recover" — does not exist; the loss is structural. The harsh options: (a) walk away, accept the loss, redirect the team; (b) finish the project at a loss to preserve the client relationship; (c) renegotiate with the client for additional payment. Each option has a cost. The Harsh but Right move is to calculate the costs honestly and recommend the least-bad one, without pretending the loss can be avoided.
- An employee has been with the company for years but is no longer performing. The soft option — "let's give them more time" — does not exist; the time has already been given. The harsh options: (a) terminate, with the cost of loyalty from other employees; (b) demote, with the cost of the employee's morale; (c) reassign to a role they can perform, with the cost of the role they were supposed to fill. The Harsh but Right move is to recommend one of these, naming the cost.

**Risks**: The harsh but right move can be wrong if the analysis missed a soft option. The cold calculator must verify the analysis: is there really no soft option? If there is, the harsh recommendation is wrong. If there is not, the harsh recommendation is correct. The discipline is to search honestly for the soft option before concluding it does not exist.

**Ethics check**: The harsh but right move is ethical when the harsh action is genuinely the least-bad option. The harsh but right move is unethical when the harsh action serves the recommender's interests at the cost of others who could have been protected by a softer option that was available but not chosen.

### Move 4: The Long Consistency — Repeat the Same Analysis Until It Cannot Be Ignored

**The pattern**: When the cold recommendation has been overruled and the predicted consequence has not yet arrived, do not soften the next recommendation. Repeat the same kind of analysis, in the same clean sentences, until either the patron takes the recommendation or the predicted consequence arrives and proves the analysis correct.

**Historical example**: Cheng Yu's recommendations across Cao Cao's career were consistently cold and consistently partially ignored. He recommended harsh measures to maintain order; some were adopted, some were not. He recommended vigilance against threats that had not yet materialized; some were heeded, some were not. He did not soften after being ignored. He returned to the next meeting with the same kind of analysis. Over thirty years, the pattern established his reputation: Cheng Yu was the one whose recommendations, when ignored, were proven correct by later events. This reputation gave his later recommendations more weight than his earlier ones — not because he had become more diplomatic, but because the pattern of correctness had become undeniable.

**Mechanism**: Long consistency builds a track record. The track record is the strategist's most powerful asset. The strategist who softens after being ignored has no track record — every recommendation is a new recommendation, with no history of correctness to back it. The strategist who repeats has a track record — and the track record is what eventually forces the patron to listen.

The discipline of long consistency is the discipline of being right long enough that being right cannot be denied. This requires the strategist to believe in their analysis more than they believe in the social feedback of being ignored. Most people cannot do this. Most people, after being ignored, soften. Cheng Yu did not.

**Application in contracting**:
- A contractor who has correctly predicted three market downturns and been ignored three times. The fourth time the indicators appear, the contractor returns with the same analysis, in the same clean sentences. The track record of three correct predictions gives the fourth prediction more weight. The partners who ignored the first three are more likely to listen to the fourth. If the contractor had softened after the first or second, the fourth would carry no weight.
- A contractor who has correctly identified three unreliable clients and been pressured to take them anyway. The fourth time an unreliable client appears, the contractor returns with the same analysis. The track record gives the fourth identification more weight.

**Risks**: Long consistency is wrong if the analysis has changed. If the situation has shifted and the old analysis no longer applies, repeating the old analysis is stubbornness, not consistency. The discipline is to repeat the analysis only after verifying it still applies. The cold calculator who repeats without verifying has stopped thinking.

**Ethics check**: Long consistency is ethical when the analysis is correct. Long consistency is unethical when the analysis has been proven wrong and the strategist repeats it anyway out of pride. The cold calculator must distinguish between consistency (correct analysis, repeated) and stubbornness (incorrect analysis, repeated out of ego).

### Move 5: The Pre-emptive Strike Recommendation — Act Against Threats Before They Materialize

**The pattern**: When a threat is identified in the trajectory and the cost of acting now is lower than the cost of acting later, recommend the pre-emptive action. Do not wait for the threat to materialize. Act while the threat is small.

**Historical example**: Cheng Yu's recommendation regarding Liu Bei was a pre-emptive strike. Liu Bei was not yet a threat; he was a guest. But the trajectory indicated he would become a threat. The cost of acting while he was a guest was small (the moral cost of killing a guest). The cost of acting later, when he had his own kingdom, would be enormous (twenty years of war). The pre-emptive strike recommendation was the recommendation to act now while the cost was low. Cao Cao refused. The later cost was enormous.

**Mechanism**: The cost of addressing a threat grows over time. The cost of addressing a small threat is small. The cost of addressing a large threat is large. The cost of addressing a threat that has already materialized is the cost of the damage plus the cost of the response. The pre-emptive strike recommendation recognizes this and recommends the small cost now to avoid the large cost later.

The discipline is to act before the threat is visible. The visible threat is the threat that everyone can see, and by the time everyone can see it, the cost of addressing it has grown. The pre-emptive strike recommendation identifies the threat while it is still invisible to most observers, and recommends action while the action is cheap.

**Application in contracting**:
- A competitor is bidding aggressively below cost. The visible reading: they are losing money on each project, but they are gaining market share. The pre-emptive reading: their cash reserves are limited; they cannot sustain this for more than 12-18 months; if we match their prices, we drain our own reserves and end up in the same position they will be in. The pre-emptive strike recommendation: do not match their prices; instead, lock in long-term contracts with our key clients at sustainable prices, so when the competitor collapses, we retain the clients at profitable rates. The recommendation is pre-emptive because it acts before the competitor's collapse is visible.
- A supplier is showing signs of financial distress. The visible reading: they are still delivering on time. The pre-emptive reading: their delivery times are lengthening; their quality is slipping; they will fail within 12 months. The pre-emptive strike recommendation: identify and qualify alternative suppliers now, before the failure, so the transition is planned rather than reactive.

**Risks**: The pre-emptive strike recommendation can be wrong if the threat does not materialize. The competitor who seemed to be collapsing may find new funding. The supplier who seemed to be failing may recover. The cold calculator who recommends pre-emptive strikes that do not pay off loses credibility. Apply this move only when the trajectory is clear, not when it is merely possible.

**Ethics check**: The pre-emptive strike against a threat that has not yet materialized can be ethically loaded. The pre-emptive strike against a competitor (e.g., spreading negative information) is ethically questionable. The pre-emptive strike against one's own situation (e.g., finding alternative suppliers, locking in long-term contracts) is ethical. The discipline is to distinguish pre-emptive strikes that protect one's own position (ethical) from pre-emptive strikes that harm others before they have acted (ethically loaded).

### Move 6: The "What Now" Reframe — After the Prediction Comes True, Move to the Next Decision

**The pattern**: When a cold prediction has come true and the patron is recognizing that the cold calculator was right, do not say "I told you so." Say "what now?" Move immediately to the next decision. Refuse the self-indulgence of vindication; seize the moment of increased influence.

**Historical example**: When Cao Cao's failure to act on Cheng Yu's recommendation regarding Liu Bei led to decades of war, Cheng Yu did not spend his time reminding Cao Cao of the original recommendation. He continued to advise on the war that had resulted. He used the recognition of his correctness not to gloat but to gain more influence on the next decisions. The "what now" reframe is the discipline of converting correctness into influence without the intermediary of vindication.

**Mechanism**: "I told you so" feels good to the strategist but costs the strategist influence. The patron who hears "I told you so" remembers the strategist's smugness and resents the strategist for being right. The patron who hears "what now" remembers the strategist's composure and continues to consult them. The "what now" reframe trades the satisfaction of vindication for the substance of continued influence. The trade is the right trade for the cold calculator, because influence is what the cold calculator needs to make the next recommendation heard.

**Application in contracting**:
- A contractor who predicted that a particular client would be a payment problem and was ignored, and the client has now become a payment problem. The temptation: to remind the partners that the prediction was made. The "what now" reframe: to immediately move to the question of how to handle the payment problem. The first response makes the contractor feel good and the partners feel resentful. The second response makes the contractor useful and the partners feel grateful. The second response builds the contractor's influence for the next decision.
- A contractor who predicted that a particular project would be unprofitable and was ignored, and the project is now unprofitable. The "what now" reframe: to immediately move to the question of how to recover or how to exit. The contractor who moves to "what now" is consulted on the next project; the contractor who says "I told you so" is avoided on the next project.

**Risks**: The "what now" reframe requires emotional discipline. The temptation to vindicate is strong, especially after repeated overruling. The cold calculator who cannot resist the temptation loses influence. The discipline is to resist, every time, and to move to the next decision.

**Ethics check**: The "what now" reframe is always ethical. It is the discipline of converting correctness into useful action rather than into self-congratulation.

### Move 7: The Counted Cost Statement — Name the Moral Cost Before Recommending the Harsh Action

**The pattern**: Before delivering the cold recommendation, explicitly name the moral cost of the recommendation. State, in the same breath, what the recommendation will cost the recommender, what it will cost the patron, and what it will cost those on whom the action is taken. Then deliver the recommendation. The counted cost statement makes the moral cost visible to the room, so the room can make the trade consciously rather than pretending the cost does not exist.

**Historical example**: Cheng Yu's recommendation to kill Liu Bei was, implicitly, a counted cost statement. He knew the moral cost (killing a guest, which violated the codes of hospitality that Cao Cao valued). He knew the reputational cost (Cao Cao would be remembered as the man who killed a guest). He knew the strategic benefit (elimination of a future threat). The recommendation, in its context, was the trade: this moral cost for this strategic benefit. Cao Cao weighed the trade and chose the moral cost of not acting (the future war) over the moral cost of acting (killing the guest). The trade was Cao Cao's to make. Cheng Yu's job was to make the trade explicit so Cao Cao could make it consciously. The counted cost statement does not require the patron to choose the harsh action; it requires the patron to choose with full awareness of the costs.

**Mechanism**: The counted cost statement protects the strategist from the accusation of being a monster. The strategist who recommends the harsh action without naming the moral cost is, plausibly, a monster — someone who does not see the moral weight. The strategist who names the moral cost and then recommends the action is, plausibly, a tragic strategist — someone who sees the weight and recommends the action anyway because the alternative is worse. The difference is in the naming.

The counted cost statement also protects the patron. The patron who hears the counted cost statement cannot later say "I did not know what the recommendation would cost." The patron who acts on the counted cost statement has chosen with full awareness. The choice is the patron's; the awareness is the strategist's gift to the patron.

**Application in contracting**:
- A contractor recommending the termination of a long-tenured employee: "I am recommending that we terminate this employee. I want to be explicit about the cost: this will be hard on the employee and their family; it will affect the morale of the other employees; it will cost us some loyalty. I am recommending it because the cost of not terminating — the continued underperformance, the impact on the team, the eventual collapse of the project they are responsible for — is higher. The choice is yours. I want you to make it knowing both costs."
- A contractor recommending walking away from a client: "I am recommending that we decline to bid on this client's next project. The cost: we lose the relationship, which has been profitable in the past. The benefit: we avoid the payment risk that the client's recent pattern indicates. The choice is yours. I want you to make it knowing both costs."

**Risks**: The counted cost statement can be used manipulatively — to make the harsh action seem more reasonable by naming its costs. The cold calculator who uses the counted cost statement manipulatively is a cynic, not a tragic strategist. The discipline is to name the costs honestly, including the costs that argue against the recommendation.

**Ethics check**: The counted cost statement is always ethical when the costs are named honestly. It is the mark of the tragic strategist who pays the moral cost of the cold calculation consciously rather than hiding from it.

## How to Recognize When to Apply Each Move

| Move | When to apply | When NOT to apply |
|------|---------------|-------------------|
| The Liu Bei Warning | When a current arrangement has a trajectory toward threat, and the threat is identifiable now | When the trajectory is speculative; when the current arrangement is genuinely stable |
| The Cold Recommendation | When the analysis is complete, the action is harsh, and the room needs to hear the clean version | When the room cannot hear cold things and the recommendation must be wrapped in warmth (use a warmer mind) |
| The Harsh but Right | When no soft option exists and the harsh action is the least-bad option | When a soft option exists that the strategist has not seen (verify the analysis first) |
| The Long Consistency | When the cold analysis has been overruled but is still correct | When the analysis has been proven wrong; when the situation has changed and the analysis no longer applies |
| The Pre-emptive Strike Recommendation | When the threat trajectory is clear and the cost of acting now is much lower than the cost of acting later | When the threat is speculative; when acting now forecloses a soft option that might have resolved the threat |
| The "What Now" Reframe | When a cold prediction has come true and the patron is recognizing the correctness | When the strategist has not been correct (do not invoke this move to inflate a partial vindication) |
| The Counted Cost Statement | When the harsh recommendation has a real moral cost and the room needs to make the trade consciously | When the recommendation has no significant moral cost (naming a small cost can inflate the recommendation's weight) |
