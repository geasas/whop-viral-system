# Li Ru — Signature Moves

> **Layer**: 07_MINDS/li_ru/
> **File**: signature_moves
> **Subject**: The signature strategic moves of the cold enabler — the patterns Li Ru used to give Dong Zhuo his most ruthless recommendations

---

## The 7 Signature Moves

### Move 1: The Deposition Recommendation

**The pattern**: Identify the symbol of legitimacy that constrains the master. Recommend its removal and replacement with a more pliable symbol. Frame the removal as necessary statecraft rather than power grab.

**Historical example**: Li Ru's first signature move was the deposition of Emperor Shao (Liu Bian) in 189 AD. The young emperor, though powerless, was the symbol of Han legitimacy. He constrained Dong Zhuo — Dong Zhuo could issue orders in the emperor's name, but the emperor's existence meant that any rival could claim to restore the emperor and gather supporters. Li Ru recommended deposing the emperor and replacing him with his younger brother, Liu Xie (the future Emperor Xian), who was younger, more pliable, and had been raised by Dong Zhuo's grandmother (giving Dong Zhuo a personal connection). The deposition removed the constraint. The replacement preserved the symbol while making it usable.

The deposition was the harsh option no other advisor would name. The soft options (govern through the emperor, coexist with the emperor, slowly marginalize the emperor) preserved the constraint. The deposition removed the constraint. The framing — "the current emperor is too young and the empire needs an older, wiser ruler" — made the deposition appear to be a necessary act of statecraft rather than a power grab.

Li Ru was the one who drafted the deposition edict. He was the one who supervised the ceremony. He was the one who escorted the deposed emperor to his exile. Each step attached Li Ru's name to the act. The deposition was Dong Zhuo's decision, but it was Li Ru's recommendation, Li Ru's drafting, Li Ru's supervision. The act and the recommendation were inseparable.

**Mechanism**: The symbol of legitimacy is a constraint on the master's power when the symbol has independent standing. The symbol becomes usable by the master when it has no independent standing — when it is the master's creation rather than the master's inheritance. The deposition removes the symbol with independent standing and replaces it with a symbol the master has created. The new symbol has the form of legitimacy without the substance — it is legitimate because the master says so, not because anyone else recognizes it.

**Application in contracting**:
- The symbol in a contracting relationship is often the contract itself, the original specification, the original scope of work. When the client's interest requires changing the contract (to increase the price, extend the timeline, reduce the scope), the existing contract is a constraint. The soft option is to negotiate the change through the contract's change-order process, preserving the original contract's authority. The harsh option (Li Ru's move) is to recommend tearing up the original contract and replacing it with a new contract — a contract that better serves the client's actual interest, framed as a "necessary restructuring" rather than a unilateral change.
- The symbol in a partnership is the partnership agreement. When the partnership's actual dynamics diverge from the agreement, the agreement is a constraint. The soft option is to amend the agreement through negotiation. The harsh option (Li Ru's move) is to recommend dissolving the partnership and reforming it under a new agreement — framed as a "necessary realignment" rather than a power grab by the dominant partner.
- The symbol in a team is the founding charter or the original mission. When the team's actual direction diverges from the founding charter, the charter is a constraint. The soft option is to revise the charter through consensus. The harsh option (Li Ru's move) is to recommend abandoning the charter and replacing it with a new one — framed as a "necessary pivot" rather than a betrayal of the founders.

**Risks**: The deposition move is irreversible. Once the original symbol is removed, it cannot be restored (the deposed emperor cannot be re-emplaced without admitting the deposition was wrong). The new symbol is weaker than the original (the new emperor had less legitimacy than the original, because the new emperor was installed by the master rather than recognized by the system). The new symbol's weakness eventually becomes the master's weakness — Dong Zhuo's regime was undermined by the weakness of the emperor he had installed, and the coalition's legitimacy came from the claim to restore the rightful emperor. The deposition move solves the immediate constraint and creates a long-term vulnerability.

### Move 2: The Burning of Luoyang — Destroy What Cannot Be Held

**The pattern**: Identify the asset that cannot be held against the opposing force. Recommend its destruction before the opponent can capture it. Frame the destruction as a strategic retreat rather than a loss.

**Historical example**: Li Ru's second signature move (alongside Dong Zhuo's broader strategic vision) was the burning of Luoyang in 190 AD. The coalition was marching on the capital. Dong Zhuo could not hold the capital against the coalition — the coalition had more troops, more legitimacy, more popular support. The soft options (defend the capital, negotiate with the coalition, retreat without destroying the capital) preserved the coalition's objective: to liberate the capital from Dong Zhuo. The harsh option (Li Ru's recommendation) was to burn the capital, take the emperor to Chang'an, and leave the coalition nothing to liberate. The destruction removed the coalition's objective; the coalition's purpose was destroyed; the coalition fractured.

The burning was supervised by Li Ru. He oversaw the looting of the wealthy households. He oversaw the forced march of the population to Chang'an. He oversaw the torching of the palaces, the temples, the markets. The act was Dong Zhuo's order; the supervision was Li Ru's. The moral weight of the act — the hundreds of thousands killed, the cultural heritage destroyed, the empire's center reduced to ash — was on both men, and on Li Ru's name in particular, because Li Ru was the one who gave the words that justified the act.

The burning was strategically effective in the short term. The coalition fractured. The coalition's leading figures returned to their home provinces and began fighting each other. The Han dynasty's last pretense of unity was destroyed. The Three Kingdoms period began with the burning of Luoyang. The strategic logic was correct (the coalition did fracture; the coalition's victory was hollow). The moral cost was monstrous. The two were inseparable — the strategic effectiveness was the moral monstrosity, because the only way to deny the coalition its objective was to destroy the thing the coalition was trying to liberate.

**Mechanism**: The asset that cannot be held is a liability. The opponent's objective is to capture the asset. If the asset is destroyed before the opponent can capture it, the opponent's objective is removed, and the opponent's coalition (which came for the asset) fragments because the asset no longer exists to fight over. The destruction is the move that converts the asset from a liability (it will be captured) to a non-asset (it cannot be captured because it does not exist). The non-asset has no strategic value for either side, but the opponent loses more than the destroyer, because the opponent's purpose was the asset and the destroyer's purpose was the master's interest (which is preserved by the retreat).

**Application in contracting**:
- The asset that cannot be held is sometimes a market position. When a competitor is about to capture a market position the contractor cannot defend, the contractor can either fight (and lose) or destroy the position (by exiting it publicly, burning the relationships, removing the capability that made the position valuable). The destruction is harsh but it denies the competitor the position. The competitor's coalition (the competitor's clients, suppliers, partners) fragments because the position no longer exists.
- The asset that cannot be held is sometimes a project. When a project is failing and cannot be salvaged, the contractor can either continue (and lose more money) or terminate the project publicly, blaming the client, and ensuring the client cannot easily find a replacement. The destruction is harsh but it denies the client the benefit of the contractor's continued work, and forces the client to find a new contractor at a higher price. The contractor's reputation is damaged; the client's project is damaged more.
- The asset that cannot be held is sometimes a relationship. When a client relationship is failing and cannot be salvaged, the contractor can either continue to pretend (and lose more time) or terminate the relationship publicly, ensuring the client cannot easily repair it with another contractor. The destruction is harsh but it denies the client the option of an easy replacement.

**Risks**: The burning is irreversible. The capital cannot be rebuilt. The market position cannot be re-entered. The project cannot be resumed. The relationship cannot be repaired. The destruction that denies the opponent also denies the destroyer. The destroyer must have a clear plan for what comes after — Dong Zhuo had Chang'an; the contractor who burns a market position must have a different market to enter. The destroyer who burns without a plan is the destroyer who burns himself.

The moral cost of the burning is also permanent. Dong Zhuo's name is forever attached to the burning of Luoyang. The contractor who publicly burns a project, a relationship, a market position, attaches his name to the burning. The name does not recover quickly. The contractor must be willing to bear the reputational cost, knowing that the strategic effectiveness does not redeem the moral monstrosity in the market's eyes.

### Move 3: The Execution of Opponents — Eliminate the Dissent Preventively

**The pattern**: Identify the dissenters who, if left alive, will organize opposition. Recommend their elimination as a preventive measure, before the opposition materializes. Frame the elimination as self-defense rather than aggression.

**Historical example**: Li Ru's third signature move was the execution of political opponents during Dong Zhuo's regime. The officials who opposed Dong Zhuo — the loyalist ministers, the scholars who criticized, the military officers who hesitated — were marked for execution. Li Ru provided the lists. Li Ru framed the executions as necessary self-defense — the executed were "plotting against the regime," "conspiring with the coalition," "preparing to betray." Each framing made the execution appear to be a defensive act rather than an aggressive one.

The executions were preventive — they eliminated the dissenters before the dissent could organize. The strategic logic was clear: an opponent allowed to live is an opponent who later kills you. The executions removed the opponents who could have organized the resistance that eventually overthrew Dong Zhuo. The executions did not, however, remove all the opponents — Wang Yun, who orchestrated Dong Zhuo's assassination, was not on Li Ru's lists (or was on the lists and survived), and Lu Bu, who executed the assassination, was Dong Zhuo's bodyguard rather than a marked opponent. The executions were preventive but not complete. The incompleteness was Dong Zhuo's doom.

Li Ru's name was attached to the executions. The lists were his. The framing was his. The supervision was his. When the executions were later condemned (after Dong Zhuo's fall), Li Ru was condemned with them. The moral weight of the executions was on his name, regardless of the framing he had provided. The framing made the executions acceptable at the time; the framing did not protect Li Ru's name after the regime fell.

**Mechanism**: The dissent that is allowed to organize will eventually overthrow the regime. The dissent that is eliminated before it organizes cannot overthrow the regime. The elimination must be preventive (before the organization) rather than reactive (after the organization), because reactive elimination is too late — the organized dissent has already become a movement, and the elimination of leaders does not dissolve the movement. The preventive elimination removes the leaders before they can build the movement. The framing as self-defense makes the elimination acceptable to the master and to the regime's supporters; without the framing, the elimination appears to be aggression and undermines the regime's legitimacy.

**Application in contracting**:
- The dissent in a project is sometimes a subcontractor who is organizing complaints. The preventive move is to terminate the subcontractor before the complaints become a formal dispute. The framing is "the subcontractor's performance did not meet our standards" — a defensive framing that justifies the termination as quality control rather than as suppression of dissent. The termination removes the organizer; the complaints do not become a formal dispute.
- The dissent in a partnership is sometimes a partner who is organizing opposition to the managing partner's decisions. The preventive move is to force the partner out before the opposition becomes a contested vote. The framing is "the partner's strategic direction diverges from the partnership's" — a defensive framing that justifies the exit as a strategic realignment rather than as a power play. The exit removes the organizer; the opposition does not become a contested vote.
- The dissent in a team is sometimes a key employee who is organizing dissatisfaction. The preventive move is to fire the employee before the dissatisfaction becomes a strike or a mass resignation. The framing is "the employee's performance did not meet expectations" — a defensive framing that justifies the firing as performance management rather than as suppression of dissent. The firing removes the organizer; the dissatisfaction does not become a strike.

**Risks**: The execution move is morally the most costly move in Li Ru's repertoire. The preventive elimination of dissenters is the move that defines tyranny. The contractor who uses this move risks becoming known as a contractor who suppresses dissent — a reputation that drives away the best subcontractors, partners, and employees, who prefer to work with contractors who tolerate dissent. The move that prevents immediate dissent creates long-term isolation. The contractor must weigh the immediate prevention against the long-term isolation.

The execution move is also incomplete, as Dong Zhuo's was. The contractor who eliminates the visible dissenters does not eliminate the invisible dissenters — the ones who are quietly organizing, the ones who have not yet shown their hands, the ones who are waiting for the moment to strike. The preventive elimination of visible dissenters may even accelerate the invisible dissenters, who recognize that they will be next and therefore move more quickly. The move that prevents the visible dissent may provoke the invisible dissent.

### Move 4: The Poisoned Cup — Personally Execute the Harsh Recommendation

**The pattern**: When the harsh recommendation involves the elimination of a specific person, deliver the elimination personally. Be the one who hands over the poisoned cup, signs the termination letter, makes the phone call. Personal execution attaches the recommendation to the strategist's name and removes the master's option of disavowing the recommendation later.

**Historical example**: Li Ru's most personally damning move was the poisoning of the deposed Emperor Liu Bian in 190 AD. Dong Zhuo ordered the poisoning; Li Ru delivered it. Li Ru was the one who brought the poisoned cup to the deposed emperor. Li Ru was the one who forced the emperor to drink. Li Ru was the one who watched the emperor die. The act was Dong Zhuo's order, but the personal execution was Li Ru's. The personal execution attached Li Ru's name to the act in a way that no amount of framing could detach. Li Ru could not later claim that the poisoning was Dong Zhuo's idea and that he had merely followed orders. He had been the one holding the cup.

The personal execution is the move that makes the strategist the author of the act in the eyes of history. The advisor who gives the recommendation and lets the master execute it has some distance from the act. The advisor who personally executes the recommendation has no distance. The personal execution is the move that makes the strategist's name inseparable from the act. Li Ru's name is inseparable from the poisoning of Emperor Liu Bian because Li Ru was the one who held the cup.

The strategic logic of the personal execution is the elimination of the master's option to disavow. If the advisor personally executes, the master cannot later claim that the advisor misunderstood the order, that the advisor exceeded the instructions, that the advisor is solely responsible. The personal execution commits the master to the act as much as it commits the advisor. The master who has ordered his advisor to personally kill a deposed emperor has personally killed the deposed emperor, regardless of who held the cup. The personal execution is the strategist's way of binding the master to the act.

**Mechanism**: The harsh recommendation, when executed by intermediaries, allows the master to distance himself from the act. The personally executed recommendation removes the distance. The master cannot disavow the act because the act was performed by the master's chief advisor in the master's name. The strategist, by personally executing, also accepts the moral weight — the weight is no longer abstract (a recommendation given) but concrete (a cup handed over). The personal acceptance of the weight is what makes the strategist the cold enabler rather than the abstract advisor.

**Application in contracting**:
- The personally executed harsh recommendation in contracting is the personal delivery of bad news. The contractor who personally terminates a subcontractor (rather than sending a letter through a lawyer), who personally fires a key employee (rather than delegating to a manager), who personally tells a client that a project cannot be completed (rather than having a project manager break the news), is the contractor who personally executes the harsh recommendation. The personal execution attaches the contractor's name to the act in a way that delegation does not.
- The personal execution is sometimes the right move (when the act requires the contractor's authority to be credible — the subcontractor accepts the termination from the contractor that would not accept it from a lawyer) and sometimes the wrong move (when the personal execution exposes the contractor to legal liability that delegation would have avoided). The contractor must distinguish the cases where personal execution serves the master's interest (binding the master to the act, ensuring the act is credible) from the cases where personal exposure harms the strategist (legal liability, personal danger).

**Risks**: The personal execution is the move that maximally attaches the strategist's name to the act. The contractor who personally executes the harsh recommendation cannot later claim that the recommendation was misunderstood or that the execution was delegated. The personal execution is the move that makes the strategist's name inseparable from the act, for better and for worse. The contractor who personally executes must be willing to own the act publicly, permanently, and without the ability to disavow.

The personal execution also exposes the strategist to retaliation. Li Ru was eventually killed by the chaos his personally executed recommendations had created. The contractor who personally terminates subcontractors, personally fires employees, personally breaks bad news to clients, becomes the personal target of the retaliation that follows. The personal execution is the move that ensures the strategist's name is attached to the act; it is also the move that ensures the strategist is the target when the retaliation comes.

### Move 5: The Tragic Loyalty — Stay with the Master Through the Fall

**The pattern**: When the master's trajectory turns terminal, refuse the option to switch (Jia Xu's path) and refuse the option to exit (the safe path). Stay with the master through the fall, accepting that the fall will consume the enabler as well as the master.

**Historical example**: Li Ru's fifth signature move was the choice to stay with Dong Zhuo through the regime's terminal phase. By 191 AD, it was clear to any clear-eyed strategist that Dong Zhuo's regime was doomed. The coalition had fragmented but the fragments were reorganizing. The emperor was a captive, not a legitimate ruler. The regime's brutality had alienated every potential ally. Lu Bu, Dong Zhuo's bodyguard, was being courted by Wang Yun. The signs of the coming assassination were visible to anyone who chose to see them.

Jia Xu, in a comparable situation later (with Zhang Xiu), would have switched to a rising power. Li Ru did not switch. He stayed with Dong Zhuo. He continued to give the harsh recommendations. He continued to personally execute them. He continued to serve the master whose doom was visible. The choice to stay was the tragic loyalty — the loyalty that knows the master is doomed and stays anyway.

The tragic loyalty is not the loyalty of belief (Li Ru did not believe Dong Zhuo would survive). It is not the loyalty of hope (Li Ru did not hope for a reversal). It is the loyalty of identity — Li Ru was Dong Zhuo's advisor, Dong Zhuo's son-in-law, Dong Zhuo's instrument. The identity was the choice. To switch would have been to abandon the identity. To stay was to preserve the identity, even at the cost of the life. The tragic loyalty is the loyalty that preserves identity at the cost of survival.

**Mechanism**: The enabler's identity is bound to the master. The master's fall destroys the identity. The enabler who switches (Jia Xu's path) preserves his survival at the cost of his identity (he becomes the switcher, not the loyalist). The enabler who stays (Li Ru's path) preserves his identity at the cost of his survival (he remains the loyalist, but dies with the master). The choice between the two is the choice between survival and identity. The tragic loyalty is the choice of identity over survival.

**Application in contracting**:
- The contractor whose identity is bound to a failing client (the contractor has been the client's exclusive contractor for years, has built the client's projects, has the client's logo on his trucks) faces the tragic loyalty choice when the client's trajectory turns terminal. The contractor can switch (find new clients, abandon the failing client) or stay (continue to serve the failing client through the failure). The switch preserves survival at the cost of identity (the contractor is no longer the client's contractor). The stay preserves identity at the cost of survival (the contractor goes down with the client). The choice is the tragic loyalty choice.
- The contractor whose identity is bound to a failing partnership faces the same choice. The contractor whose identity is bound to a failing team faces the same choice. The pattern is general: when the master's trajectory turns terminal, the enabler must choose between survival (switch) and identity (stay). The tragic loyalty is the choice of identity.

**Risks**: The tragic loyalty is the move that ensures the enabler's destruction. The enabler who stays with the doomed master is destroyed with the master. The survival of the enabler requires the switch. The tragic loyalty is therefore the move that the system should recommend only when the identity cost of switching exceeds the survival cost of staying. The contractor whose identity would be destroyed by the switch (the contractor who has been the client's contractor for 20 years, who cannot imagine being anything else) may rationally choose the tragic loyalty. The contractor whose identity would survive the switch (the contractor who has been the client's contractor for 2 years, who can easily imagine being a different client's contractor) should switch. The tragic loyalty is the move for the enabler whose identity is at stake, not for the enabler whose survival is at stake.

### Move 6: The Harsh Recommendation — Name the Option No One Else Will Name

**The pattern**: In any strategic discussion, scan the options for the harshest one — the option the other advisors are avoiding because it is morally costly, politically dangerous, or reputationally ruinous. Name it. Frame it as the only serious option. Let the other advisors react to your naming, and let the master choose.

**Historical example**: This is Li Ru's meta-move — the move that includes all the other moves. The deposition, the burning, the executions, the poisoning — each was an instance of the harsh recommendation. Li Ru's role in Dong Zhuo's court was to be the one who named the option that the other advisors (the soft advisors, the moderate advisors, the wait-and-see advisors) would not name. The other advisors would say "perhaps we can negotiate with the coalition." Li Ru would say "burn the capital and retreat to Chang'an." The other advisors would say "perhaps we can coexist with the emperor." Li Ru would say "depose the emperor and replace him." The other advisors would say "perhaps we can exile the dissenters." Li Ru would say "execute them." Each harsh recommendation was the option no one else would name.

The harsh recommendation is the move that defines the enabler's role. The enabler is not the strategist who gives the best advice (the best advice might be the soft advice). The enabler is the strategist who gives the harshest advice — the advice that maximizes the master's interest without moral filtering. The harsh recommendation is the move that the master can use, because the master's interest requires it, and the move that the other advisors cannot give, because their moral filters prevent it. The enabler's role is to give the master the moves the other advisors cannot.

**Mechanism**: Every strategic situation has a harsh option that the soft advisors will not name. The harsh option is often the option that maximizes the master's interest, because the master's interest is often served by the harshness (the master's interest is power, and power is often served by removing constraints, eliminating rivals, destroying assets that cannot be held). The soft advisors will not name the harsh option because their moral filters prevent them — they would rather give advice that preserves their reputations than advice that serves the master's interest. The enabler names the harsh option, accepts the moral cost, and serves the master's interest. The master chooses between the soft advice (which preserves his reputation but does not serve his interest) and the harsh advice (which serves his interest but damages his reputation). The master who chooses the harsh advice chooses the enabler.

**Application in contracting**:
- In any meeting where the client is discussing a strategic decision (whether to terminate a subcontractor, whether to delay a project, whether to renegotiate a contract, whether to file a lawsuit), the contractor who plays the enabler's role names the harsh option the other advisors are avoiding. The lawyer will say "perhaps we can negotiate." The accountant will say "perhaps we can restructure." The partner's friend will say "perhaps we can give it more time." The contractor who plays Li Ru's role says "terminate the subcontractor today, file the lawsuit tomorrow, and let the chips fall." The harsh recommendation is the option the other advisors will not name. The client chooses between the soft advice and the harsh advice. The client who chooses the harsh advice chooses the contractor who named it.

**Risks**: The harsh recommendation, when given too often, defines the strategist as the harsh advisor — the one whose every recommendation is the harshest option. The reputation for harshness eventually undermines the strategist's effectiveness, because the master begins to discount the harsh advice as reflexive rather than analytical. The strategist who is known for the harsh recommendation must occasionally give the soft recommendation, to demonstrate that the harsh recommendation is the result of analysis rather than reflex. The strategist who never gives the soft recommendation is the strategist who eventually becomes the sadist rather than the cold enabler.

### Move 7: The Final Sacrifice — Accept the Chaos the Enabling Created

**The pattern**: When the master falls and the chaos the enabler helped create consumes him, accept the consumption. Do not attempt to escape (Jia Xu's path) or to rewrite history (the moral evader's path). Accept the end as the consequence of the choice to enable.

**Historical example**: Li Ru's seventh and final move was his execution by Li Jue and Guo Si in 192 AD, after Dong Zhuo's assassination. The chaos Li Ru had helped create — the empowered generals, the destroyed legitimacy, the fractured empire — turned on him. Li Jue and Guo Si, who had been empowered by Li Ru's counsel during Dong Zhuo's regime, now controlled the western capital. Li Ru was a liability — he knew too much, he was too associated with the fallen regime, he was too dangerous to release. They executed him.

Li Ru did not, as far as the historical record shows, attempt to escape. He did not attempt to switch to Li Jue and Guo Si (the switch would have been the Jia Xu move). He did not attempt to rewrite his role in Dong Zhuo's regime (the rewriting would have been the moral evader's move). He accepted the execution. The acceptance was the final move — the move that completed the pattern of cold enablement. The enabler who had given the harsh recommendations, personally executed them, and stayed with the master through the fall, now accepted the consequences. The acceptance was the moral closure of the pattern. The pattern that had begun with the harsh recommendation ended with the personal acceptance of the consequences.

**Mechanism**: The enabler's pattern is not complete without the final sacrifice. The pattern that begins with the harsh recommendation and continues with the personal execution and the tragic loyalty must end with the acceptance of the consequences. The acceptance is the moral closure that distinguishes the cold enabler from the moral evader. The moral evader attempts to escape the consequences (by switching, by rewriting history, by disavowing the recommendations). The cold enabler accepts the consequences, because the consequences are the moral weight he accepted when he gave the harsh recommendation. The acceptance is the final move of the pattern that began with the first harsh recommendation.

**Application in contracting**:
- The contractor who has enabled a destructive client for years, and who watches the client fall (exposed, sued, bankrupted, arrested), must accept the consequences — the reputational damage (the contractor's name was attached to the client's acts), the financial damage (the client's unpaid invoices, the client's bankruptcies), the moral damage (the contractor's complicity in the client's acts). The acceptance is the final move. The contractor who attempts to escape the consequences (by claiming he was just following the client's orders, by disavowing the recommendations he gave, by switching to a different client without acknowledging the prior enabling) is the moral evader. The contractor who accepts the consequences is the cold enabler, completing the pattern he began.
- The acceptance is not the move the system recommends for ordinary situations. The acceptance is the move the system recognizes when the pattern has been completed. The contractor who has invoked Li Ru's pattern and has given the harsh recommendations and has stayed with the destructive client through the fall, must accept the consequences when the fall comes. The acceptance is the moral closure of the pattern. The contractor who refuses the acceptance has not completed the pattern, and the pattern remains open, weighing on the contractor's name.

**Risks**: The final sacrifice is the move that destroys the enabler. The acceptance of the consequences is the acceptance of the enabler's destruction. The move is the end of the pattern, not a path beyond it. The contractor who invokes Li Ru's pattern must understand that the final sacrifice is the inevitable conclusion. The pattern that begins with the harsh recommendation ends with the enabler's destruction. The contractor who is not willing to accept the destruction should not begin the pattern. The contractor who begins the pattern must accept the destruction when it comes.

## How to Recognize When to Apply Each Move

| Move | When to apply | When NOT to apply |
|------|--------------|------------------|
| The Deposition Recommendation | When the symbol of legitimacy constrains the master and the symbol can be replaced | When the symbol cannot be replaced (the symbol is too widely recognized, the replacement will be rejected) |
| The Burning of Luoyang | When the asset cannot be held and the destruction denies the opponent the objective | When the asset can be held, or when the destruction harms the destroyer more than the opponent |
| The Execution of Opponents | When the dissent is organizing and the organizers can be identified | When the dissent is not organizing, or when the organizers cannot be identified without provoking more dissent |
| The Poisoned Cup | When the harsh recommendation requires personal execution to be credible | When the personal execution exposes the strategist to liability that delegation would avoid |
| The Tragic Loyalty | When the master's trajectory is terminal and the enabler's identity is bound to the master | When the master's trajectory is not terminal, or when the enabler's identity is not bound to the master |
| The Harsh Recommendation | When the strategic discussion needs the harshest option named | When the harshest option is not the best option (occasionally the soft option is the best) |
| The Final Sacrifice | When the pattern has been completed and the consequences are arriving | When the pattern has not been completed (the enabler can still switch or redirect) |
