# Anti-Pattern Villains — Blind Spots (Cross-Cutting, Shared Across the Cast)

> **Layer**: 07_MINDS/anti_pattern_villains/
> **File**: blind_spots
> **Subject**: The cross-cutting blind spots shared across the anti-pattern characters in Ravages of Time — each a lesson for the user
> **Character class**: COLLECTIVE ANTI-PATTERN — read alongside the individual anti-pattern minds (yuan_shao/blind_spots.md, yuan_shu/blind_spots.md, dong_zhuo/blind_spots.md, lu_bu/blind_spots.md, liu_biao/blind_spots.md, liu_zhang/blind_spots.md), which provide the specific blind spots of each villain. This file extracts the blind spots that recur across multiple villains.

---

## The Premise

A blind spot is not a flaw in the world. It is a flaw in perception — a thing that exists, that matters, that the mind cannot see. The other `blind_spots.md` files in this system describe the limitations of a strong worldview (where Sima Yi's patience fails, where Cao Cao's decisiveness fails, etc.). This file describes something different: the things the anti-pattern villains could not see that produced their failures.

For the anti-pattern villains, the blind spots are not limitations of a strong mind. They are the constitutive features of a failing mind. The villains' minds failed because they had structured themselves to not see certain things. The things they could not see were the things that would have saved them. The blindness and the failure are the same thing.

The six blind spots below are cross-cutting. They are not specific to one villain. They are shared — in different proportions, with different emphases — across the full cast. The same blind spot appears in Yuan Shao and in Liu Biao; the same blind spot appears in Yuan Shu and in the Yellow Turbans; the same blind spot appears in Lu Bu and in Li Jue/Guo Si. The cross-cutting nature is the point: the blind spots are not exotic vulnerabilities of unusual men. The blind spots are the default blind spots of a person in power who has not examined how power affects perception.

Read each blind spot as a lesson — a question to ask yourself. "Am I blind to this?" If yes, the lesson is to find a way to see it. The way to see it is usually through other people — the people you have stopped listening to. The lesson of the anti-pattern villains is that the people you have stopped listening to are the people who would have told you what you needed to hear.

## The 6 Cross-Cutting Blind Spots

### Blind Spot 1: The Inability to See That Advantages Are Becoming Liabilities

**What the villains could not see**: The villains could not see that their advantages — the very things that made them powerful — were also the things that were destroying them. They saw their advantages as assets. They could not see that the same advantages, combined with their character, were liabilities.

Yuan Shao's case is the canonical example. His noble birth (the four generations of five dukes) made him the natural leader of the coalition against Dong Zhuo. It also made him contemptuous of capable men beneath his standing. His vast army made him overconfident at Guandu. His large court of advisors made him paralyzed by their infighting. His wealth made him believe he could buy outcomes that required judgment. His popularity made him afraid to make decisions that would displease anyone. Each advantage, mishandled, became a liability.

Liu Biao's case is the parallel. His reputation as a scholar made him the natural governor of Jing Province (a province of cultured gentry). The same reputation made him contemptuous of the military men (Wen Pin, Huang Zhong, Wei Yan) who could have defended the province. His wealth made him able to host literary gatherings. The same wealth made him believe he could ignore the military threat from Cao Cao. His territory (the strategic middle Yangtze) made him important to every warlord. The same territory made him the target of every warlord.

Dong Zhuo's case is the parallel. His military force (the Liang Province troops) made him able to seize the capital. The same military force made him unable to govern (the troops demanded plunder, and the plunder turned the population against him). His control of the Emperor made him able to issue edicts in the Emperor's name. The same control made him the target of every other warlord (who could not acknowledge his legitimacy and survive).

**The mechanism**: Advantages are not neutral. They shape the person who holds them. A large army breeds overconfidence. A large court breeds factionalism. A prestigious name breeds vanity. Wealth breeds risk-aversion (you have more to lose). Popularity breeds image-optimization (you have an audience to perform for). A military force breeds plunder (the force demands rewards the leader cannot legitimately pay). Control of a symbol breeds the confusion of symbol with substance. The advantages do not stay as they were given. They become what the holder's character makes them. The villains' characters made their advantages into traps.

The blindness was not lack of access to the truth. The villains' best advisors — Tian Feng (for Yuan Shao), Ju Shou (for Yuan Shao), Kuai Liang (for Liu Biao), Li Ru (for Dong Zhuo, in his early career) — told them. The warnings were the truth. The truth was rejected because the truth was unwelcome. The blindness was self-inflicted. The villains chose not to see what their advisors told them.

**The lesson**: Your advantages are not your friend. They are tools. Tools can be used well or badly. The man who treats his advantages as proof of his rightness is the man whose advantages will destroy him. The man who treats his advantages as tools — to be used carefully, to be sharpened, to be put down when they are not needed — is the man whose advantages will serve him. The difference is in how you relate to your advantages. The relation determines whether they save you or kill you.

**Application to your practice**: A contractor with a strong trade, a good network, and growing capital is in danger if he treats these as proof of his rightness. The trade becomes a reason not to learn new skills. The network becomes a reason not to develop new relationships. The capital becomes a reason not to be careful with bids. Each advantage, mishandled, becomes a liability. The contractor must treat his advantages as tools — to be used where they apply, to be supplemented where they don't, to be sharpened continuously, to be put down when the situation requires a different tool. The contractor who treats his advantages as proof of his rightness is the contractor whose advantages will destroy him.

**Counter-mind**: Sima Yi — treat advantages as tools to be concealed and deployed, not as proof of rightness. The patient strategist uses advantages when needed and puts them aside when they would be a liability.

### Blind Spot 2: The Inability to See That Indecision Is Itself a Decision

**What the villains could not see**: The villains could not see that their failure to decide was itself a decision — a decision to let the situation evolve without their intervention. They treated indecision as the absence of decision. The reality was that indecision was a particular decision: the decision to do nothing while the world moved.

He Jin's case is the canonical example. He had the legal authority, the military force, and the political position to remove the eunuch faction. He delayed. He sought further counsel. He deliberated. He treated the delay as the absence of decision. The reality was that the delay was a decision to let the eunuchs act first. The eunuchs, knowing their destruction was planned, assassinated him. The assassination triggered the massacre of the eunuchs and the entry of Dong Zhuo. He Jin's indecision produced the warlord era.

Yuan Shao's case is the parallel. He delayed the decision to attack Cao Cao while his advisors argued. He treated the delay as the absence of decision. The reality was that the delay was a decision to let Cao Cao eliminate his other enemies and entrench at Guandu. The decision Yuan Shao eventually made — to attack at Guandu — was the only decision left, because the better decisions had been closed by the delay. The indecision that felt like preserving options was actually the destruction of options.

Liu Zhang's case is the parallel. He could not decide whether to trust Liu Bei, to resist Liu Bei, or to negotiate with Liu Bei. He treated the indecision as the absence of decision. The reality was that the indecision was a decision to let Liu Bei consolidate his position inside Yi Province. By the time Liu Zhang decided to resist, the resistance was too late.

Liu Biao's case is the parallel. He could not decide whether to fight Cao Cao, to negotiate with Cao Cao, or to surrender to Cao Cao. He treated the indecision as the absence of decision. He died before Cao Cao arrived. His successor (Liu Cong) inherited the indecision and surrendered.

**The mechanism**: The mind reasons that deciding later is better than deciding now, because later the information will be better. The reasoning has some validity in static situations. The reasoning is wrong in dynamic situations — and most situations are dynamic. While the mind waits for better information, the situation changes. The decision that would have been good now is no longer available. The decision that is available later is worse. The wait has not preserved the option; it has narrowed the option set to the worst options.

**The lesson**: Indecision is not the absence of decision. It is a decision to let circumstances decide for you. Circumstances do not decide in your favor. Circumstances decide in favor of the person who is acting — your opponent. The man who waits while his opponent acts gives his opponent the choice of all the options. The man who acts first chooses from the full option set and forces his opponent to respond. The first mover has more options than the waiter; the waiter's options narrow with every day of waiting.

**Application to your practice**: A contractor who delays the decision to invest in a new capability (a new certification, a new piece of equipment, a new hire) — waiting for more information about whether the market will justify the investment — is making the anti-pattern mistake. The information he is waiting for will arrive too late. By the time the market clearly demands the capability, the contractor who invested a year ago has the capability and the contractor who waited does not. The waited-for information was a trap. The information that mattered was the early, ambiguous signal — and that signal required a decision, not a wait. A contractor who delays the decision to address a cash flow crisis — convening meeting after meeting, calling advisor after advisor — is making the anti-pattern mistake. The crisis does not wait. Each week of indecision, the cash position weakens. The late decision is the more expensive decision.

**Counter-mind**: Guo Jia — act on the early signal, accept the uncertainty, adjust as you go. The decision made early with imperfect information beats the decision made late with perfect information, because the late decision is being made into a worse situation.

### Blind Spot 3: The Inability to See That the Symbol Is Not the Substance

**What the villains could not see**: The villains could not see that the symbols they held (the seal, the name, the commission, the credential, the award) were not the substance those symbols described. They treated the symbols as evidence of the substance. The symbols produced no military, political, or economic effect — except on the men who held them, who behaved as if the symbols had given them the substance.

Yuan Shu's case is the canonical example. He held the imperial seal. The seal was the symbol of the mandate of heaven. The mandate of heaven was the substance of imperial authority. Yuan Shu treated the seal as evidence that he had the mandate. The seal produced no soldiers, no grain, no allies. The seal's only effect was on Yuan Shu himself, who behaved as if the seal had made him emperor. The behavior alienated his peers. The peers destroyed him.

Yuan Shao's case is the parallel. He held the Yuan name (the four generations of five dukes). The name was the symbol of strategic legitimacy. The legitimacy was the substance of coalition leadership. Yuan Shao treated the name as evidence of his strategic capability. The name produced no strategy. The name's only effect was on Yuan Shao himself, who believed that the name made his counsel better than his advisors' counsel. The belief produced the Guandu defeat.

Liu Biao's case is the parallel. He held the scholar's reputation. The reputation was the symbol of cultured governance. The substance of cultured governance was the strategic capability to defend the territory. Liu Biao treated the reputation as evidence of his strategic capability. The reputation produced no defense. The reputation's only effect was on Liu Biao himself, who believed that the reputation made him the natural governor of Jing Province. The belief produced the surrender of Jing Province to Cao Cao.

The minor warlords' cases are the parallel. Han Fu held the Ji Province commission. The commission was the symbol of provincial authority. The substance of provincial authority was the military capability to hold the province. Han Fu treated the commission as evidence of his capability. The commission produced no military capability. Han Fu, fearing Yuan Shao, surrendered the province to Yuan Shao. The commission's only effect was on Han Fu himself, who believed the commission entitled him to a province he could not hold.

**The mechanism**: The mind reasons that the symbol is the substance. The reasoning is wrong. The symbol is a claim about the substance. The claim can be true (the substance exists behind the symbol) or false (the substance does not exist). The mind that treats the symbol as the substance stops building the substance. The building was the only path to making the symbol true. The mind that stops building loses both.

**The lesson**: The symbol is a checkpoint, not a destination. The certification confirms that you have built enough to receive the certification. The work of building continues. The symbol that is treated as the destination is the symbol that destroys the man who treats it that way. The symbol that is treated as a checkpoint is the symbol that supports the man who treats it that way.

**Application to your practice**: A contractor who obtains a major certification (a class-A contractor license, an ISO certification, an award from a trade association) and concludes that the certification proves he is now a different class of contractor — and who stops building the crews, the systems, the cash reserves, and the client relationships that the certification describes — is making Yuan Shu's mistake. The certification is the seal. The certification does not give him the projects, the crews, the cash flow, or the systems. The certification confirms (to himself) what he wanted to believe. It does not confirm anything to his clients, his competitors, or his suppliers. They will evaluate him on what he can do, not on what his certificate says he is authorized to do. The contractor who rests on the credential will discover, as Yuan Shu discovered, that the credential is a piece of paper and that his competitors are still evaluating his substance.

**Counter-mind**: Cao Cao — substance over symbol. Build the capability; let the symbol follow. The symbol that follows is grounded. The symbol that leads is ungrounded.

### Blind Spot 4: The Inability to See That the Counsel Rejected Was the Counsel That Would Have Saved Them

**What the villains could not see**: The villains could not see, in the moment of rejecting counsel, that the counsel was correct. They evaluated counsel by how it felt — whether it confirmed their self-image, whether it pleased their court, whether it advanced their factional favorites. They could not evaluate counsel by its likely correctness, because evaluating by likely correctness requires humility about one's own judgment, and humility was the thing they could not afford.

Yuan Shao's case is the canonical example. Tian Feng argued against the Guandu campaign. Ju Shou argued against the late campaign. The counsel was correct. The counsel was rejected because it contradicted Yuan Shao's self-image (he believed the campaign would be his crowning victory), because it advanced the caution faction over the war faction (and Yuan Shao had favored the war faction for years), and because accepting the counsel would have required Yuan Shao to publicly acknowledge that he had been wrong. The acknowledgment was the thing Yuan Shao could not afford. The counsel that was rejected was the counsel that would have saved him.

Liu Biao's case is the parallel. Kuai Liang and other advisors warned him to prepare the military defense of Jing Province. The counsel was correct. The counsel was rejected because it contradicted Liu Biao's self-image as a scholar-governor (who governed through culture, not force), because it advanced the military faction over the scholar faction, and because accepting the counsel would have required Liu Biao to publicly acknowledge that the cultured governance had not produced the defense. The acknowledgment was the thing Liu Biao could not afford. The counsel that was rejected was the counsel that would have saved him.

Dong Zhuo's case is the parallel (in his late years). Li Ru, his senior advisor, warned him against the excesses that were turning the court against him. The counsel was correct. The counsel was rejected because it contradicted Dong Zhuo's self-image as the undisputed master of the court, because it advanced the caution faction over the tyranny faction, and because accepting the counsel would have required Dong Zhuo to publicly acknowledge that the tyranny was producing the resistance. The acknowledgment was the thing Dong Zhuo could not afford. The counsel that was rejected was the counsel that would have saved him (or at least delayed his destruction).

Liu Zhang's case is the parallel. Wang Lei (王累) and others warned him against inviting Liu Bei into Yi Province. The counsel was correct. The counsel was rejected because it contradicted Liu Zhang's self-image as a generous host, because it advanced the caution faction over the invitation faction, and because accepting the counsel would have required Liu Zhang to publicly acknowledge that his own subordinates (Fa Zheng, Meng Da) were plotting against him. The acknowledgment was the thing Liu Zhang could not afford. The counsel that was rejected was the counsel that would have saved him.

**The mechanism**: The mind evaluates counsel through two filters: a content filter (is the counsel likely correct?) and an emotional filter (does the counsel feel welcome?). In a healthy mind, the content filter is primary and the emotional filter is secondary. In the villains' minds, the emotional filter was primary. Counsel that felt welcome was evaluated as likely correct; counsel that felt unwelcome was evaluated as likely incorrect. The evaluation followed the emotion, not the other way around.

The consequence is that the counsel the villains rejected was, by definition, the counsel that felt unwelcome. The counsel that felt unwelcome was the counsel that contradicted their self-image. The counsel that contradicted their self-image was the counsel that pointed to errors they were about to make. The counsel that pointed to errors they were about to make was the counsel that would have saved them. The emotional filter systematically rejected the counsel that would have saved them, and systematically accepted the counsel that would destroy them. The destruction was built into the filter.

**The lesson**: The counsel that feels most unwelcome is the counsel that is most likely correct — because the counsel that feels unwelcome is the counsel that contradicts you, and the counsel that contradicts you is the counsel that sees what you do not. The counsel that feels most welcome is the counsel that is most dangerous — because it confirms you, and confirmation is what you do not need. You need contradiction. You need the counselor who will tell you that you are wrong. The counselor who tells you that you are wrong is the counselor who is doing the work you hired him to do. The counselor who tells you that you are right is the counselor who is doing the work of protecting his position.

**Application to your practice**: A contractor who, in the course of a project, receives a warning from a subcontractor that the design will not work — and rejects the warning because the subcontractor's tone was insubordinate, or because the subcontractor's warning contradicts the contractor's earlier decision, or because the warning feels like an attack — is making the anti-pattern mistake. The warning is the counsel that would have saved him. The rejection of the warning is the destruction of his own project. The contractor must learn to evaluate counsel by its content, not by its tone — and to suspect, in himself, the tendency to accept the flattering counsel and reject the correcting counsel.

**Counter-mind**: Liu Bei — build relationships where hard counsel is welcome. The relationship where the counselor can tell you that you are wrong without fear of punishment is the relationship that produces the counsel that saves you. The relationship takes years to build and is the most valuable asset a leader can have.

### Blind Spot 5: The Inability to See That Reputation Compounds (and That Betrayal Compounds Negatively)

**What the villains could not see**: The villains could not see that reputation is not a snapshot. Reputation is a compounding asset — or a compounding liability. Each action adds to the reputation. Each betrayal subtracts from the reputation. The compounding is slow and invisible. The compounding is, eventually, decisive.

Lu Bu's case is the canonical example. He betrayed Ding Yuan (in 189 AD, to join Dong Zhuo). He betrayed Dong Zhuo (in 192 AD, manipulated by Wang Yun). He plundered Yuan Shao's territory (after being given refuge by Yuan Shao in 192-193 AD). He seized Xiapi from Liu Bei (in 196 AD, while Liu Bei was away fighting Yuan Shu). Each betrayal produced a short-term gain. Each betrayal subtracted from his reputation. The reputation was, by 199 AD, that Lu Bu could not be trusted. Cao Cao, considering whether to employ him at Xiapi, was reminded by Liu Bei of the pattern. Cao Cao executed him. The reputation that had compounded over a decade of betrayals produced the execution. The execution was the cumulative consequence of the betrayals, not the consequence of any single betrayal.

Li Jue and Guo Si's case is the parallel. They were Dong Zhuo's subordinates. They were refused amnesty by Wang Yun. They gathered the remnants of Dong Zhuo's army and seized Chang'an. They then betrayed their alliance and fought each other for control of the puppet Emperor. The betrayal of the alliance subtracted from their reputation with each other. The fight destroyed the Guanzhong region. The reputation of the Liang Province troops (as plunderers who could not be trusted) compounded negatively. Eventually, the troops were defeated by Cao Cao, who rescued the Emperor in 196 AD. The negative compounding of the reputation produced the defeat.

The minor warlords' cases are the parallel. Wei Xu, Hou Cheng, and Song Xian (Lu Bu's subordinates) betrayed Lu Bu to Cao Cao at Xiapi. The betrayal produced a short-term reward (Cao Cao's favor). The betrayal subtracted from their reputation. The reputation of men who would betray their patron followed them. The men who would betray their patron were not employed in positions of trust. The negative compounding of the reputation produced the marginalization.

**The mechanism**: The mind reasons that each action is local — that the betrayal of today does not affect the alliances of tomorrow. The reasoning is wrong. Each action is observed by the network. The network updates its evaluation of the actor. The updated evaluation affects the next alliance the actor can form. The cumulative evaluation is the reputation. The reputation compounds — positively (each kept alliance makes the next alliance easier to form) or negatively (each betrayal makes the next alliance harder to form). The compounding is slow. The compounding is decisive. The actor who does not see the compounding treats each betrayal as local. The actor who sees the compounding treats each betrayal as a long-term liability that exceeds the short-term gain.

**The lesson**: Reputation is a compounding asset. Each kept alliance adds to the reputation. Each betrayal subtracts from the reputation. The compounding is slow and invisible. The compounding is, eventually, decisive. The actor who wishes to maintain the reputation must treat each alliance as a long-term investment, not as a short-term cost to be optimized. The betrayal that saves 5% today may cost 50% over the next decade, as the network that would have supported the actor narrows.

**Application to your practice**: A contractor who, in his network of subcontractors, suppliers, and clients, switches sides whenever the switch serves him — who drops a subcontractor for a slightly cheaper one, drops a supplier for a slightly faster one, drops a client for a slightly larger one, without loyalty and without warning — is making the anti-pattern mistake. The switches produce short-term savings. The switches produce a long-term reputation: this contractor cannot be relied on. The subcontractors who would have given him priority work stop giving him priority. The suppliers who would have extended credit stop extending credit. The clients who would have referred him stop referring him. The contractor finds his network narrowing year by year, and he does not understand why — because each switch, in isolation, looked like a rational cost-saving. The pattern of switches, in aggregate, destroyed the network he was optimizing.

**Counter-mind**: Liu Bei — loyalty as the foundation. The alliance is the asset, not the cost to be optimized. The alliance that lasts decades produces more than the alliance that lasts months.

### Blind Spot 6: The Inability to See That the Removal of the Tyrant Without Successor Structure Produces Civil War

**What the villains could not see**: The villains could not see that the removal of the tyrant, without a prepared successor structure, did not produce the order the tyrant had prevented. The removal produced a vacuum. The vacuum was filled by the tyrant's subordinates, who fought each other. The fight destroyed the territory.

Wang Yun's case is the canonical example. He orchestrated the assassination of Dong Zhuo in 192 AD. He refused to amnesty Dong Zhuo's subordinates (Li Jue, Guo Si, Fan Chou, Zhang Ji). The four subordinates, facing punishment, gathered the remnants of Dong Zhuo's army and marched on Chang'an. They captured the city. They captured the Emperor. They then fought each other for control of the puppet Emperor. The fight destroyed the Guanzhong region. The famine (cannibalism in their territories) ended the Han's last claim to central authority. Wang Yun was executed with his family. The liberation he had engineered was the start of the warlord era.

Yuan Shao's case is the parallel. He died in 202 AD without naming an heir. The ambiguity produced a vacuum. The vacuum was filled by his sons (Yuan Tan, Yuan Xi, Yuan Shang) and their factions. The factions fought. The fight destroyed the Yuan empire. Cao Cao absorbed the remains within a few years. The civil war among the sons did Cao Cao's work for him.

Liu Biao's case is the parallel. He died in 208 AD without naming a clear heir. The ambiguity produced a vacuum. The vacuum was filled by the Cai family faction (supporting Liu Cong) and the Liu Qi faction. The Cai faction won the court and surrendered Jing Province to Cao Cao without a fight. The civil war was avoided only by surrender. The surrender was the absorption of Jing Province into Cao Cao's empire.

The Yellow Turban aftermath is the parallel. The uprising was suppressed in 184 AD. The suppression produced a vacuum in the regional command structure. The vacuum was filled by the regional governors (Dong Zhuo, Sun Jian, Cao Cao, Liu Bei) who had been given military commands to suppress the uprising. The governors retained the commands after the uprising was suppressed. The governors became warlords. The warlords fought each other. The civil war after the suppression was the warlord era.

**The mechanism**: The mind reasons that the removal of the tyrant is the liberation, and that the liberation will produce the order the tyrant prevented. The reasoning is wrong. The tyrant, however tyrannically, was holding a structure together. The structure was the tyrant's structure (built for the tyrant's benefit, not for the territory's benefit). The removal of the tyrant removes the structure. The structure is the only order that existed. The removal produces a vacuum. The vacuum is filled by the tyrant's subordinates, who fight each other because the subordinates have no other mechanism for resolving the succession. The fight destroys the territory. The liberation that felt like the start of a better order was the start of a worse chaos.

**The lesson**: The removal of the tyrant without a prepared successor structure is the start of the civil war. The successor structure must be built before the removal. The successor structure is the documented processes, the reassigned client relationships, the new authority structure, the new cash flow arrangements. The removal without the preparation is the civil war. The removal with the preparation is the start of the next phase.

**Application to your practice**: A contractor who is in a partnership (with a brother, a cousin, an investor, a senior employee) and who is contemplating the removal of the partner — without a clear succession plan, without a documented transition, without a prepared structure for the post-removal period — is making the anti-pattern mistake. The removal of the partner (whether by firing, by buy-out, or by legal action) will produce a vacuum. The vacuum will be filled by the partner's clients, the partner's subordinates, the partner's suppliers — each of whom has an interest in the post-removal order and no mechanism for resolving their competing interests. The fight over the remains will destroy the contractor's business. The contractor must prepare the successor order BEFORE removing the partner: the documented processes, the reassigned client relationships, the new authority structure, the new cash flow arrangements. The removal without the preparation is the start of the civil war. The removal with the preparation is the start of the next phase.

**Counter-mind**: Cao Cao — institutional building. The structure that survives the leader is the leader's true legacy. The structure must be built before the removal. The removal without the structure is the civil war.

## How to Recognize When You Are Anti-Pattern-Blind

The system should warn the user when these blind spots are active. The warnings are:

- **"You're treating your advantages as proof of your rightness."** → The advantages are tools, not vindication. Apply Sima Yi's mind.
- **"You're treating indecision as the absence of decision."** → Indecision is a decision to let circumstances choose. Apply Guo Jia's mind.
- **"You're treating the symbol as the substance."** → The symbol is a checkpoint, not a destination. Apply Cao Cao's mind.
- **"You're rejecting counsel that feels unwelcome."** → The unwelcome counsel is the counsel that would save you. Apply Liu Bei's mind.
- **"You're treating each alliance as a short-term cost to be optimized."** → The alliance is a compounding asset. Apply Liu Bei's mind.
- **"You're contemplating the removal of a partner without preparing the successor structure."** → The removal without the preparation is the civil war. Apply Cao Cao's mind.

Each of these warnings is the system recognizing an anti-pattern in the user's thinking. The recognition is the first step to correction. The correction is to switch to the counter-mind. The switch is the work of a lifetime — the anti-patterns are seductive because they are easy, and the counter-minds require effort. The effort is the price of not becoming one of the men in this catalog.

## The Discipline

The anti-pattern blind spots are the most dangerous blind spots in this system because they are the easiest to fall into. The other minds' blind spots are limitations of strength (Sima Yi's patience fails when speed is required; Cao Cao's decisiveness fails when patience is required). The anti-pattern blind spots are limitations of weakness — and weakness is the default condition of a successful person who has not examined his success.

The discipline is to audit yourself periodically — monthly, at minimum, and at major decision points — against the six blind spots. The audit is simple: ask, for each blind spot, "am I doing this now?" If the honest answer is yes, the discipline is to act on the counter-mind immediately, before the blind spot compounds into a disaster.

The audit is uncomfortable. The blind spots are blind spots precisely because they are comfortable to live with. The audit forces you to see what you have been choosing not to see. The seeing is the first step. The acting on the seeing is the work.

The contractor who audits himself against the anti-pattern blind spots every month, and acts on what he finds, will not become one of the men in this catalog. The contractor who does not audit himself will become one of them — slowly, comfortably, and without noticing until it is too late.
