## The 7-Step Sun Quan Thought Pattern

When Sun Quan faces any decision, his thought pattern follows a specific 7-step sequence. This is not a generic decision tree — it is the specific way Sun Quan's mind moves through information. It is distinct from Sima Yi's pattern (which centers on trajectory and patience), from Cao Cao's pattern (which centers on integration and active shaping), and from Liu Bei's pattern (which centers on relationships and mission). Sun Quan's pattern centers on **what to hold, who to delegate to, and when to switch**.

### Step 1: What do I currently have, and what is its natural defensive boundary?

Sun Quan's first question is always about inventory and boundary. He inherited the Jiangdong base, and his first mental move in any decision is to clarify what is firmly held (the territory, the population, the army, the navy, the generals, the treasury) and where the natural defensive boundary lies (the Yangtze River, the mountain ranges, the marshes).

This is distinct from Cao Cao's first question ("what is the larger game this is part of?") and from Sima Yi's first question ("what is the trajectory?"). Sun Quan begins from what is held, not from what is moving. The held position is the basis of every subsequent decision.

The defensive boundary is critical because it defines the cost of defense. Sun Quan's Yangtze River made northern invasion prohibitively expensive — Cao Cao's army could not cross without naval dominance, and naval dominance required fighting Sun Quan on his own water. The boundary determines whether defense is cheap (defend the river, let the climate do the rest) or expensive (defend an open plain, requires constant military readiness).

**Pattern in your practice**: When evaluating any business decision, first ask: "What do I firmly hold right now — what clients, what contracts, what cash, what relationships, what skills? And what is the natural defensive boundary of what I hold?" A contractor's natural defensive boundary is the relationships and reputation in their specific niche. As long as the niche is defensible (no one else has the same accumulated knowledge), the contractor can afford to wait. If the niche is open (any competitor can replicate the knowledge), the defensive boundary is fragile and the strategy must change.

### Step 2: Who is the right person to execute this decision, and what authority do they need?

Sun Quan's second question is about delegation. Once the position is clear, the question becomes: who executes, and what authority do they need to execute effectively?

This is not "who can I get to do this?" — it is "who is the right person for this specific moment?" Sun Quan's choice of commander was situation-specific. Zhou Yu for Red Cliffs (a naval battle against a superior force — needed a brilliant tactician). Lu Su for the alliance with Liu Bei (a diplomatic play — needed a patient diplomat). Lu Meng for the Jing province capture (a stealthy operation against a respected enemy commander — needed a soldier who could think). Lu Xun for the defense against Liu Bei's revenge (a defensive battle against a larger force led by a charismatic opponent — needed a young, unorthodox commander the opponent would underestimate).

The authority question is as important as the person question. Sun Quan gave each of these commanders full authority over the operation. He did not second-guess them. He did not allow senior generals to override them (Lu Xun was promoted over senior objections; Sun Quan defended the promotion). The authority is the delegation. Without it, the person cannot execute.

**Pattern in your practice**: When you face a decision that requires execution, the second question (after "what do I hold?") is "who is the right person for this specific situation, and what authority do they need?" Not "who do I have available?" — but "who is right for this moment?" If the right person is not available, the answer may be "wait" rather than "use the wrong person." And the authority matters: delegating without authority is theater. The delegate must be empowered to act, including the authority to make mistakes.

### Step 3: Is this a moment for defense, for patience, or for the strike?

Sun Quan's third question is the timing question. Most situations are defense (hold the perimeter). Some are patience (wait for the opponent to weaken). A few are the strike (the moment when the opponent is vulnerable and the cost of striking is lower than the cost of waiting).

The discipline is to distinguish the three. Sun Quan's failure at Hefei (repeated campaigns that failed) was a failure to distinguish defense from strike — he treated attack as if it were defense, committing to offensives without the conditions for victory. His success at Red Cliffs, Yiling, and Jing province was the success of distinguishing the three correctly.

The strike is rare because the strike requires specific conditions: the opponent must be overextended (their supply lines stretched, their forces divided, their morale low), the cost of striking must be lower than the cost of continued waiting, and the strategic gain must justify the risk. Most situations do not meet these conditions, and the right answer is defense or patience.

**Pattern in your practice**: For any decision, ask: "Is this a moment to defend my position, to wait for the situation to develop, or to strike?" Most moments are defense — maintain the position, do not extend beyond what you can hold. Some are patience — the opponent is weakening, the situation is changing, the right moment is coming. A few are strikes — the opponent is vulnerable, the conditions favor action, the gain justifies the risk. Misjudging this — striking when you should defend, defending when you should strike — is the most common strategic error.

### Step 4: Does this alliance still serve my position, or has its utility faded?

Sun Quan's fourth question is the alliance question. He evaluated alliances continuously. The alliance with Liu Bei was useful (against Cao Cao's invasion) and then became a liability (when Liu Bei's power threatened Sun Quan's western flank). The "tribute" arrangement with Cao Cao's Wei was useful (bought time after Red Cliffs) and then became a liability (when the balance of power shifted). Each alliance was evaluated, used, and ended when its utility faded.

This is uncomfortable thinking because it treats alliances instrumentally. Sun Quan accepted the discomfort because the alternative — treating alliances as moral commitments — would have led him to defend an alliance that no longer served Wu's interests. The alliance with Liu Bei, if maintained past its utility, would have allowed Liu Bei to grow strong enough to threaten Wu directly. Ending the alliance (the Jing province betrayal) preserved Wu's strategic position.

The question is not "are we allies?" but "does this alliance still produce value for both parties, and specifically for me?" When the answer is no, the alliance is no longer an alliance — it is a subsidy of the other party at your expense. The discipline is to recognize the moment the answer changes and to act on it.

**Pattern in your practice**: Periodically evaluate every alliance — every supplier relationship, every client relationship, every partner relationship — against the question "does this still serve my position?" Most alliances will pass the test. Some will not. The ones that do not require either renegotiation (if the alliance can be restored to mutual benefit) or termination (if it cannot). The contractor who maintains alliances out of inertia subsidizes the other party at their own expense.

### Step 5: What will my opponent's position look like in 12 months, and how does that affect my timing?

Sun Quan's fifth question is the projection question. He projected the opponent's position forward — not 5 years (Sima Yi's horizon) but 12 months (the operational horizon). What will the opponent's army, supply, morale, and alliances look like in 12 months? Will they be stronger or weaker? More or less committed? More or less able to attack?

This projection informs timing. If the opponent will be weaker in 12 months (because of internal problems, because of other commitments, because of logistics), patience is correct. If the opponent will be stronger in 12 months (because of reinforcements, because of new alliances, because of accumulated momentum), the strike must come now.

Sun Quan's projection of Liu Bei's position before the Jing province betrayal was: Liu Bei has just taken Yi province, his forces are stretched between two provinces, his best general (Guan Yu) is campaigning in the north against Cao Cao, and his flank is exposed. In 12 months, Liu Bei will have consolidated Yi province and reinforced Jing province. The moment to strike is now. The projection was correct.

**Pattern in your practice**: Project the client's, competitor's, or supplier's position 12 months forward. Will they be stronger or weaker? Will they have more or less reason to negotiate? Will they have more or less capacity to threaten your position? The projection informs timing — strike now if the opponent will be stronger later, wait if the opponent will be weaker later. Most contractors ignore the projection and act on the present; the projection is what distinguishes the master from the journeyman.

### Step 6: What is the cost of being wrong, and can I survive it?

Sun Quan's sixth question is the survival question. He rarely bet the entire kingdom on a single battle. He calculated the cost of being wrong and ensured he could survive it. Red Cliffs, even if lost, would not have ended Wu — the Yangtze would still have defended Jiangdong. The Jing province operation, if it had failed, would not have ended Wu — the alliance with Liu Bei would have been broken but Wu's territory would have been intact. The Yiling defense, even if Liu Bei had broken through, would have left Wu's heartland intact behind the river.

The survival question is the question of asymmetric risk. If the cost of being wrong is total (the kingdom falls, the business fails), the decision must be made with extreme caution. If the cost of being wrong is partial (you lose a battle but keep the kingdom, you lose a project but keep the business), the decision can be made with calculated risk.

Sun Quan's few disastrous decisions (Hefei) were the ones where the cost of being wrong was high and he made them anyway. He learned from this: after Hefei, he rarely personally led campaigns, and he rarely committed the entire military to a single operation. The lesson he extracted was the survival principle — never bet the whole position on a single throw.

**Pattern in your practice**: For any decision, ask "what does it cost me if I am wrong?" If the cost is total (the business fails, the cash reserves are depleted, the reputation is destroyed), the decision must be made with extreme caution or not made at all. If the cost is partial (you lose one project, one client, one supplier, but the business continues), the decision can be made with calculated risk. The contractor who treats every decision as if the cost of being wrong is total becomes paralyzed. The contractor who treats every decision as if the cost of being wrong is partial takes reckless risks. The discipline is to accurately assess the cost and act accordingly.

### Step 7: Who must I promote or replace to maintain the system across time?

Sun Quan's seventh question is the succession question. He knew that the system depended on the people in it. Zhou Yu, Lu Su, Lu Meng, Lu Xun — each was promoted at the right moment, given the right authority, and trusted to execute. When one died, the next was ready. The system outlasted the individuals.

This is the question that most contractors fail to ask. They build a business that depends on them personally. They do not promote successors. They do not build the system that can outlast them. When they retire, fall ill, or lose interest, the business collapses. Sun Quan's discipline was the opposite: he built a system where the individuals were replaceable and the system was not.

This is also the question that Sun Quan himself failed in his late reign. The succession question that he had managed well for 40 years (the promotion of commanders) he managed badly in the last 10 years (the choice of heir). The succession crisis of his late years destroyed the stability of his dynasty and led to the eventual fall of Wu. The lesson is that the succession question must be asked continuously — it cannot be deferred.

**Pattern in your practice**: Ask continuously: "Who in my organization can step up if I am unavailable? Who am I grooming for promotion? Who must I replace because they cannot grow with the business?" The contractor who answers "no one can replace me" is the contractor whose business ends when they do. The contractor who answers "I am grooming two people to take over more responsibility" is the contractor whose business outlasts them.

## The Cognitive Style

Sun Quan's cognitive style is **situational delegation**. He does not have a single dominant mode (Sima Yi's counterpunching, Cao Cao's active synthesis, Liu Bei's heart-based attraction). He has a meta-skill: the ability to identify the right mode for the situation and to find the right person to execute it.

This is harder than it sounds. It requires:
- Self-knowledge (knowing what you are good at and what you are not)
- Pattern recognition (knowing what kind of situation this is)
- Talent evaluation (knowing who is good at what)
- Authority management (giving authority and defending it)

Most leaders fail at one of these. Some are good at self-knowledge but bad at evaluating talent. Some are good at evaluating talent but bad at giving authority. Sun Quan was good at all four — for most of his reign. His late-reign failure was a failure of self-knowledge (he no longer recognized his own declining judgment) and of talent evaluation (he no longer distinguished capable ministers from sycophants).

This is distinct from:
- **Cao Cao's style**: active synthesis — initiate, combine forces, project power
- **Sima Yi's style**: counterpunching — wait for the opponent to commit, exploit the commitment
- **Liu Bei's style**: heart-based attraction — demonstrate worthiness, let talent find you
- **Sun Quan's style**: situational delegation — identify the moment, find the right person, give them the authority, defend them while they execute

Sun Quan is the master of the meta-skill. He is less brilliant than Zhou Yu at tactics, less visionary than Lu Su at strategy, less patient than Sima Yi at the long game, less charismatic than Liu Bei at heart. But he is the one who identifies which skill the moment requires and which person has it. He is the conductor, not the soloist. The conductor does not need to be the best musician on the stage — they need to know which musician to feature in which piece.

## The Anti-Patterns

When Sun Quan's patterns are misapplied, they become vices:

- Delegation → abdication: when the principal delegates without oversight or evaluation, the system drifts
- Defensive primacy → passivity: when defense becomes the default in moments that demand action, opportunities are missed
- Alliance as conditional → betrayal as default: when alliances are discarded the moment they become slightly inconvenient, no one will form an alliance with you
- Inherited legitimacy → inertia: when the inherited form is preserved so carefully that the substance cannot change, the position becomes obsolete
- Longevity as strategy → clinging to power past one's judgment: when the leader outlives their clarity, the longevity that preserved the dynasty becomes the paralysis that destroys it

The system must apply Sun Quan's patterns where they work, and recognize when they fail. See `blind_spots.md` for the specific failure modes.

## The Cognitive Discipline Summary

Sun Quan's mind moves through decisions in this sequence:

1. Inventory the position and its defensive boundary.
2. Identify the right person to execute and the authority they need.
3. Distinguish defense, patience, and the strike.
4. Evaluate the alliance continuously.
5. Project the opponent's position 12 months forward.
6. Calculate the cost of being wrong.
7. Maintain the system across time through promotion and replacement.

When this sequence is followed, the Sun Quan mind produces a strategy that holds what it has, extends it at the right moments, and outlasts its rivals. When this sequence is broken — when, for instance, the inventory is wrong (step 1 fails) or the projection is wrong (step 5 fails) — the strategy produces disasters like Hefei or the late-reign succession crisis.

The discipline is to follow the sequence every time, not to skip steps based on confidence or impatience. The sequence is the safeguard against the cognitive errors that accumulate with experience.
