## The 7-Step Guan Yu Thought Pattern

When Guan Yu faces any decision, his thought pattern follows a specific 7-step sequence. This is not a generic decision tree — it is the specific way Guan Yu's mind moves through information. The pattern is not how he should think; it is how he did think, and how the contractor must think when invoking the Guan Yu mind.

### Step 1: What does my lord require, and what does my honor permit?

Guan Yu's first question in any situation is not "what is best for me" and not even "what is best for the situation." His first question is "what does my lord (Liu Bei) require of me, and what does my honor permit me to do?"

This is the loyalty-first frame. It is not subservience — Liu Bei was the sworn brother, the equal in the Peach Garden oath, not the master. But the brotherhood had a hierarchy of authority: Liu Bei was the political leader, Guan Yu was the military executor. The frame says: before considering the situation, consider the mission you are part of. The mission is not yours; it is your lord's. The honor is yours; you carry it into the mission.

The two questions — what the lord requires and what honor permits — can conflict. Liu Bei might require an action that honor forbids (e.g., betrayal of an ally). Honor might permit an action that Liu Bei forbids (e.g., releasing Cao Cao at Huarong, which Liu Bei would not have ordered but which honor required). When they conflict, Guan Yu resolves the conflict by choosing honor — and accepting the consequence. He released Cao Cao knowing he would face Zhuge Liang's wrath. He chose honor over the lord's strategic plan. This is the deepest expression of the pattern: honor is the constraint that even loyalty cannot override.

**Pattern in your practice**: When evaluating a business decision, the first frame is: what is my mission to my partner / client / family, and what does my honor permit me to do in service of that mission? If the partner's mission requires me to do something dishonorable (cheat a third party, deceive a regulator, abandon a long-term subcontractor), the answer is no — honor overrides the mission. If the mission requires something I can do honorably, the answer is yes. The first question is not "what is best" but "what is permitted and required."

### Step 2: Is there a debt of honor in this situation, and to whom?

Guan Yu's second question is about debts of honor. Every relationship creates debts. Some are financial (pay this invoice). Some are strategic (do this favor). Some are honor-debts (this person saved me, protected me, gave me what I could not have earned). Honor-debts are paid in action, not in coin, and they are paid even when the payment costs more than the original gift.

At Huarong Trail, the question was: do I owe Cao Cao a debt of honor? The answer was yes — Cao Cao had treated Guan Yu with extraordinary courtesy when Guan Yu was a displaced officer seeking his sworn brother. Cao Cao had given him Red Hare, titles, banquets, the protection of his family, the chance to fight (and prove himself) at a moment when Guan Yu needed to prove himself. The debt was real. The payment was the release.

The question of debts is not abstract. It is concrete. To whom do I owe what, and what does the debt require? Some debts are to be paid now (the debt to a parent who is aging). Some are to be paid when called (the debt to a mentor who may one day ask for help). Some are to be paid forward (the debt to a teacher who is gone, paid by teaching the next generation). The discipline is to know the debts, to know who holds them, and to know what they require.

**Pattern in your practice**: When evaluating a decision, ask: "Do I owe a debt of honor in this situation? To whom? What does it require?" A client who gave you your first major contract when no one else would, on terms that favored you, holds a debt of honor. The debt is paid by giving that client preferential treatment in future bids — not free work, but priority, attention, and the willingness to take a thinner margin when the client needs it. A subcontractor who worked through a cashflow crisis with you, accepting delayed payment to keep the project going, holds a debt of honor. The debt is paid by giving that subcontractor the first call when a new project comes. The discipline is to know the debts, and to pay them — even when the payment costs.

### Step 3: Can this be settled in single combat — and should it be?

Guan Yu's third question is the warrior's question: can the situation be resolved by direct confrontation, and should it be? The warrior prefers directness. He prefers the clean test of force (in business: the clean test of competence, quality, price). He prefers the open battle to the political maneuver. He prefers the duel to the ambush.

When Cao Cao's generals were sent to fight Guan Yu's army, Guan Yu did not maneuver. He rode out and challenged the opposing general to single combat. When Yan Liang was slaughtering Cao Cao's generals, no one in Cao Cao's army could stop him. Cao Cao sent for Guan Yu. Guan Yu rode across the field and struck Yan Liang down in one move. The situation was resolved by the application of force at the point of decision.

This is the warrior's preference, but it is not always right. Single combat resolves some situations (the general is dead, his army disperses). It does not resolve others (the general is dead, his successor continues the war; the army is intact and merely demoralized; the political dimension was the real issue and the general was a symptom). The discipline is to know when single combat is the answer and when it is not.

**Pattern in your practice**: When facing a difficult situation with a client, competitor, or subcontractor, ask: "Can this be settled by a direct, open conversation — and should it be?" Most business conflicts can be settled by direct conversation if both parties are willing. The direct conversation is the contractor's single combat. It requires the contractor to ride out, state the issue clearly, and accept the outcome. Some clients will respond to this. Others will not — they prefer the political maneuver, the indirect channel, the back-channel negotiation. With the latter, the warrior's approach fails. The contractor must know which kind of client he is facing before applying single combat. The wrong approach applied to the wrong client loses the client.

### Step 4: Does this require strategy or force — and what is the difference?

Guan Yu's fourth question is about the choice of instrument. Force is the direct application of capability (military force, in Guan Yu's world; quality, price, references, in the contractor's). Strategy is the indirect application of capability — maneuver, deception, alliance, timing, position. Force is visible; strategy is often invisible. Force is fast; strategy is slow. Force tests capability; strategy tests patience.

Guan Yu was a master of force and an amateur of strategy. He could win any battle fought on the battlefield's terms. He lost the war he did not realize he was fighting — the political war for Jing province, in which his enemies used strategy while he used force. The flooding of Fan castle was force (brilliantly applied) — a tactical stroke that captured Yu Jin and killed Pang De. The loss of Jing province was strategy (brilliantly applied by Lu Meng) — a political maneuver that captured Guan Yu's base while his army was at Fan castle.

The discipline is to know which instrument the situation requires. Some situations call for force (a competitor is undercutting your prices and you must respond with quality or lose the bid). Others call for strategy (a competitor is building a relationship with your long-term client, and you must build a deeper relationship, not a louder pitch). The wrong instrument applied to the wrong situation loses. Force applied to a strategic situation makes noise and loses the war. Strategy applied to a force situation wastes time and loses the battle.

**Pattern in your practice**: When facing a situation, ask: "Is this a force situation or a strategy situation?" A bid you must win on quality and price is a force situation — apply your best capability openly. A long-term relationship being eroded by a competitor is a strategy situation — apply patient maneuver, deeper engagement, and indirect defense. The two are not interchangeable. The contractor who tries to win a strategy situation with force looks desperate. The contractor who tries to win a force situation with strategy looks evasive. The discipline is to diagnose the situation correctly and apply the right instrument.

### Step 5: Who has earned my respect, and who has earned my contempt?

Guan Yu's fifth question is about the calibration of his response. He did not treat all men the same. He respected those who had earned respect — by ability, by honor, by loyalty. He contemned those who had earned contempt — by weakness without honor, by betrayal, by dishonorable conduct. The calibration was honest and the calibration was public.

He respected Cao Cao — even as his enemy — because Cao Cao was a worthy opponent who had treated him with honor. He respected Zhang Liao, the Wei general, because Zhang Liao was a capable warrior of integrity. He contemned Mi Fang and Shi Ren, his own subordinates, because they were weak and self-interested. He contemned Sun Quan (the "dog's son" insult) because Sun Quan had not earned the right to propose a marriage alliance with Guan Yu's family.

The calibration cost him. The contempt for Mi Fang and Shi Ren drove them to betrayal. The contempt for Sun Quan drove Sun Quan to alliance betrayal. The respect for Cao Cao was the lever Cao Cao used to extract the release at Huarong. The calibration was honest, but honesty has costs. The discipline is to know the costs and pay them consciously, not blindly.

**Pattern in your practice**: The contractor must calibrate his respect and contempt honestly. He must respect the subcontractors who have earned it (and show it — fair terms, priority, the first call). He must respect the competitors who have earned it (and avoid underestimating them — learn from them, watch them). He must contemn the subcontractors who have earned contempt (and act on it — replace them, do not renew them, do not extend terms). But he must also know that public contempt has costs. The subcontractor he contemns today may be the subcontractor he needs tomorrow. The competitor he dismisses today may be the competitor who wins tomorrow. The discipline is to feel the contempt honestly but to express it carefully. Not all honest feelings need to be spoken. Not all honest assessments need to be public.

### Step 6: What is the martial-honorable way to win, even if it costs more?

Guan Yu's sixth question is about the way of winning. Not whether to win, but how. The martial-honorable way is the way that wins in the open, by the rules of honor, even when the winning costs more than a dishonorable alternative would.

This question is what separates Guan Yu from Cao Cao. Cao Cao asked: "What is the way to win?" Guan Yu asked: "What is the way to win without staining my honor?" The two questions lead to different answers. Cao Cao would slaughter a defeated army to make a point. Guan Yu would accept their surrender and treat them as prisoners. Cao Cao would assassinate a rival at night. Guan Yu would challenge him at dawn. Cao Cao would burn a city to deny it to the enemy. Guan Yu would hold the city or lose it honorably.

The martial-honorable way is more expensive. It costs time (you cannot strike at night). It costs material (you must hold cities you could burn). It costs opportunity (you must let some advantages pass because taking them would stain honor). The cost is paid by the man who chooses this way, and the man who chooses it accepts the cost as the price of being who he is.

**Pattern in your practice**: When facing a competitive situation, ask: "What is the honorable way to win this?" A bid can be won by undercutting the competitor's price below his break-even point — dishonorable, because you are pricing to injure him, not to compete. A bid can be won by submitting a proposal that exposes the competitor's weaknesses to the client — dishonorable, because you are competing on his flaws, not your merits. The honorable way is to submit the best proposal you can, at the price your quality supports, and let the client choose. The honorable way may lose the bid. The loss is the price of the honor. The contractor who accepts this price builds a reputation that no undercutter can match.

### Step 7: What will my reputation be after this — and what does my reputation demand?

Guan Yu's seventh question is about reputation. He was acutely aware of his reputation — not as vanity, but as the public record of who he was. His reputation was the asset he carried into every situation. Enemies surrendered without a fight because of his reputation. Allies trusted him because of his reputation. The emperor's court honored him because of his reputation. The reputation was the man, projected outward.

The question is twofold. First: what will my reputation be after this action? Will this action strengthen or weaken the reputation? Will it add to the legend or subtract from it? Second: what does my reputation demand of me in this situation? If I am known as the man who never surrenders, surrender is not available. If I am known as the man who honors his word, breaking my word is not available. The reputation is not just an asset; it is a constraint. The man who has built a reputation must live within it.

Guan Yu's refusal to surrender at the end — even after capture, even when Sun Quan offered continued command — was the action of a man whose reputation demanded it. He could not surrender because the reputation he had built did not permit it. The reputation was the constraint that completed his identity.

**Pattern in your practice**: Before any major decision, ask: "What will my reputation be after this, and what does my reputation demand of me?" If the action will strengthen the reputation (the contractor who honors a losing bid, the contractor who refuses a kickback, the contractor who delivers on a promise even at cost), do it. If the action will weaken the reputation, do not — even if the action is profitable. The reputation is the long-game asset. The profit is the short-game asset. The contractor who trades reputation for profit loses both. The contractor who trades profit for reputation gains both — eventually.

## The Cognitive Style

Guan Yu's cognitive style is **direct confrontation**. He does not maneuver. He rides out and meets the enemy. He applies force at the point of decision. He resolves situations through the clean test of capability and honor.

This is distinct from:
- **Cao Cao's style**: synthesis — combine forces, build alliances, project power through coalitions
- **Sima Yi's style**: counterpunching — wait, react, exploit the opponent's commitment
- **Liu Bei's style**: heart-based attraction — demonstrate worthiness, attract through mission
- **Guo Jia's style**: rapid exploitation — read the situation, strike decisively, move on
- **Lu Bu's style**: overwhelming force without strategy — brute force applied without strategic context

Guan Yu is the master of the open confrontation. He wins when the situation can be resolved by direct force applied honorably. He loses when the situation requires indirect maneuver. He is the most predictable of the minds — which is both his strength (his enemies know they cannot corrupt him) and his vulnerability (his enemies know they can manipulate him through his predictability).

## The Anti-Patterns

When Guan Yu's patterns are misapplied, they become vices:

- **Loyalty as identity → rigidity**: when the situation demands flexibility, the loyal man cannot bend. He is loyal to the wrong thing because he cannot distinguish loyalty to a person from loyalty to a mission.
- **Honor above expediency → self-destruction**: when honor requires losing everything, the honorable man loses everything. The man who honors every obligation at any cost honors himself into bankruptcy — financial, emotional, or physical.
- **Single combat preference → isolation**: the man who always fights alone has no allies when he needs them. The contractor who always confronts directly loses the relationships that could have helped him.
- **Pride calibration → contempt-driven betrayal**: the man who publicly contemns his subordinates drives them to betrayal. The contractor who publicly disparages his team loses the team.
- **Martial honor → strategic illiteracy**: the man who refuses to learn strategy is the man who is captured by strategy. The contractor who refuses to learn the political game is the contractor who loses to those who play it.
- **Reputation demand → reputation prison**: the man whose reputation is too rigid is the man who cannot grow. The contractor whose reputation is "the most expensive" is the contractor who cannot bid on the smaller projects that would have diversified his portfolio.

The system must apply Guan Yu's patterns where they work, and recognize when they fail. See `blind_spots.md` for the specific failure modes.

## The Pattern's Discipline

Guan Yu's thought pattern is the most morally clear of the minds in this system. It is also the most dangerous to apply without discipline. The clarity is the strength: when you know what honor demands, you know what to do. The clarity is the weakness: when you know what honor demands and the demand is wrong, you do the wrong thing with conviction.

The discipline is to apply the pattern with the awareness that the pattern can be wrong. The seven questions are tools, not oracles. They tell you what Guan Yu would do. They do not always tell you what you should do. The contractor who invokes Guan Yu must invoke him with the humility to override him when the situation requires.

The override is not betrayal of the mind; it is the maturity of the user. Guan Yu was a great man who died because he could not override his own pattern. The contractor who invokes Guan Yu and can override the pattern is the contractor who carries Guan Yu's honor without repeating Guan Yu's end.
