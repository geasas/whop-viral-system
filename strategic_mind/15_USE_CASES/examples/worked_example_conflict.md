## Why this file exists

Partner conflict is the single most common strategic problem a contractor faces after the bid decision and the cash flow problem. Partnerships in Egyptian contracting are common — a structural contractor and an electrical contractor partner to bid on projects neither could win alone; a contractor and a developer partner for in-kind contributions; a contractor and a supplier partner for material credit. Each partnership is a relationship under stress: the partners have aligned interests at the start (win the bid, deliver the project, share the profit) and divergent interests under stress (one partner's margin squeeze becomes the other partner's margin squeeze; one partner's payment delay becomes the other partner's cash flow problem; one partner's client relationship becomes the other partner's excluded relationship).

This file exists because partner conflicts are where contractors lose the most reputation and the most money. A bid that goes wrong costs one project's profit. A partnership that goes wrong costs a relationship, a recurring pipeline, and often a market reputation. The conflict decision deserves more processing than the bid decision because the conflict's consequences are larger and longer-lasting.

This file is the worked example of how the system processes a partner-conflict question. The situation is composite (anonymized from several real partnership conflict patterns), but every behavioral signal, every power asymmetry, and every relational dynamic is realistic for the Egyptian market in 2026.

## The situation

It is mid-November 2026. The user — an Egyptian electrical contractor at Stage 4 of the expansion arc — is in a 3-year-old partnership with a structural contractor ("Partner Z").

**The partnership's history**:

- The partnership began in late 2023, when Partner Z (a structural contractor with a Class B classification and a strong New Cairo developer client base) and the user (an electrical contractor with a Class C classification and a growing New Cairo presence) agreed to bid jointly on a 30-unit residential project for a mid-tier developer. The joint bid won at a combined EGP 18M (EGP 12M structural, EGP 6M electrical), with the developer's preference for a single-point-of-contact contractor leading Partner Z to act as the lead contractor and the user as the subcontractor through Partner Z.
- The structure was informal — no joint venture agreement, no SPV, just a mutual understanding that the two firms would bid jointly on suitable projects, Partner Z as the lead, the user as the electrical subcontractor. The profit-sharing was also informal: each firm kept its own scope's profit; the developer's payment flowed through Partner Z, who deducted his scope's revenue and passed the electrical scope's revenue to the user within 7-10 days of receipt.
- The first project (the 30-unit residential) was delivered on schedule in 2024, with one payment delay from the developer (40 days late on the second progress certify) that Partner Z absorbed by fronting the user's electrical payment from his own cash flow. This was the partnership's high-water mark — Partner Z demonstrated that he treated the user's cash flow as his own.
- A second project (a 22-unit residential in 6th of October) followed in 2024-2025, delivered on schedule, with no payment issues.
- A third project (a 40-unit mixed-use in New Cairo) began in early 2026, currently at month 8 of 14, with two prior progress certifies paid on schedule.

**The conflict signal (the past 6 weeks)**:

- **Three weeks ago**: Partner Z signed a variation order with the developer on the third project (the 40-unit mixed-use) for an additional EGP 800K of structural scope (a parking garage expansion) without consulting the user. The variation order does not affect the electrical scope directly, but it extends the project duration by 6 weeks — which extends the user's electrical scope by 6 weeks (the user's commissioning phase depends on the structural completion).
- **Two weeks ago**: Partner Z signed a second variation order with the developer for an additional EGP 350K of low-current scope (CCTV and access control) — work that has historically been the user's scope on this partnership. The variation order was awarded to a different subcontractor (a low-current specialist Partner Z has used on prior projects without the user). The user learned of the variation order from the developer's consultant, not from Partner Z.
- **Last week**: The user attended the developer's monthly coordination meeting (the first the user has attended in 6 months — Partner Z had been attending alone, telling the user the meetings were routine). At the meeting, the developer raised with the user directly the question of "your next project with us" — implying the developer has been discussing the next project with Partner Z without including the user in the discussion. The user, unprepared, gave a noncommittal answer.

**The user's current state**:

- **Pipeline**: 5 active projects, of which 2 are with Partner Z (the third project at month 8, plus a smaller 12-unit project at month 2). The 2 Partner Z projects together represent EGP 5.5M of the user's EGP 18M annualized revenue (roughly 30% of the pipeline).
- **Working capital**: EGP 2.0M, plus EGP 1.2M in receivables from Partner Z (the third project's last progress certify, due in 8 days).
- **Crew**: 2 senior site engineers, 4 junior engineers, 22 electricians. The 2 Partner Z projects consume 1 senior site engineer (50% commitment) and 8 electricians.
- **The relationship with Partner Z**: 3 years, 3 projects, no prior conflict. The user considered Partner Z a friend (they have shared meals at each other's homes, their families have met). The user's father and Partner Z's father knew each other professionally 20 years ago.

**The conflict pattern**:

The three-week, two-week, one-week sequence is not isolated incidents. The sequence is a pattern: Partner Z is making decisions on the partnership without consulting the user, is redirecting scope from the user to other subcontractors, and is cultivating the developer relationship independently of the user. The user has not yet confronted Partner Z. The user has not yet spoken to the developer. The user is at the moment of decision: how to respond.

## The user's first instinct (and the system's audit)

The user's first instinct: **"I will confront Partner Z at the next site meeting. I will tell him that the variation orders without consultation are unacceptable, that the low-current scope was my scope, and that the developer's next-project discussion should include me. If he refuses to acknowledge the issue, I will pull out of the third project and the 12-unit project."**

This is the contractor's first instinct on a partner conflict. It is the instinct that destroys partnerships and damages reputations. The instinct treats the conflict as a personal betrayal (correct, in the emotional sense) and skips the strategic and relational audit (incorrect, in the structural sense).

**The system's audit of the first instinct**:

1. **Emotional reactivity bias**: The first instinct is emotional — the user feels betrayed and wants to act on the feeling. Emotional reactivity in a partner conflict is the most expensive mistake a contractor can make. The instinct must be cooled before the conversation, not during it.
2. **Attribution bias**: The instinct assumes Partner Z is acting from betrayal motives (cutting the user out, cultivating the developer independently, redirecting scope for personal gain). This attribution may be correct, may be partial, or may be wrong. Partner Z may be acting under his own pressure (cash flow, a developer squeeze, a competing offer from another electrical subcontractor) that the user does not see. The attribution must be tested before it is acted on.
3. **Binary framing bias**: The instinct frames the response as "confront or pull out." The binary framing eliminates the middle path — the structured conversation that clarifies the situation, the renegotiation of the partnership terms, the strategic repositioning that preserves the relationship while protecting the user's interests.
4. **Leverage blindness**: The instinct does not consider the user's leverage. The user has EGP 5.5M of annual revenue tied to the partnership (30% of the pipeline), and 1 senior site engineer and 8 electricians committed. The user cannot pull out of the third project at month 8 without breaching the contract and damaging the developer relationship. The user's leverage is limited; the instinct over-estimates it.
5. **Reputation blindness**: The instinct does not consider the reputation cost of a public confrontation or a mid-project pull-out. The Egyptian contracting market is small; the user's behavior in this conflict will be known to the developer, the consultant, the other subcontractors, and the Federation of Contractors' informal channel within 30 days. The user must act in a way that, when known, strengthens the user's reputation rather than weakening it.
6. **Long-game blindness**: The instinct does not consider the user's Stage 4 → Stage 5 expansion arc. The user is building toward real estate development. The developer (the third project's developer) is exactly the kind of mid-tier New Cairo developer the user needs for the Stage 5 expansion. A confrontation with Partner Z that damages the developer relationship damages the user's Stage 5 path. The instinct does not see this.
7. **Self-deception blindness**: The instinct does not ask whether the user contributed to the conflict. Has the user been declining the monthly coordination meetings? Has the user been slow on the third project's electrical scope, prompting Partner Z to seek alternatives? Has the user been visibly distracted by the partnership evaluation with Developer Y (per `worked_example_partnership.md`)? The user's own behavior is part of the conflict's cause; the instinct does not examine it.

The system's response: **"Stop. Before you confront Partner Z, run the conflict-decision framework."** The system then walks the user through the seven-step pipeline.

## Mind routing — Liu Bei, Chen Gong, Sima Yi

### Mind 1: Liu Bei — relationship and legitimacy

**Why Liu Bei**: The partnership is a 3-year relationship with a friend, not just a commercial arrangement. Liu Bei's question: "What does the user's response signal about the user as a long-term partner and as a person?"

**What Liu Bei sees**:
- The user's response will be observed by Partner Z (who knows the user), the developer (who is forming an opinion of the user), the consultant (who sees the user at the monthly meetings), and the user's own crew (who hear the user's conversations on site). The response is a signal to all four audiences.
- A response that is firm but respectful signals a partner who holds the line without destroying the relationship. A response that is emotional and confrontational signals a partner who is reactive and unreliable. A response that is silent signals a partner who accepts the disrespect. Liu Bei's principle is to choose the response that signals the user's character — not to react to the partner's behavior but to act from the user's principles.
- The user's deepest principle (per `10_PERSONAL/spiritual/practical_faith.md` and `10_PERSONAL/psychology_self/self_knowledge.md`) is integrity: the user does not deceive, does not betray, does not abandon a relationship without a fair conversation. The response that aligns with the user's principle is the structured conversation, not the public confrontation and not the silent withdrawal.
- Liu Bei's specific recommendation: the user requests a private meeting with Partner Z (not at the site, not in front of the crew, not at the developer's meeting). The user opens the conversation not with accusations but with questions: "I noticed the variation orders were signed without consultation. I noticed the low-current scope was redirected to another subcontractor. I noticed the developer has been discussing the next project with you without me. I want to understand the situation before I respond." The questions are Liu Bei's tool — they signal respect and invite explanation, without conceding the user's right to be consulted.

### Mind 2: Chen Gong — honest counsel about the principal

**Why Chen Gong**: The user, in this partnership, has bound himself to Partner Z as a principal. Chen Gong's worldview (see `07_MINDS/chen_gong/worldview.md`) is that the choice of principal is a moral act and the principal's character under stress is what matters. The user is now seeing Partner Z's character under stress (or under temptation) for the first time.

**What Chen Gong sees**:
- The 3-year partnership has been a fair-weather partnership. The first project's payment delay (Partner Z fronted the user's payment from his own cash flow) was the partnership's only stress test, and Partner Z passed it. The current pattern (variation orders without consultation, scope redirection, developer relationship cultivation) is the second stress test — and Partner Z is failing it, or at least not passing it cleanly.
- The question is not "is Partner Z a bad person" — the question is "is Partner Z a principal the user can bind to for the next 3 years." Chen Gong's worldview: the principal's character under stress is what the user is bound to. If Partner Z's character under stress is to exclude the user from decisions, redirect the user's scope, and cultivate the developer independently, the user is bound to that character for the duration of the partnership. The user must decide whether that character is acceptable.
- Chen Gong's principled position: the user should not stay in a partnership with a principal whose character the user cannot defend. The user's principled options are (a) restructure the partnership so the user is no longer bound to Partner Z's character (formalize the joint venture with consultation rights, scope protection, and developer-relationship co-management), or (b) exit the partnership with integrity (deliver the third project and the 12-unit project, decline the next partnership offer, end the relationship with a fair conversation).
- Chen Gong's anti-pattern: the user should not stay in the partnership silently absorbing the disrespect. Silent absorption is the advisor who chooses to remain with a principal whose character is unworthy, which is Chen Gong's definition of the tragic advisor.

### Mind 3: Sima Yi — long-game patience

**Why Sima Yi**: The user's response to this conflict has long-game consequences for the Stage 5 expansion. Sima Yi's question: "What response advances the user's 5-10 year strategic position, not just the next 30 days?"

**What Sima Yi sees**:
- The conflict is happening at a moment when the user is also evaluating the Developer Y partnership (per `worked_example_partnership.md`). The two decisions are coupled: if the user enters the Developer Y partnership, the user's reliance on Partner Z decreases (the Developer Y partnership provides a different development path). If the user declines the Developer Y partnership, the user's reliance on Partner Z increases (the user needs the recurring pipeline).
- The conflict is also happening at a moment when the user is at Stage 4 → Stage 5. The conflict's resolution determines whether the user exits Stage 4 with the Partner Z pipeline intact, with the Partner Z pipeline restructured, or with the Partner Z pipeline terminated. Each exit has different Stage 5 implications.
- The long-game response is the response that (a) preserves the developer relationship regardless of the Partner Z resolution, (b) preserves the user's market reputation as a partner who handles conflict with integrity, (c) does not consume the user's working capital or crew capacity in a way that compromises the Stage 5 expansion, (d) extracts the maximum information from the conflict about Partner Z's character, which is the information the user needs for the next partnership decision.
- Sima Yi's specific recommendation: the user should slow the response down. The conflict has been building for 6 weeks; the user does not need to respond in 6 days. The user should spend 1-2 weeks preparing the structured conversation with Partner Z (gathering the facts, modeling the leverage, scripting the questions) before having it. The slow response is the long-game response.

## Hypothesis generation — 5 hypotheses

### Hypothesis 1 (from Liu Bei): "Structured conversation, restructure the partnership, preserve the relationship"

**The hypothesis**: Have the structured conversation with Partner Z (Liu Bei's recommended opening), present the user's concerns as questions, listen to Partner Z's explanation, and propose a restructured partnership: a formal joint venture agreement (the current arrangement is informal) with consultation rights on material decisions, scope protection for the user's electrical and low-current scopes, and co-management of the developer relationship. If Partner Z accepts the restructure, the partnership continues on firmer terms. If Partner Z rejects the restructure, the user has the information to make the exit decision.

**Assumptions**:
- Partner Z will engage with the structured conversation (Partner Z has not been hostile; he has been excluding, not attacking).
- The restructured terms are negotiable (Partner Z may resist formalization; he may prefer the informal arrangement that lets him act without consultation).
- The developer will tolerate the renegotiation (the developer's interest is project delivery, not the user's partnership structure).

**Failure modes**:
- Partner Z rejects the restructure and the conversation becomes the confrontation the user was trying to avoid.
- Partner Z accepts the restructure but does not honor it (signs the formal agreement, continues the exclusionary behavior).
- The conversation surfaces a deeper issue (Partner Z is in financial trouble and is redirecting scope for cash flow reasons; the user's partnership is collateral damage) that the restructure cannot fix.

### Hypothesis 2 (from Chen Gong): "Deliver the current projects, decline the next partnership, exit with integrity"

**The hypothesis**: Do not confront Partner Z with a restructure proposal. Continue to deliver the third project and the 12-unit project on schedule and to quality. Decline Partner Z's next partnership offer (when it comes, since the third project will complete in 6 months and the next project is typically discussed 3-4 months before). Exit the partnership with a clear and kind conversation at the natural end of the third project: "We have delivered 3 projects together over 3 years. I am moving my firm in a different direction (the Stage 5 expansion). I value the relationship and want to end it cleanly." No accusations, no restructure, no confrontation.

**Assumptions**:
- The user can deliver the third project and the 12-unit project without further conflict (Partner Z may continue the exclusionary behavior; the user must absorb it for the project duration).
- The user has an alternative pipeline for the 30% of revenue currently provided by the Partner Z partnership (the user must develop the alternative in the next 6 months).
- Partner Z will accept the exit without retaliation (Partner Z may speak negatively of the user in the market; the user must be prepared).

**Failure modes**:
- The user cannot develop the alternative pipeline in 6 months and faces a revenue gap when the Partner Z partnership ends.
- Partner Z escalates the exclusionary behavior during the project delivery (more scope redirection, more developer-relationship exclusion), making the user's silence harder to maintain.
- Partner Z does not accept the exit and tries to renegotiate or to involve the developer in the partnership dispute.

### Hypothesis 3 (from Sima Yi): "Cultivate the developer relationship independently of Partner Z, position for the direct partnership"

**The hypothesis**: The user's long-game response is to cultivate the developer relationship directly, independently of Partner Z. The user attends the developer's monthly coordination meetings consistently (not occasionally). The user initiates direct technical communications with the consultant (not through Partner Z). The user, when the developer raises the "next project" question, responds directly: "We are interested in the next project; we would welcome a direct conversation." The user positions for a direct electrical contract with the developer on the next project, bypassing the Partner Z partnership.

**Assumptions**:
- The developer will accept a direct relationship with the user (the developer's interest is the best electrical contractor; the user may or may not be that contractor in the developer's view).
- Partner Z will not obstruct the direct relationship (Partner Z may see the direct relationship as a betrayal and respond aggressively).
- The user can deliver the next project directly without the structural lead that Partner Z provides (the user's electrical scope does not require Partner Z; but the developer's preference for a single-point-of-contact may require a structural lead, which Partner Z has been).

**Failure modes**:
- The developer prefers the Partner Z partnership (the developer has been working with Partner Z for 3 years and may not want the disruption of a direct electrical contract).
- Partner Z obstructs the direct relationship (Partner Z speaks negatively of the user to the developer; the developer's preference for Partner Z is reinforced).
- The user wins the direct electrical contract but loses the structural lead relationship, complicating the project execution (the user must coordinate with a different structural contractor, who may be less compatible than Partner Z).

### Hypothesis 4 (from Cao Cao): "Multi-stakeholder renegotiation — bring the developer into the conversation"

**The hypothesis**: The conflict is not just between the user and Partner Z; the developer is a stakeholder whose interests are affected (the variation orders, the scope redirection, the next-project discussion). The user's response is to bring the developer into the conversation: request a meeting with the developer (without Partner Z) to discuss "the structure of the electrical scope on the next project." The user presents the user's perspective (the partnership has worked well; the user is now considering direct contracts for the next project; the developer's preference is solicited). The developer's response informs the user's next move.

**Assumptions**:
- The developer will meet with the user without Partner Z (the developer may feel uncomfortable being drawn into a partnership dispute).
- The developer's preference is informative (the developer may not have a clear preference; the developer may say "I work with whatever structure works").
- The user can present the perspective without attacking Partner Z (the framing must be positive — the user is interested in direct contracts, not in complaining about Partner Z).

**Failure modes**:
- The developer refuses the meeting (the developer does not want to be drawn into the dispute).
- The developer meets with the user but tells Partner Z about the meeting (Partner Z sees the meeting as a betrayal; the conflict escalates).
- The developer's preference is for Partner Z (the developer prefers the single-point-of-contact structure; the user's direct contract is not desired).

### Hypothesis 5 (from Chen Gong + Liu Bei): "Self-audit first — examine the user's contribution to the conflict"

**The hypothesis**: Before responding to Partner Z, the user audits the user's own behavior in the partnership over the past 6-12 months. Has the user been declining coordination meetings? Has the user been slow on the electrical scope, prompting Partner Z to seek alternatives? Has the user been visibly distracted by the Developer Y partnership evaluation, signaling reduced commitment to the Partner Z partnership? Has the user been communicating less with Partner Z, allowing the distance to grow? The user's self-audit may reveal that the conflict is partly the user's creation, which changes the response: the structured conversation becomes a joint audit, not a confrontation about Partner Z's behavior.

**Assumptions**:
- The user has the self-discipline to audit honestly (the user's emotional reactivity bias will resist the audit).
- The audit produces useful information (the user's behavior may be unremarkable; the audit may not change the response).
- The audit does not become self-blame (the user's behavior is part of the conflict's cause, but Partner Z's behavior is also part; the audit should not absolve Partner Z).

**Failure modes**:
- The audit becomes self-blame, the user apologizes to Partner Z, and Partner Z's behavior is not addressed.
- The audit becomes a weapon ("you did this, but I did that"), turning the conversation into a contest of grievances.
- The audit is honest but the user's behavior is not the cause (Partner Z's behavior is driven by Partner Z's own pressures, not by the user's behavior).

## The analysis (per 03_ANALYSIS)

### Power dynamics

Partner Z has more power than the user. Partner Z is the lead contractor, the developer's primary contact, the structural scope owner (which dominates the project's value), and the senior partner in the informal partnership. The user is the subcontractor, the secondary contact, the electrical scope owner (a smaller share of the project value).

The user's power is enhanced by the user's quality and reliability (the developer's consultant respects the user's electrical work) and by the user's Stage 5 trajectory (the user is becoming a contractor-developer, which is a higher-value position than Partner Z's structural contractor position).

The power asymmetry is structural (Partner Z is the lead) but reducible (the user can cultivate the developer directly, can position for direct contracts, can become a higher-value player through the Stage 5 expansion).

### Interests behind positions

Partner Z's stated position (implicit in his behavior): "I am the lead contractor; I make the decisions; the user is my subcontractor." Partner Z's interest: maintain the lead position, capture more of the project's value (the low-current scope redirection increases Partner Z's scope and margin), reduce the developer's dependence on the user (the developer-relationship cultivation reduces the user's leverage).

Partner Z's deeper interest may be: financial pressure (the structural contracting market in 2026 is tight; Partner Z may be capturing scope to maintain his margin), or competitive pressure (Partner Z may be positioning against the user's Stage 5 expansion, which would make the user a more senior player than Partner Z), or simple drift (Partner Z may not have consciously decided to exclude the user; the exclusion may be the result of busy-ness and short-cuts).

The user's interest: maintain the relationship (for the recurring pipeline), protect the user's scope and reputation, advance the Stage 5 expansion (which requires the developer relationship), preserve the user's integrity (the user does not silently accept disrespect).

### Leverage detection

The user's leverage points:
- The user's quality and reliability (the developer's consultant respects the user; the developer's handover quality depends on the user's electrical commissioning).
- The user's Stage 5 trajectory (the user is becoming a contractor-developer; Partner Z is not. In 2-3 years, the user will be the senior player; Partner Z will be the subcontractor).
- The user's partnership with Developer Y (if it materializes) — the user's alternative development path reduces the user's reliance on Partner Z.

The user's leverage gaps:
- The user's revenue reliance on Partner Z (30% of the pipeline).
- The user's crew commitment to the Partner Z projects (1 senior site engineer, 8 electricians).
- The user's emotional reactivity (the user's leverage is reduced by the user's visible emotional state).

## The recommendation

**Recommendation: Hypothesis 5 + Hypothesis 1, in sequence — self-audit first, then structured conversation with a restructure proposal.**

**Confidence: MEDIUM (7 of 12 on the `00_META/confidence_scoring.md` scale).**

**Why MEDIUM, not HIGH**:
- Partner Z's response to the structured conversation is uncertain. The conversation may surface information that changes the user's response.
- The self-audit may not produce useful information (the user's behavior may be unremarkable, in which case Hypothesis 5 is empty).
- The developer's response to the user's independent cultivation (if Hypothesis 3 is layered in later) is uncertain.

**Why MEDIUM, not LOW**:
- The structure of the recommendation (self-audit → structured conversation → restructure proposal → fallback to deliver-and-exit) is robust to most unknowns. The user's integrity is preserved. The developer relationship is preserved. The market reputation is preserved.
- The strategic fit is verified — the recommendation advances the Stage 5 expansion by positioning the user for either a restructured Partner Z partnership or a direct developer relationship.

**Counter-evidence**:
- The self-audit may surface the user's contribution to the conflict in a way that deflates the user's leverage (the user's realization that he has been distant may make the restructure proposal feel less defensible).
- The structured conversation may not produce a clear answer from Partner Z (Partner Z may be evasive, non-committal, or defensive) — in which case the user is left with the deliver-and-exit path without a restructure.
- The deliver-and-exit path (Hypothesis 2 fallback) consumes 6 months of silence, during which Partner Z may continue the exclusionary behavior and the user must absorb it.

**The conditional path**:

- **Days 1-7 (self-audit, Hypothesis 5)**: The user writes out, in private, the user's behavior in the partnership over the past 12 months. The categories: coordination meeting attendance, electrical scope delivery timing, communication frequency with Partner Z, communication frequency with the developer, time and attention to the partnership relative to other projects, visible signals of commitment or distraction. The user identifies 2-3 specific behaviors that may have contributed to the distance (e.g., "I declined the April coordination meeting because I was at the Developer Y partnership meeting," "I was 12 days late on the August progress report because I was distracted").
- **Days 8-14 (scripting, Hypothesis 1 preparation)**: The user scripts the structured conversation with Partner Z. The opening (Liu Bei's question, not accusation): "I want to understand the situation before I respond. I noticed three things in the past 6 weeks [the variation orders, the scope redirection, the developer's next-project discussion without me]. Help me understand what's happening." The middle (the self-audit acknowledgment): "I've also realized I've been more distant in the past 6 months — [the specific behaviors]. I want to address that too." The close (the restructure proposal): "I'd like to formalize the partnership — a written joint venture agreement with consultation rights on material decisions, scope protection, and developer-relationship co-management. Can we discuss this?"
- **Days 15-21 (the conversation)**: The user requests the private meeting with Partner Z. The meeting is at Partner Z's office or a neutral location (not at the site, not at the user's office). The user opens with the script. The user listens more than speaks. The user does not respond to Partner Z's first reaction — the user asks for time to think about Partner Z's response before deciding the next step.
- **Days 22-30 (the decision)**: Based on Partner Z's response, the user decides:
  - If Partner Z acknowledges the exclusionary behavior, explains the cause (financial pressure, competitive pressure, drift), and accepts the restructure proposal: proceed with the restructured partnership.
  - If Partner Z acknowledges the behavior but rejects the restructure (prefers the informal arrangement): the user has the information to make the exit decision (Hypothesis 2). The user delivers the third project and the 12-unit project, declines the next partnership offer, and exits at the natural end of the third project.
  - If Partner Z denies the behavior or attacks the user: the user has the information that the partnership is no longer tenable. The user delivers the third project and the 12-unit project with formal documentation of every interaction, declines the next partnership offer, and exits at the natural end of the third project. The exit conversation is brief and final.

## The follow-up — what to monitor, what to do if conditions change

### If the restructured partnership proceeds

- **Month 1-3**: Monitor Partner Z's behavior against the restructured agreement. Does Partner Z consult before material decisions? Does Partner Z protect the user's scope? Does Partner Z include the user in the developer relationship? The monitoring is weekly for the first month, monthly thereafter. Any breach of the restructured agreement is documented immediately (in writing, to Partner Z) — not at the end of the project.
- **Month 4-12**: The restructured partnership is in operation. The user continues to cultivate the developer relationship directly (Hypothesis 3, layered in) — attend the monthly coordination meetings, communicate directly with the consultant. The user is not dependent on the restructured partnership; the user is positioned for either a continued partnership or a direct contract on the next project.

### If the deliver-and-exit path is taken

- **Month 1-6**: Deliver the third project and the 12-unit project on schedule and to quality. Document every interaction with Partner Z (in writing). Decline any new partnership offers from Partner Z with a kind and firm response: "We have a different direction for the next phase."
- **Month 4-6**: Begin the alternative pipeline development. Identify 2-3 candidate projects (direct electrical contracts or new partnerships) that can replace the EGP 5.5M of annual revenue currently tied to Partner Z. Pursue 1-2 of these candidates with the same bid discipline the user applies to any bid (per `worked_example_bidding.md`).
- **Month 6 (third project completion)**: Exit conversation with Partner Z. The framing: "We have delivered 3 projects together over 3 years. I am grateful for the partnership. I am moving my firm in a different direction. I want to end the partnership cleanly and remain on good terms." No accusations, no rehashing. If Partner Z asks why, the user gives a one-sentence answer: "The partnership structure has not been working for me for the past 6 months. I'd rather end it cleanly than continue with the tension." The user does not elaborate.
- **Month 6-12**: Cultivate the developer relationship directly. Attend the developer's next-project discussions. Position for a direct electrical contract on the developer's next project. The user has 6-12 months of direct cultivation to convert the developer from a Partner Z-led relationship to a direct relationship.

### If conditions change

- **If Partner Z escalates the exclusionary behavior during the deliver-and-exit period (more scope redirection, public criticism, developer-relationship obstruction)**: The user must decide whether to exit earlier (terminate the third project contractually, accept the breach cost) or to absorb the behavior until the natural end. The decision depends on the breach cost (the contract's termination clauses) and the reputation cost (the market's perception of the user's behavior). Sima Yi's principle: the user should not absorb the behavior past the point where the absorption damages the user's market reputation. The threshold is the point at which the developer's consultant or the developer himself raises the issue with the user — at that point, the user must respond, not absorb.
- **If the Developer Y partnership (per `worked_example_partnership.md`) is signed**: The user's reliance on Partner Z decreases. The deliver-and-exit path becomes easier (the user has an alternative development path; the revenue reliance on Partner Z is less critical). The user may accelerate the exit (decline the next partnership offer sooner, deliver the third project and the 12-unit project, exit in 6 months).
- **If the Developer Y partnership is declined**: The user's reliance on Partner Z increases. The deliver-and-exit path becomes harder (the user has no alternative development path; the revenue reliance on Partner Z is more critical). The user may slow the exit (continue the partnership for another cycle, accept the exclusionary behavior for another 12 months, prepare the alternative pipeline in parallel). The slow exit is the Sima Yi response — patience until the alternative is ready.

## The post-decision audit — what went right, what went wrong

### The decision audit (after the structured conversation, day 22-30)

1. **Did the user conduct the self-audit honestly?** Did the user identify the user's contribution to the conflict, or did the user rationalize the user's behavior? The honest self-audit is the foundation of the structured conversation; without it, the conversation becomes an accusation.
2. **Did the user open the conversation with questions, not accusations?** The opening frame determines the conversation's tone. The question frame invites explanation; the accusation frame triggers defensiveness.
3. **Did the user listen more than speak?** The user's scripted conversation should be 30% speaking, 70% listening. If the user spoke more than 50%, the conversation was not structured; it was a presentation.
4. **Did the user avoid responding to Partner Z's first reaction?** The instinct to respond immediately is the emotional reactivity bias. The user should have asked for time to think. If the user responded immediately, the response was likely emotional, not strategic.
5. **Did the user document the conversation?** The user should write a private memo to himself within 24 hours of the conversation, recording what was said, what was agreed, what was left open. The memo is the user's record for the next decision point.

### The execution audit (at project completion, month 6)

1. **Did the deliver-and-exit path hold?** Did the user deliver the third project on schedule and to quality, or did the conflict's emotional weight affect the delivery? A contractor who lets a conflict damage the project delivery damages his own reputation, not just the partnership.
2. **Did the user maintain the developer relationship?** Did the developer's perception of the user improve, decline, or stay the same through the conflict period? The developer relationship is the partnership's most valuable asset; the user's behavior in the conflict determines whether the asset is preserved.
3. **Did the user develop the alternative pipeline?** Did the user identify and pursue 2-3 candidate projects to replace the Partner Z revenue? Without the alternative pipeline, the exit creates a revenue gap.
4. **Did the user's market reputation strengthen or weaken?** Did the market (the Federation of Contractors' informal channel, the developer's network, the consultant's network) hear about the user's conflict resolution as a story of integrity or as a story of acrimony? The story is the user's market asset.

### The strategic audit (at year-end 2027, 12 months after the conflict)

1. **Did the user's Stage 5 expansion advance?** Did the conflict's resolution (restructure or exit) advance or retard the Stage 5 expansion? The conflict is a strategic event, not just a relational event; the strategic audit measures whether the strategic outcome was positive.
2. **Did the user's partnership pattern change?** Did the user enter new partnerships with more formal structures (written agreements, consultation rights, scope protection) as a result of the Partner Z conflict? The learning from one conflict should change the structure of the next partnership.
3. **Did the user's character strengthen?** Did the user's response to the conflict align with the user's principles (integrity, patience, honesty), or did the user compromise the principles under stress? The character audit is the deepest audit; the user's character is the user's most strategic asset.

## Closing note

The partner conflict is the contractor's deepest strategic test — deeper than the bid, deeper than the partnership, deeper than the expansion. The conflict tests the contractor's character under stress, the contractor's ability to think strategically while emotional, the contractor's willingness to audit his own behavior before auditing the partner's. The contractor who masters the conflict decision becomes a contractor other contractors want to partner with — which is the most valuable market position a contractor can hold. The contractor who fails the conflict decision becomes a contractor other contractors avoid — which is the most damaging market position. The conflict is the test that determines which contractor the user becomes.
