## Why this file exists

The partnership decision is the most consequential single decision in the contractor's expansion arc. A bid commits 6-18 months and EGP 1-5M. A partnership commits 18-36 months, EGP 1-10M, and a substantial portion of the contractor's reputation and crew capacity. More importantly, a partnership determines who the contractor becomes — a contractor who has done one development partnership is no longer the same contractor; he is a contractor-developer, with a different identity, different relationships, and a different risk profile.

This file exists because partnership decisions are reversible only at high cost. A bid can be lost; a partnership, once entered, can be exited only through sale, dispute, or default — each of which damages the contractor's reputation and capital. The partnership decision deserves more processing than the bid decision, not less. This file is the worked example of how the system processes a partnership question.

## The situation

It is early November 2026. The user — an Egyptian electrical contractor at late Stage 4 of the expansion arc — is approached by a real estate developer (call him "Developer Y").

**The offer**:

- **Project**: A 50-unit residential development in 6th of October City (West of Cairo), on a 4,500 m² plot in a mid-tier residential district. The project is a 4-building, 5-story configuration, with 2-bedroom and 3-bedroom units, target price EGP 4.5-6.5M per unit. Total project revenue projection: EGP 250M over a 24-month sales window.
- **The developer's contribution**: The land (valued at EGP 60M), the project licensing (final, building permit issued 2 months ago), the development management, the sales and marketing. Developer Y has completed 2 prior residential projects in 6th of October (12 units in 2021, 28 units in 2023) and one in New Cairo (36 units in 2024, currently in delivery).
- **The contractor's (user's) contribution**: Full electrical works (valued at EGP 9-11M, depending on materials specification) for the 50 units, executed over 18 months. The contribution is "in-kind" — the user performs the electrical works without cash payment; the value of the works is credited as the user's equity contribution.
- **The equity structure**: 30% equity to the user, 70% to the developer, in a special-purpose vehicle (SPV) formed for the project. Profits distributed pro-rata at project completion (or earlier, if the developer chooses to distribute interim profits from unit sales).
- **The governance**: A two-person board (the developer's CEO and the user), with the developer as the managing partner with day-to-day operating authority. Material decisions (scope changes, sales price changes, contract variations above EGP 500K, additional financing) require unanimous board approval.
- **The exit**: The user's exit is at project completion (after the retention period of 12 months post-handover), with the user's 30% share convertible to either a cash distribution (if the SPV is wound down) or a continued equity stake in the developer's next SPV (if the developer chooses to roll the partnership forward).
- **The timeline**: The developer proposes to sign the partnership agreement within 30 days. Site mobilization within 60 days. First unit delivery targeted for Q3 2027; full delivery Q2 2028.

**The user's current state**:

- **Pipeline**: 5 active projects totaling EGP 18M annualized, with the largest at month 4 of 8.
- **Working capital**: EGP 2.2M liquid, plus EGP 1.5M in receivables due over the next 90 days.
- **Crew**: 2 senior site engineers, 4 junior engineers, 22 electricians. Capacity for 1-2 more projects of moderate scale.
- **Strategic position**: Late Stage 4. The user has built a credible electrical contracting business and a mid-tier client base. The next strategic move (Stage 5) is the move into real estate development. This offer is the first concrete opportunity to make that move.
- **The relationship**: Developer Y is known to the user through a mutual friend (a senior engineer at the Federation of Contractors). The user has met Developer Y three times: once at the mutual friend's office, once at a developer site visit, once for the formal partnership offer.

**The decision window**: 30 days (the developer has stated other potential partners are being evaluated; the user does not know whether this is true or a negotiation tactic).

## The user's first instinct (and the system's audit)

The user's first instinct: **"This is the opportunity to enter real estate development. 30% equity for in-kind electrical work is a great deal — I'm contributing labor and materials I would otherwise sell for a 12% margin, and getting 30% of a project that projects EGP 60-80M profit. The math is obvious. Let's say yes."**

This is the contractor's first instinct on a development partnership. It is the instinct that has trapped contractors in structures they could not exit. The instinct treats the partnership as an opportunity (correct, in the abstract) and skips the structural and strategic audit (incorrect, in the concrete).

**The system's audit of the first instinct**:

1. **Anchoring bias**: The user has anchored on the 30% equity number and on the EGP 60-80M profit projection. Both are salient. Neither is verified — the 30% is conditional on the project's actual profit (which depends on sales velocity, cost control, and the developer's management quality), and the EGP 60-80M is the developer's projection, not an independent estimate.
2. **Optimism bias**: "The math is obvious" assumes the project executes on plan. The developer's prior 28-unit project (2023) was delivered 9 months late; the 36-unit New Cairo project (2024) is currently in delivery with reported scope variations. The track record is mixed, not stellar.
3. **Sunk-cost framing**: The user has invested nothing in this partnership yet, but the framing "this is the opportunity" suggests the partnership is already a foregone conclusion. The system rejects the framing.
4. **Cash flow blindness**: The user's "in-kind" contribution is the electrical works valued at EGP 9-11M. The user must perform the works at his own cost (labor, materials, overhead) over 18 months, with no cash payment from the developer. The user's working capital is EGP 2.2M. The cash flow cycle of in-kind contribution is the binding constraint, not the equity math.
5. **Governance blindness**: The developer is the managing partner with day-to-day operating authority. The user is a 30% minority with veto on "material" decisions (defined as scope changes, price changes, variations above EGP 500K, additional financing). The user has no operating authority. The user cannot control the project; the user can only block material changes. The governance asymmetry is significant.
6. **Exit blindness**: The user's exit is at project completion (12 months post-handover). If the project is delayed (as the developer's prior projects were), the user is locked in for 30-36 months, not 24. If the developer chooses to roll the partnership forward into the next SPV, the user's exit is conditional on the developer's choice, not the user's.
7. **Strategic-fit blindness**: The instinct does not consider whether this developer is the right partner for the user's first development partnership. The user's Stage 5 expansion requires a partner who teaches the user the development business, not just a partner who extracts the user's electrical work at a discount. The instinct does not ask whether Developer Y is a teaching partner or an extracting partner.

The system's response: **"Stop. Before you sign the term sheet, run the partnership decision framework."** The system then walks the user through the seven-step pipeline.

## Mind routing — Sima Yi, Cao Cao, Liu Bei (and Chen Gong as the audit mind)

### Mind 1: Sima Yi — long-game patience

**Why Sima Yi**: The partnership is a 30-36 month commitment that affects the user's strategic arc. Sima Yi's question: "Is this the right long-game move?"

**What Sima Yi sees**:
- The user is at late Stage 4. The next move (Stage 5) is the move into real estate development. The right Stage 5 move is the move that builds the user's development capability, not the move that converts the user into a discount electrical contractor for a developer who needed a cheap electrical scope.
- The 30% equity is conditional profit. The user's actual return depends on the project's actual profit, which depends on the developer's management quality (mixed track record), the sales velocity (Egyptian real estate market in 2027-2028 is uncertain — currency, demand, mortgage rates), and the cost control (the developer's prior projects had scope variations).
- The strategic value of the partnership is the learning value: does the user learn the development business (the land acquisition, the licensing, the financing, the sales, the marketing, the project management) by being a 30% partner with governance rights, or does the user remain an electrical contractor with a paper equity stake?
- Sima Yi's first demand: a structured "learning audit" — what does the user observe, what does the user participate in, what does the user decide? If the answer is "observe little, participate less, decide nothing material," the partnership is not a learning vehicle; it is a discount electrical contract with a paper upside.

### Mind 2: Cao Cao — multi-stakeholder integration

**Why Cao Cao**: The partnership involves the developer, the user, the SPV's other stakeholders (the developer's lenders if any, the unit buyers, the contractor for the structural works, the consultant, the sales agent, the Egyptian regulatory bodies), the user's existing clients (whose projects may be compromised if the user commits 18 months of crew to this partnership), the user's crew (whose capacity is the binding constraint), the user's family (the in-kind contribution ties up EGP 9-11M of working capital that affects family reserves).

**What Cao Cao sees**:
- The crew constraint is binding. The user has 2 senior site engineers and 22 electricians. The 50-unit electrical scope over 18 months requires 1 senior site engineer (50-70% commitment) and 10-14 electricians (committed over the 18 months). The user cannot take the partnership and continue the current electrical pipeline at full capacity. The user must either decline 1-2 upcoming bids, hire additional crew, or accept execution risk on the partnership project.
- The working capital constraint is binding. The user must perform EGP 9-11M of electrical works at his own cost over 18 months, with the contribution credited as equity. The user's working capital is EGP 2.2M plus EGP 1.5M in receivables. The cash cycle: the user spends on materials and labor monthly, with no cash receipt from the developer, until the project's profit distribution (Q2 2028 or later). This is a 24-month financing gap. The user must finance the gap with (a) the receivables from existing projects, (b) supplier credit on materials, (c) the user's reserves. The math must be modeled explicitly; the "in-kind" framing hides the cash cost.
- The bank credit constraint: the user will need to provide performance bonds, advance payment guarantees, or other bank instruments for the partnership project. The user's bank credit line is EGP 3-4M (depending on classification). The partnership's bank instrument demand is EGP 1-2M over 18 months. The user's existing pipeline already consumes EGP 1-1.5M of bank credit. The partnership consumes the remaining credit and leaves no room for new electrical bids for 18 months.
- The stakeholder integration: the developer's lenders (if any — the user should verify whether the developer has bank financing on the land, which would subordinate the user's equity in a default scenario), the unit buyers (whose payments fund the project's cash flow — the user should verify the sales velocity assumption), the structural contractor (whose schedule determines when the electrical scope can start), the consultant (whose quality determines the project's variation order pattern), the sales agent (whose commission structure determines the project's net sales realization).

### Mind 3: Liu Bei — relationship and reputation

**Why Liu Bei**: The partnership is a 30-36 month relationship with a developer who will be the user's reference for future development partnerships. Liu Bei's question: "Is this developer the right partner, and what does the user's behavior in this partnership signal to the market?"

**What Liu Bei sees**:
- The developer's track record is mixed: 12 units delivered on time (2021), 28 units delivered 9 months late (2023), 36 units in delivery with reported variations (2024). The mixed track record is not a disqualifier (most developers have mixed records), but it is a yellow flag. The user should investigate the causes of the delays — were they the developer's fault (poor cost control, scope changes, payment disputes with contractors) or external (regulatory delays, market downturns)?
- The relationship structure: the user is a 30% minority with veto rights but no operating authority. The user is a junior partner. The relationship is asymmetric. The user must accept the asymmetry or not enter. The asymmetry is acceptable if the developer treats the user as a respected junior partner (consults before material decisions, shares information promptly, honors the veto). The asymmetry is unacceptable if the developer treats the user as a discount electrical contractor (consults after decisions, shares information late, overrides the veto).
- The market signal: if the user enters the partnership and the project succeeds, the user is positioned as a contractor-developer in the market — which is the user's Stage 5 strategic objective. If the user enters and the project fails or stalls, the user is positioned as a contractor who over-reached, which damages the electrical contracting reputation. The partnership is a market signal regardless of outcome.

### Mind 4 (audit mind): Chen Gong — honest counsel about the principal

**Why Chen Gong**: The user, contemplating a partnership, is contemplating the choice of a principal. Chen Gong's worldview (see `07_MINDS/chen_gong/worldview.md`) is that the choice of principal is the most consequential decision an advisor can make, and the choice binds the advisor to the principal's fate. The user, entering a partnership, is binding himself to the developer's fate. Chen Gong's audit question: "Is this developer the principal the user can bind to?"

**What Chen Gong sees**:
- The developer's character: the user has met the developer three times. The user has not seen the developer under stress (a payment dispute, a regulatory delay, a cost overrun). The user does not know the developer's character; the user knows the developer's pitch.
- The principal's prior partners: the developer's prior electrical contractor on the 28-unit project (2023) — was that contractor retained for the 36-unit project (2024)? If not, why not? If the developer has cycled through electrical contractors, the user is being invited to be the next cycle. The user should investigate the developer's contractor relationships.
- The principled question: Chen Gong's worldview is that the choice of principal is a moral act, not a transactional one. The user is choosing to bind his reputation to the developer's reputation. If the developer's reputation is questionable (the user should verify through the Federation of Contractors' informal channel), the user is binding his reputation to a questionable reputation. The principled choice is to bind only to a principal whose character the user can defend.

## Hypothesis generation — 5 hypotheses

Per `00_META/multi_hypothesis_engine.md`, the system generates 5 hypotheses for a high-stakes question.

### Hypothesis 1 (from Sima Yi): "Accept the partnership with a restructured governance — demand a learning role, not just a 30% equity stake"

**The hypothesis**: Accept the partnership, but restructure the governance to include an explicit learning role for the user. The user's board seat comes with a right to observe the developer's land acquisition, financing, sales, and marketing decisions; the user's 30% equity comes with a right to participate in the developer's project management meetings; the user's veto on material decisions is preserved. The user is not just a 30% equity contributor; the user is a 30% partner who learns the development business by participation.

**Assumptions**:
- The developer will accept the restructured governance (the developer's current offer does not include an explicit learning role).
- The user has the time to participate in the developer's decisions (the user's current pipeline and the partnership electrical scope already consume most of the user's time).
- The learning role produces real development capability in 24-36 months (the user can apply the learning to the user's first solo development in Stage 5+).

**Failure modes**:
- The developer rejects the restructured governance (the developer wanted a discount electrical contractor, not a learning partner).
- The developer accepts the governance but does not honor it (the user observes but does not participate; the veto is overridden informally).
- The learning role produces observation but not capability (the user watches the development business without doing it).

### Hypothesis 2 (from Cao Cao): "Accept the partnership with a restructured cash flow — demand interim distributions tied to electrical scope milestones, not only profit at completion"

**The hypothesis**: Accept the partnership, but restructure the cash flow so the user is not financing EGP 9-11M of electrical works at his own cost over 18 months with no interim cash receipt. The restructure: the user's electrical scope is compensated partially in cash (EGP 4-5M, the materials and direct labor cost) at standard milestone payments, and the remaining EGP 4-6M (the user's margin plus the equity contribution) is converted to the 30% equity. This converts the user's exposure from a 24-month financing gap to a 6-month financing gap (the user's cash exposure is the equity portion, not the full electrical scope).

**Assumptions**:
- The developer will accept the restructure (the developer's current offer assumes the user finances the full electrical scope at his own cost; the restructure shifts the financing burden to the developer's project cash flow).
- The developer's project cash flow can absorb the EGP 4-5M interim payments to the user (the developer's unit sales must generate the cash; if sales are slow, the developer may not be able to pay).
- The user's margin on the electrical scope is preserved in the equity conversion (the user is not double-counting — the cash payment covers cost, the equity covers margin).

**Failure modes**:
- The developer rejects the restructure (the developer wanted the user to finance the electrical scope; the restructure defeats the developer's purpose).
- The developer accepts the restructure but delays the interim payments (the user is back to the original cash flow problem, with a smaller exposure).
- The restructure converts the partnership from a clean equity deal to a hybrid that the developer's accountant and the user's accountant struggle to track.

### Hypothesis 3 (from Liu Bei): "Decline the 30% equity; counter with a fixed-fee electrical contract with a small success bonus — preserve the relationship, exit the equity exposure"

**The hypothesis**: Decline the 30% equity offer. Counter with a fixed-fee electrical contract at a fair margin (15-18%) plus a success bonus of 2-3% of the developer's profit, paid at project completion. This preserves the relationship (the user is still the developer's electrical contractor for this project, building the relationship for the next project) and exits the equity exposure (the user is not bound to the project's profit, the developer's management quality, or the 30-month timeline). The success bonus is a token of the partnership intent without the equity commitment.

**Assumptions**:
- The developer will accept the counter (the developer wanted a partner; the counter is a contractor with a bonus, which is a different offer).
- The user's Stage 5 expansion can wait (the user does not need this specific partnership to enter development; the user can enter development through a different path — solo, with a different partner, or via a smaller first project).
- The relationship survives the counter (the developer may take the counter as a rejection of the partnership and exclude the user from the next offer).

**Failure modes**:
- The developer rejects the counter and walks (the user has spent the relationship capital on the counter and gets nothing).
- The developer accepts the counter but excludes the user from the next partnership offer (the user has the fixed-fee contract but no future development path with this developer).
- The user loses the learning opportunity (the fixed-fee contract does not include a learning role; the user remains a contractor).

### Hypothesis 4 (from Sima Yi + Cao Cao): "Decline this partnership; wait for the user's own first solo development in 12-18 months via mudarabah"

**The hypothesis**: Decline the partnership. Use the next 12-18 months to (a) maintain the electrical contracting pipeline and working capital, (b) build the user's first solo development capability via a mudarabah structure (per `09_BUSINESS/halal_business/mudarabah_partnership.md`) with a capital provider who provides the cash and the user provides the development management — a smaller first project (8-12 units) in which the user is the managing partner, not the junior partner. The user enters development as a principal, not as a junior partner.

**Assumptions**:
- The user can find a capital provider for a mudarabah in 12-18 months (the user has not verified this; the capital provider search is a real task).
- The user can develop the development management capability in 12-18 months (the user has electrical and construction experience but no development management experience — the capability gap is real).
- The market for an 8-12 unit first project in 2027-2028 is favorable (the Egyptian real estate market in 2027-2028 is uncertain; the user must do the market analysis).

**Failure modes**:
- The capital provider is not found in 12-18 months (the user has declined this partnership and has no development path).
- The capability gap is larger than the user can close in 12-18 months (the user enters a solo development without sufficient capability and fails).
- The market in 2027-2028 is unfavorable for a small first project (the user enters at the wrong cycle).

### Hypothesis 5 (from Chen Gong): "Decline the partnership on principled grounds — the developer's character is not yet verified"

**The hypothesis**: Decline the partnership. The reason: the user has not verified the developer's character under stress, and Chen Gong's worldview is that the choice of principal is a moral act that requires character verification. The user spends the next 3-6 months building the relationship with the developer — observing the developer's behavior on the developer's current 36-unit New Cairo project, talking to the developer's prior contractors, attending the developer's sales launches — and re-evaluates the partnership in 6 months with verified character information.

**Assumptions**:
- The developer will tolerate a 6-month delay (the developer has stated other partners are being evaluated; a 6-month delay may close the opportunity).
- The user has the discipline to do the character verification (the user is excited about the partnership; the discipline of waiting 6 months is hard).
- The character verification produces a definitive answer in 6 months (it may not — some character questions require years to answer).

**Failure modes**:
- The developer moves on to another partner in 30-60 days (the user has spent the time and lost the opportunity).
- The character verification is inconclusive (the user has spent 6 months and has no clearer answer).
- The user's patience runs out (the user accepts the partnership after 6 months of waiting, with no better information than at the start).

## The analysis (per 03_ANALYSIS)

### Power dynamics

The developer has more power than the user. The developer has the land, the license, the development management capability, and the choice of partner. The user has the electrical scope (which the developer could buy from another contractor for cash) and the bid (which the developer can take or leave). The user's 30% equity is a minority stake with veto rights; the user's power is defensive (block material changes), not offensive (initiate changes).

The user's power is enhanced by the developer's need for an in-kind electrical partner (the developer is conserving cash; the developer wants the user to finance the electrical scope). The developer's need is the user's leverage — but the leverage is only useful if the user is willing to walk away. The user's first instinct ("this is the opportunity") signals that the user is not willing to walk away, which eliminates the leverage.

### Interests behind positions

The developer's stated position: "We offer 30% equity for the contractor's electrical contribution." The developer's interest: conserve cash by receiving the electrical scope as in-kind equity; lock in a contractor who has incentive to deliver on quality and schedule (the contractor's equity is at risk); build a track record of contractor partnerships for future projects. The interest behind the position is cash conservation and contractor alignment.

The user's stated position (first instinct): "I want to enter real estate development." The user's interest: build development capability, build a development track record, build a development income stream distinct from electrical contracting. The interest behind the position is capability and diversification.

The intersection: the developer's interest (cash conservation, contractor alignment) and the user's interest (capability, diversification) are not perfectly aligned. The developer wants a discount electrical contractor with a paper upside; the user wants a learning partner with a real upside. The asymmetry is in the developer's favor unless the user restructures the deal (Hypothesis 1 or 2) or declines (Hypothesis 3, 4, or 5).

### Leverage detection

The user's leverage points:
- The developer's need for an in-kind partner (the developer is conserving cash; the developer's alternatives are a cash contractor or a different in-kind partner — both are less attractive to the developer than the user).
- The user's credible Stage 5 alternative (the user can enter development via mudarabah with a different capital provider; the developer is not the only path).
- The user's reputation (the developer wants a contractor with a strong reputation for quality and schedule; the user has that reputation).

The user's leverage gaps:
- The user's first instinct has already signaled a low willingness to walk away (the user must consciously re-establish the willingness to walk before negotiating).
- The user has no prior development partnership experience (the user cannot credibly claim development management capability, which limits the user's negotiating position on the governance restructure).
- The user has not verified the developer's character or track record in depth (the user is negotiating with incomplete information).

## The recommendation

**Recommendation: Hypothesis 2 — accept the partnership with a restructured cash flow (the user's electrical scope is compensated partially in cash at standard milestones, with the margin and equity contribution converted to the 30% equity). Reject the developer's original cash flow structure (full in-kind contribution). If the developer rejects the restructure, fall back to Hypothesis 3 (fixed-fee with success bonus) and use the next 12 months to prepare for Hypothesis 4 (solo development via mudarabah).**

**Confidence: MEDIUM (6 of 12 on the `00_META/confidence_scoring.md` scale).**

**Why MEDIUM, not HIGH**:
- The restructure's acceptance by the developer is uncertain (the developer's interest is cash conservation; the restructure reduces the cash conservation).
- The user's working capital sufficiency under the restructure is verified for normal scenarios but not for stress scenarios (a 30-day payment delay on the interim cash payments would still stress the user's working capital).
- The developer's character verification is incomplete (Chen Gong's audit is partially answered; the user has not spoken to the developer's prior contractors).

**Why MEDIUM, not LOW**:
- The structure of the recommendation (restructure the cash flow, fall back to fixed-fee, prepare for solo) is robust to most unknowns. If the developer rejects the restructure, the user has a clean fallback. If the developer accepts, the user's exposure is reduced from EGP 9-11M to EGP 4-5M.
- The strategic fit is verified — the partnership, if restructured, advances the user's Stage 5 expansion by giving the user equity participation and learning exposure, without consuming the user's entire working capital.

**Counter-evidence (the case against the recommendation)**:
- The restructure may be rejected by the developer (the developer's interest is the user's cash conservation; the restructure shifts the burden to the developer's cash flow). The user must be prepared for the rejection.
- The fallback (fixed-fee with bonus) is a fine outcome but does not advance the Stage 5 expansion; the user remains an electrical contractor for this developer. The user must be prepared to spend the next 12 months building the mudarabah path (Hypothesis 4) in parallel.
- The developer's character (Chen Gong's audit) is not fully verified under the restructure path. The user is still binding his reputation to the developer's reputation. The user should run the character verification in parallel with the restructure negotiation, not after.

**The conditional path**:

- **Days 1-7 (investigation)**: Verify the developer's track record in depth. Talk to the developer's prior electrical contractor (the contractor on the 28-unit 2023 project — was he retained for the 36-unit 2024 project? If not, why not?). Talk to the developer's bank (does the developer have bank financing on the land? If yes, what is the seniority of the user's equity in a default?). Talk to the consultant on the 36-unit project (the developer's behavior under the scope variations — was it the developer's fault or the consultant's?).
- **Days 8-15 (modeling)**: Model the user's working capital cycle under three scenarios: (a) original offer (full in-kind), (b) Hypothesis 2 restructure (partial cash, partial equity), (c) Hypothesis 3 fallback (full cash, no equity). Identify the binding constraint in each scenario. If scenario (b) leaves the user with less than EGP 800K of working capital buffer at any point in the 18 months, the restructure is insufficient — fall back to scenario (c).
- **Days 16-25 (negotiation)**: Present the restructure to the developer. The framing: "We are excited about the partnership. The 30% equity is the right structure for the long-term relationship. The cash flow structure needs adjustment so we can deliver the electrical scope at the quality the project requires without compromising our existing pipeline. We propose: EGP 4.5M cash at standard milestone payments (materials and direct labor), EGP 4-5M converted to the 30% equity (our margin and our equity contribution)." The developer's response is the negotiation's signal.
- **Days 26-30 (decision)**: If the developer accepts, sign the restructured term sheet. If the developer rejects, present the fallback (fixed-fee with bonus). If the developer rejects the fallback, walk away and begin the mudarabah preparation (Hypothesis 4) within 30 days.

## The follow-up — what to monitor, what to do if conditions change

### If the restructured partnership is signed

- **Month 1-3**: Mobilize the user's electrical scope. Verify the developer's interim payment schedule is honored (the EGP 4.5M cash portion). Monitor the developer's sales velocity (are units selling at the projected pace?). If the sales velocity is below 60% of projection by month 6, the project's profit is at risk, and the user's equity is at risk.
- **Month 4-9**: Monitor the developer's cost control (is the project on budget? Are there scope variations being initiated by the developer?). The user has veto rights on material changes; the user should exercise the veto if the variations erode the project's profit (and the user's equity). The veto is the user's only offensive power; the user must be willing to use it.
- **Month 10-18**: Monitor the developer's schedule discipline (is the project on schedule?). If the project is slipping by more than 3 months by month 12, the user should engage the developer in a frank discussion about the recovery plan. The user is a 30% partner; the user has the right and the responsibility to participate in the recovery discussion.
- **Month 18-24**: Project delivery. Monitor the unit handover. Monitor the sales completion. Monitor the developer's profit calculation (the user should hire an independent accountant to verify the project's profit at completion; the user's 30% equity depends on the profit calculation, and the developer's accountant is not the user's accountant).
- **Month 24-36**: Profit distribution. Retention period. Exit decision (cash distribution vs roll into the next SPV). The user's exit decision should be made at month 18 (when the profit projection is firming), not at month 30 (when the developer is pressuring the user to roll).

### If the fallback (fixed-fee with bonus) is signed

- The user is the developer's electrical contractor for this project. The execution follows the standard bid pipeline (see `15_USE_CASES/examples/worked_example_bidding.md`). The success bonus is a small upside, not a strategic asset. The strategic value is the relationship for the next project, which the user should cultivate through excellent execution.

### If the partnership is declined

- The user has 12 months to prepare the mudarabah path (Hypothesis 4). The preparation has four tracks: (a) capital provider search (3-6 months — the user's network, the Federation of Contractors' capital contacts, the Islamic finance institutions in Cairo); (b) development management capability (the user should hire or partner with a development manager for the first solo project, since the user's capability gap is real); (c) market analysis (the user should identify 2-3 candidate sites for an 8-12 unit first project, with realistic acquisition costs and sales projections); (d) the legal structure (the user should engage a lawyer familiar with mudarabah structures to draft the first SPV agreement).

### If conditions change

- **If the developer's 36-unit New Cairo project (in delivery) stalls or fails**: Re-evaluate the partnership. The developer's track record is now confirmed as poor; the user should walk away from the partnership even if the restructure is signed (subject to the partnership agreement's exit clauses).
- **If the Egyptian real estate market turns sharply down in 2027 (currency, demand, mortgage rates)**: Re-evaluate the project's profit projection. The user's 30% equity is at risk in a market downturn; the user should consider whether the restructure includes a downside protection (a minimum return guarantee from the developer — unlikely but worth asking).
- **If the user's working capital drops below EGP 1.5M before signing**: Decline the partnership. The user's existing pipeline and family reserves take priority.

## The post-decision audit — what went right, what went wrong

### The decision audit (at signing, day 30)

1. **Did the system apply the partnership audit framework?** Yes — the cash flow audit, the governance audit, the character audit, the strategic-fit audit.
2. **Did the user anchor on the first instinct?** No — the user's first instinct was "say yes." The system's audit rejected the instinct and walked through the framework.
3. **Did the user generate multiple hypotheses?** Yes — five hypotheses from four minds.
4. **Did the user verify the premises?** Partially — the developer's track record was investigated; the developer's bank financing was checked; the developer's prior contractor relationships were probed. The capital provider availability for the mudarabah fallback was not verified (the user did not start that search).
5. **Did the user push back on the system's recommendation?** The user should: "Why Hypothesis 2 and not Hypothesis 4 (decline and prepare for solo)?" The system's response: Hypothesis 2 captures the learning value and the equity upside if the developer accepts the restructure, with a clean fallback (Hypothesis 3) if the developer rejects. Hypothesis 4 is the right path if the user has high confidence in the mudarabah option — but the user has not verified that option, so committing to Hypothesis 4 without verification is a different kind of leap.
6. **Did the user ask the meta-question?** The user should ask: "What should I be asking that I am not?" The system's likely response: "The user has not asked about the tax treatment of the in-kind contribution — the user's electrical works are performed at the user's cost, but the equity is received as a non-cash contribution. The Egyptian tax treatment of in-kind equity contributions (capital gains at distribution, vs income tax on the electrical scope's margin) needs explicit analysis by the user's accountant before signing."

### The execution audit (at project completion, month 24-30)

The audit questions:

1. **Did the restructured cash flow hold?** Did the developer honor the EGP 4.5M cash payments on schedule, or did the developer delay? If delayed, by how much, and how did the delay affect the user's working capital cycle?
2. **Did the user's learning role materialize?** Did the user observe and participate in the developer's decisions, or did the developer exclude the user from operational decisions? If the learning role did not materialize, the user's Stage 5 capability has not advanced — the partnership was a financial exposure without a learning return.
3. **Did the project's profit hold?** Did the developer's profit projection of EGP 60-80M materialize, or did cost overruns and sales shortfalls erode the profit? The user's 30% equity depends on the actual profit, not the projection.
4. **Did the user's relationship with the developer strengthen or weaken?** Did the developer respect the user's veto, share information promptly, treat the user as a respected junior partner? Or did the developer override the veto informally, share information late, treat the user as a discount electrical contractor?
5. **Did the user's market reputation improve?** Did the user exit the partnership positioned as a contractor-developer in the market, or as a contractor who over-reached?

The execution audit's output updates the user's partnership decision framework for the next partnership — the partnership after the first partnership, which is the partnership that confirms the user's Stage 5 expansion.

## Closing note

The partnership decision is a decision about who the user becomes, not just what the user does. The user who signs the developer's original offer becomes a discount electrical contractor with a paper equity stake. The user who restructures the offer becomes a contractor-developer with a real equity stake and a learning role. The user who declines becomes a contractor who chose to enter development as a principal in his own time. The system does not pick the path; the system clarifies the paths so the user can pick the path that fits who the user wants to become. That is the partnership audit's deepest function.
