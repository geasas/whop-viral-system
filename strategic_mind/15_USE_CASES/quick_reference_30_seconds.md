## Why this file exists

The full system is a 7-step pipeline (diagnosis → mind routing → hypothesis → knowledge retrieval → audit → delivery → follow-up) that takes 5-30 minutes to run for a non-trivial decision. The contractor often does not have 5-30 minutes. The contractor has 30 seconds — on site, on the phone, in a meeting — to decide whether to act, defer, or escalate. This file is the 30-second version of the pipeline: a one-page reference the contractor can keep on his phone, glance at, and apply in the moment.

This file is not a substitute for the full pipeline. It is a triage tool. The 30-second framework tells the contractor whether the decision is (a) safe to make in 30 seconds, (b) safe to defer to a 5-minute pipeline later in the day, or (c) requires the full pipeline before any action. The contractor who uses the 30-second framework to triage his decisions makes better decisions in the moment and reserves the full pipeline for the decisions that deserve it.

## The 30-second decision framework

The framework is a 4-step sequence the contractor runs mentally in 30 seconds:

### Step 1 — Diagnose the situation type (5 seconds)

Ask: **"What kind of decision is this?"**

The five situation types:

| Type | Description | Default mind | Default action |
|------|-------------|--------------|----------------|
| **Strategic** | Affects the firm's 1-5 year position (a bid, a partnership, an expansion, a hire) | Sima Yi or Cao Cao | Defer to full pipeline (do not act in 30 seconds) |
| **Tactical** | Affects the current project's execution (a variation order, a subcontractor issue, a material substitution) | Guo Jia | Act in 30 seconds if reversible; defer if not |
| **Relational** | Affects a client, partner, or crew relationship (a conflict, a request, a refusal) | Liu Bei | Defer to a structured conversation; do not act in 30 seconds |
| **Ethical** | Affects the firm's halal compliance or integrity (a riba question, a deception temptation, a quality compromise) | Chen Gong (audit) | Never act in 30 seconds; the ethical decision requires the full pipeline |
| **Emergency** | Affects safety, cash, or contract breach (a site accident, a payment default, a deadline miss) | Lu Bu or Guo Jia | Act in 30 seconds if the cost of waiting exceeds the cost of acting imperfectly |

The single most important triage question: **"Is this decision reversible in 24 hours?"**

- If YES (reversible in 24 hours): act in 30 seconds if the situation type permits (Tactical, Emergency).
- If NO (not reversible in 24 hours): defer to the full pipeline. Do not act in 30 seconds, regardless of pressure.

### Step 2 — Invoke the default mind (5 seconds)

Ask: **"Which mind does this situation type default to?"**

The five default minds (one per situation type):

- **Strategic** → **Sima Yi** (patience; the long-game lens). The strategic question is almost always "what does the long game dictate?" — and Sima Yi is the mind that answers it.
- **Tactical** → **Guo Jia** (rapid situation reading; the fast-decision lens). The tactical question is "what does the situation reading suggest, and what is the calculated risk?" — and Guo Jia is the mind that answers it.
- **Relational** → **Liu Bei** (heart and legitimacy; the relationship lens). The relational question is "what does the relationship's long-term health dictate?" — and Liu Bei is the mind that answers it.
- **Ethical** → **Chen Gong** (honest counsel about the principal; the integrity lens). The ethical question is "what does the user's integrity require?" — and Chen Gong is the mind that audits the answer.
- **Emergency** → **Lu Bu** (direct action; the force lens) for clear-action emergencies, **Guo Jia** (rapid reading; the calculated risk lens) for ambiguous emergencies.

### Step 3 — Generate one alternative hypothesis (10 seconds)

Ask: **"What is the opposite of my first instinct?"**

This is the multi-hypothesis engine in 10 seconds. The full pipeline generates 3-5 hypotheses; the 30-second pipeline generates one — the opposite of the first instinct. The opposite hypothesis is the antidote to the anchoring bias.

Examples:
- First instinct: "Take the variation order at the developer's request." Opposite: "Decline the variation order; the schedule impact exceeds the price."
- First instinct: "Confront the subcontractor about the delay." Opposite: "Ask the subcontractor about the cause; the delay may not be the subcontractor's fault."
- First instinct: "Accept the late payment; the developer will pay next month." Opposite: "Send the formal notice today; the late payment is the developer's choice, not the user's concession."
- First instinct: "Pull the crew off the slow project to the urgent one." Opposite: "Keep the crew on the slow project; the urgent project's delay is recoverable, the slow project's quality loss is not."

If the opposite hypothesis is more defensible than the first instinct, switch. If the first instinct survives the opposite hypothesis, act on it (with the confidence that the hypothesis was tested, not assumed).

### Step 4 — Apply the confidence test (10 seconds)

Ask: **"What confidence do I have in this decision — LOW, MEDIUM, or HIGH?"**

- **LOW**: Treat as hypothesis. Do not act without verification. State to the counterparty: "I will get back to you in [time]." Do not commit in the moment.
- **MEDIUM**: Act with caution. State to the counterparty: "I will do this, with the following condition: [condition]." Act, but build in the reversal.
- **HIGH**: Act. Commit. Do not equivocate.

The single rule: **never act on LOW confidence in 30 seconds.** If the confidence is LOW, the decision requires more time. The contractor who acts on LOW confidence in 30 seconds is the contractor who makes decisions he regrets.

## The 5 most common decision types and their default mind

The contractor's 5 most common decision types, with the default mind and the default 30-second action:

### Decision type 1: "Should I accept this variation order?"

- **Default mind**: Guo Jia (rapid situation reading) for the tactical decision; Sima Yi (long-game) for the strategic implication.
- **30-second action**: If the variation is small (under EGP 50K), reversible, and within scope — accept with a written confirmation. If the variation is large (over EGP 50K), affects schedule, or expands scope — defer. "I need to review the schedule and cost impact; I will respond by [time]."

### Decision type 2: "Should I commit my crew to this urgent task?"

- **Default mind**: Guo Jia (calculated risk).
- **30-second action**: If the urgent task is safety-related — commit immediately, no calculation. If the urgent task is schedule-related — calculate the crew's current commitment; if pulling the crew compromises an active project, defer. "I will send a crew in [time]; the current project's critical task will complete first."

### Decision type 3: "Should I extend payment terms to this client?"

- **Default mind**: Sima Yi (long-game) for the relationship; Cao Cao (multi-stakeholder) for the cash flow.
- **30-second action**: Never extend in 30 seconds. The cash flow implication requires modeling. "I cannot extend the terms without checking my cash flow; I will respond by [time]."

### Decision type 4: "Should I confront this partner/subcontractor about [issue]?"

- **Default mind**: Liu Bei (relationship) for the framing; Chen Gong (integrity) for the content.
- **30-second action**: Never confront in 30 seconds. Emotional reactivity destroys the conversation. "I want to discuss [issue] with you; can we meet [time]?" Defer the content to the structured conversation.

### Decision type 5: "Should I bid on this project?"

- **Default mind**: Sima Yi (long-game) for the strategic fit; Guo Jia (rapid reading) for the competitive landscape.
- **30-second action**: Never commit to bid in 30 seconds. "I need to run the bid decision framework; I will let you know by [time]." Apply the six filters from `09_BUSINESS/contracting/project_bidding.md` before any commitment.

## The 3 questions to ask before any urgent decision

These three questions, asked in 5 seconds each, are the contractor's protection against the worst 30-second decisions:

### Question 1: "What is the worst that happens if I do nothing?"

If the worst outcome of doing nothing is acceptable (no safety risk, no contract breach, no cash flow crisis, no relationship damage), the decision is not truly urgent — defer to the full pipeline.

If the worst outcome of doing nothing is unacceptable (safety risk, contract breach, cash flow crisis, relationship damage), the decision is genuinely urgent — proceed with the 30-second framework.

### Question 2: "What is the worst that happens if I act on my first instinct?"

If the worst outcome of acting on the first instinct is acceptable (the decision is reversible, the cost of being wrong is low), act.

If the worst outcome of acting on the first instinct is unacceptable (the decision is irreversible, the cost of being wrong is high), do not act on the first instinct. Generate the opposite hypothesis (Step 3 of the framework).

### Question 3: "What is the reversible option?"

If a reversible option exists (a partial commitment, a conditional commitment, a deferred commitment with a deadline), take the reversible option. The reversible option preserves the user's optionality; the irreversible option eliminates it.

If no reversible option exists (the decision is binary, time-bounded, and irreversible), apply the full 30-second framework with extra care — and consider whether the decision is truly urgent or whether the urgency is manufactured.

## The "do not act" list — when to slow down even when urgent

The contractor must learn to recognize the situations in which the urgency is a trap, not a real constraint. The "do not act" list is the list of situations in which the contractor must slow down, regardless of the perceived urgency:

### Do not act in 30 seconds when:

1. **The decision is irreversible and the cost of being wrong is high.** Examples: signing a contract, hiring an employee, firing an employee, terminating a subcontract, committing working capital to a project, accepting a partnership offer. These decisions affect the firm's 1-5 year position; the 30-second framework cannot process them.

2. **The urgency is being manufactured by the counterparty.** Examples: a developer pressuring a bid decision in 24 hours ("the offer expires tomorrow"), a partner pressuring a partnership agreement signature ("other partners are interested"), a supplier pressuring a material purchase ("the price goes up next week"). Manufactured urgency is a negotiation tactic, not a real constraint. The contractor who acts on manufactured urgency is the contractor who pays the urgency premium.

3. **The decision involves an ethical question.** Examples: a riba-adjacent financing structure, a deception-adjacent contract clause, a quality-compromise request from a client. Ethical decisions require the full pipeline (Chen Gong's audit mind); the 30-second framework cannot process the ethical nuance.

4. **The decision involves a relationship under stress.** Examples: a partner conflict, a client dispute, a crew disagreement. Relational decisions require a structured conversation (Liu Bei's framing); the 30-second framework cannot process the relational dynamics.

5. **The decision is being made under emotional reactivity.** Examples: a decision made in anger (after a partner's betrayal), a decision made in fear (after a payment default), a decision made in excitement (after a major bid win). Emotional reactivity biases the decision; the contractor must cool before deciding.

6. **The decision is a strategic expansion or contraction.** Examples: entering a new market, exiting a market, adding a service, dropping a service. Strategic decisions require the multi-year horizon (Sima Yi's lens); the 30-second framework cannot process the multi-year implications.

### When the contractor recognizes a "do not act" situation:

The 30-second response is: **"I need to think about this. I will respond by [specific time]."**

This response is the contractor's protection. It is not a refusal; it is a deferral. The deferral is the contractor's right; the counterparty who refuses to accept the deferral is the counterparty who is manufacturing the urgency.

## The Egyptian contractor's specific urgent situations

The four signature urgent situations the Egyptian contractor faces, with the 30-second framework applied to each:

### Urgent situation 1: The late payment demand

**The situation**: A subcontractor or supplier calls the contractor at 9 AM demanding payment on a 60-day overdue invoice, threatening to walk off the site or stop material supply by end of day.

**The 30-second framework**:

- **Situation type**: Tactical (with Relational and Cash Flow dimensions).
- **Default mind**: Guo Jia (rapid reading) — what is the subcontractor's actual pressure? Is the walk-off a real threat or a negotiation tactic?
- **The opposite hypothesis**: First instinct — "Pay them today to keep the site running." Opposite — "Do not pay today; the subcontractor has more to lose from a walk-off than the contractor; call the bluff and offer a partial payment with a schedule."
- **The confidence test**: If the subcontractor's walk-off threat is credible (the subcontractor has done this before, the subcontractor's crew is essential), MEDIUM confidence on the partial payment. If the threat is a negotiation tactic (the subcontractor is replaceable, the project can absorb a 2-3 day delay), HIGH confidence on the deferral.
- **The 30-second action**: "I hear the urgency. I cannot pay the full amount today; I can pay EGP [partial amount] today and the balance by [date]. Is that acceptable?" If the subcontractor accepts, the situation is defused. If the subcontractor refuses and walks off, the contractor has the partial payment as evidence of good faith and the balance as the negotiation lever.

### Urgent situation 2: The bid deadline

**The situation**: A developer's tender closes in 36 hours; the contractor has not started the bid preparation; the developer calls to confirm the contractor will submit.

**The 30-second framework**:

- **Situation type**: Strategic (a bid decision is a strategic decision, not a tactical one).
- **Default mind**: Sima Yi (long-game) — is this bid the right strategic move, or is the deadline pressure manufacturing urgency?
- **The opposite hypothesis**: First instinct — "I have to bid; I will work through the night to prepare." Opposite — "I will not bid; the 36-hour window means I cannot prepare a credible technical proposal; the bid will be sloppy and will damage my reputation with the developer regardless of whether I win."
- **The confidence test**: If the contractor has been preparing the bid and the 36 hours is the final push, MEDIUM confidence on the submission. If the contractor has not started the bid preparation, HIGH confidence on the decline (the bid cannot be credible in 36 hours from zero).
- **The 30-second action**: "I cannot submit a credible bid in 36 hours; I have not prepared. I would welcome a direct conversation about your next project, when I can prepare properly." The decline preserves the relationship (the developer respects the contractor who declines honestly over the contractor who submits sloppily) and the contractor's reputation.

### Urgent situation 3: The subcontractor failure

**The situation**: The contractor's mechanical subcontractor on a 40-unit project walks off the site at 7 AM, citing a payment dispute (the subcontractor claims the contractor is 30 days late on the last certify; the contractor's records show the certify is not yet due). The subcontractor's crew is essential to the project's current phase.

**The 30-second framework**:

- **Situation type**: Emergency (the project's current phase is blocked) with Relational dimension.
- **Default mind**: Lu Bu (direct action) for the immediate response; Liu Bei (relationship) for the subsequent conversation.
- **The opposite hypothesis**: First instinct — "Force the subcontractor back to the site with a contractual threat." Opposite — "Call the subcontractor's principal, ask for the actual issue (the payment dispute may be the symptom, not the cause; the subcontractor may have a cash flow crisis), and propose a partial payment plus a meeting to resolve the underlying issue."
- **The confidence test**: If the subcontractor's walk-off is a pattern (the subcontractor has done this before), MEDIUM confidence on the contractual threat (with legal counsel in parallel). If the walk-off is a first occurrence, HIGH confidence on the call-and-meet response (the relationship is worth preserving; the contractual threat destroys it).
- **The 30-second action**: Call the subcontractor's principal. "I hear your concern. I cannot pay the full amount today; let's meet at [time] today to resolve the underlying issue. In the meantime, send the crew back to the site; the project cannot absorb a stoppage." If the subcontractor agrees, the situation is stabilized. If the subcontractor refuses, escalate to legal counsel and a replacement subcontractor (the formal pipeline, not the 30-second pipeline).

### Urgent situation 4: The client dispute

**The situation**: A client calls the contractor at 5 PM furious about a quality issue on site (the finishes subcontractor's work is below the client's expectation; the client threatens to withhold the next progress payment and to write a negative review in the developer community).

**The 30-second framework**:

- **Situation type**: Relational (the relationship is under stress) with Tactical dimension.
- **Default mind**: Liu Bei (relationship) — what response preserves the relationship and the contractor's market reputation?
- **The opposite hypothesis**: First instinct — "Defend the quality; the finishes are within specification." Opposite — "Visit the site tomorrow morning, see the issue with the client, and acknowledge the issue before defending the specification. The client's perception of quality is the reality the contractor must address, regardless of the specification."
- **The confidence test**: If the contractor's specification defense is airtight and the client's expectation is unreasonable, MEDIUM confidence on the visit-and-discuss response (the defense comes after the visit, not before). If the finishes are below specification, HIGH confidence on the visit-and-acknowledge-and-fix response (the fix is the contractor's responsibility).
- **The 30-second action**: "I hear your concern. I will be on site tomorrow at [time] to see the issue with you. I will not argue the specification before I see the issue; I will see it first, then we will discuss the resolution." The deferral to the next morning is the contractor's protection — the 5 PM call cannot resolve the issue; the next morning's site visit can.

## Closing discipline — the 30-second habits

The contractor who uses the 30-second framework consistently develops three habits:

1. **The deferral habit**: The contractor learns to say "I will respond by [time]" instead of "yes" or "no" in the moment. The deferral is the contractor's most useful 30-second tool. The contractor who masters the deferral makes fewer bad decisions and more good decisions.

2. **The opposite-hypothesis habit**: The contractor learns to ask "what is the opposite of my first instinct?" before any 30-second decision. The opposite hypothesis is the contractor's protection against the anchoring bias. The contractor who masters the opposite hypothesis makes decisions that have been tested, not assumed.

3. **The confidence-stating habit**: The contractor learns to state his confidence (LOW, MEDIUM, HIGH) before acting. The confidence statement is the contractor's protection against the over-confidence trap. The contractor who masters the confidence statement acts on HIGH and MEDIUM with clarity, and defers LOW to the full pipeline.

The 30-second framework is not the full system. The full system is the 7-step pipeline applied to the strategic decisions. The 30-second framework is the triage tool that protects the contractor in the moments between the strategic decisions. The contractor who masters both — the full pipeline for the strategic decisions and the 30-second framework for the urgent decisions — is the contractor who makes fewer bad decisions and more good decisions, in every domain.
