# Company Building — From a Contracting Practice to a Company

> **Layer**: 09_BUSINESS/entrepreneurship/
> **File**: company_building
> **Subject**: Transforming a one-person contracting practice (or a small informal crew) into a formal Egyptian company capable of bidding on larger work, attracting halal capital, and surviving the founder's absence
> **User context**: Egyptian electrical contractor, halal-only, who has reached the inflection point where the practice is larger than what one person can manage by hand and must be structured as a company
> **Provenance**: Synthesized 2026-09-10 from sources across 4 tiers (Egyptian corporate law, classical fiqh on partnership contracts, modern management literature, practitioner interviews). Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH. The legal framework is current to the 2024 amendments of Egyptian Companies Law; the management principles are stable across decades; the specific tax and social insurance figures must be re-verified annually.

---

## The Core Premise

A contracting practice is not a company. A practice is a person — the contractor — performing work, with helpers, using the contractor's personal name, personal reputation, and personal bank account. The practice generates revenue, but the revenue is the contractor's personal income. The practice has no legal existence separate from the contractor. The practice's liabilities are the contractor's personal liabilities. The practice's contracts are the contractor's personal obligations. The practice ends when the contractor stops working or dies.

A company is a separate legal person. It has its own name, its own bank account, its own contracts, its own liabilities, its own tax identity, its own employees, its own contracts with those employees. The company continues to exist if the contractor is hospitalized for two months. The company can be inherited by the contractor's children (with the appropriate structure). The company can accept a partner without dissolving. The company can sue and be sued in its own name. The company's liabilities are bounded by the company's capital (in an LLC structure), not by the contractor's personal assets.

The transition from practice to company is not optional for the contractor who plans to grow. It is the structural decision that determines whether the contractor's 10-year arc ends with a business worth EGP 30 million (a real company with transferable value) or with a practice worth EGP 3 million (the contractor's personal tools, the contractor's personal relationships, and the contractor's personal reputation, all of which die with the contractor's working years).

This file is the operator's manual for that transition. It covers the legal structures available in Egypt, the tax implications of each, the accounting foundation that must precede the transition, the HR foundation (contracts, social insurance, payroll), the operational foundation (project tracking, invoicing, procurement), and the management foundation (decision-making, delegation, accountability). The Islamic dimension is woven throughout — the partnership contract in Islamic law is the foundation on which the modern LLC sits, and the management principles of classical Islamic governance (shura, accountability, justice in treatment of employees) are not decorative; they are the operational discipline that makes a company durable.

## Section 1: The Legal Structures

Egyptian corporate law (Law 159 of 1981, as amended through 2024) recognizes several structures relevant to a contracting business. Each has a specific use case, capital requirement, tax treatment, and liability profile.

### 1.1 Sole Proprietorship (منشأة فردية)

The simplest structure. The contractor registers under their own name with the commercial registry. No minimum capital. The contractor is personally liable for all obligations. Profits are taxed as personal income. The structure is appropriate for the first 12-24 months — the lean services stage described in `starting_broke.md` — when the contractor has no employees (only day-laborers), no fixed office, and is testing the market.

**Advantages**: zero setup cost (registration fees are nominal), minimal accounting requirements, full control.
**Disadvantages**: unlimited personal liability, no separation of business and personal assets, no ability to accept partners without converting, no credibility signal to large clients (large clients prefer LLCs).
**Transition trigger**: when the contractor hires the first employee on a fixed contract (rather than day-labor), or when the contractor wins the first contract above EGP 1M, or when the contractor wishes to add a partner — convert to an LLC.

### 1.2 Limited Liability Company (شركة ذات مسؤولية محدودة — LLC)

The dominant structure for Egyptian contracting businesses of small to medium scale. The LLC has 2-50 partners (a single-partner LLC is also permitted under the 2018 amendments, but multi-partner is more common for contracting). Minimum capital is not statutorily fixed in practice; the Companies Authority generally accepts EGP 10,000-50,000 as nominal capital for a small contracting LLC, though lenders and large clients will examine the actual paid-in capital. Partners' liability is limited to their capital contribution. The LLC is a separate tax person (subject to corporate income tax, currently 22.5% on net profit). Profits distributed to partners are taxed again as personal income on the partner's share (currently 10% dividend tax — verify with current tax counsel, as this has been adjusted multiple times).

The LLC is the structure of choice for the contractor's Stages 4-5 (years 4-10), because it (a) limits the contractor's personal liability to the company's capital, protecting personal assets; (b) allows the contractor to accept a partner (a halal musharaka-style partnership, see `halal_business/mudarabah_partnership.md`); (c) signals to large clients and to the Federation of Contractors that the contractor is a formal business; (d) enables the contractor to apply for higher federation grades; (e) provides a structure that can be inherited (with appropriate partnership agreement provisions).

**Advantages**: limited liability, separate legal person, can accept partners, higher credibility, can be inherited, can convert to joint-stock later.
**Disadvantages**: setup cost (legal fees, registration fees — typically EGP 15,000-30,000 total), ongoing accounting and audit requirements, corporate tax on profit, dividend tax on distributions, more complex decision-making (partners' meetings, formal resolutions).

### 1.3 Joint Stock Company (شركة مساهمة)

The structure for large-scale contracting and real estate development. Minimum capital for a private joint stock company is currently EGP 250,000 (verify current figure). Shares are transferable. The structure is appropriate for the contractor's expansion into real estate development at scale (years 8+), when the contractor may need to attract multiple investors or to list eventually (unlikely but possible). For the first 5-7 years, the joint stock structure is overkill and is not recommended; the LLC is more flexible and less costly.

**Advantages**: shares are transferable (important if the contractor plans to attract outside investors eventually), can scale to large capital, can list publicly.
**Disadvantages**: high setup cost (EGP 50,000+), complex governance (board of directors, general assembly, formal accounting), double taxation.

### 1.4 The Partnership Decision

The contractor's first major structural decision is whether to add a partner when forming the LLC. The Islamic and the practical answers converge here: a partner is appropriate if (a) the partner brings capital the contractor does not have, OR (b) the partner brings capability (technical, managerial, market access) the contractor does not have, OR (c) the partner brings a relationship network the contractor does not have. A partner who brings none of the three is a partner who adds cost without adding value. A partner who brings one or more of the three is a partner who can multiply the business.

The Islamic frame on partnership is the *musharaka* contract — a partnership in which two or more parties contribute capital and labor and share in profit and loss in agreed proportions. The *mudaraba* contract is a variant in which one party contributes capital and the other contributes labor (the contractor's situation if the contractor seeks a capital-only partner). Both are treated in detail in `halal_business/mudarabah_partnership.md` and `halal_business/islamic_commercial_fiqh.md`. The point here is that the LLC structure accommodates the Islamic partnership forms cleanly — the partners' agreement can be drafted to mirror the musharaka or mudaraba terms.

**Application to the contractor**: For Stages 1-3 (years 1-4), operate as a sole proprietorship. In Stage 4 (years 4-7), when the contractor adds the first formal employees and seeks the first partner, convert to an LLC. The partner should be either (a) a capital partner who contributes EGP 500,000-2,000,000 in exchange for 20-40% equity, with the contractor holding 60-80% as the working partner (this is a mudaraba-like structure if the partner's contribution is capital only), or (b) a capability partner (a senior general contractor who brings the general-contracting capability the contractor needs for the next stage) in exchange for 25-35% equity (this is a musharaka-like structure, with both partners contributing labor and possibly capital).

## Section 2: The Tax Implications in Egypt

Egyptian tax law for contracting businesses involves four main streams. The contractor must understand all four before structuring the company, because the structure determines the tax exposure.

### 2.1 Corporate Income Tax (ضريبة الشركات)

LLCs and joint stock companies pay corporate income tax on net profit. The current rate (as of 2024 amendments) is 22.5% for most companies, with a reduced rate of 1.5% of revenue for small businesses (those with revenue under EGP 10M — verify current threshold) under the simplified tax regime. For a contracting business at the contractor's Stage 4 scale (revenue EGP 10-30M), the standard rate applies. For the small business at Stage 2-3 (revenue under EGP 10M), the simplified 1.5% regime may apply, but the contractor should model both and choose the one that produces the lower tax (the simplified regime does not allow expense deductions; the standard regime does — for a high-expense business like contracting, the standard regime is usually lower).

The contractor must engage a CPA early — no later than Stage 3. The CPA's annual fee (EGP 15,000-50,000 for a small contracting LLC) is repaid many times in tax savings and in avoiding tax authority penalties.

### 2.2 Value Added Tax (ضريبة القيمة المضافة — VAT)

Egypt's VAT (Law 67 of 2016) applies to most goods and services at a standard rate of 14% (verify current rate — it has been adjusted). Construction services are generally taxable, with some exemptions for residential construction below a stated value threshold. The contractor registers for VAT when annual revenue exceeds the registration threshold (currently EGP 500,000 — verify). The contractor charges VAT on invoices, collects it from clients, and remits it to the Tax Authority monthly or quarterly. The contractor can credit VAT paid on inputs (materials, equipment) against VAT collected on outputs. For a contracting business, the VAT cash flow is significant: the contractor may collect EGP 500,000 of VAT in a quarter and owe EGP 200,000 to the Tax Authority after input credits. The cash must be held until remittance — DO NOT spend the VAT cash on operations. This is a common error and a common cause of contracting business failure.

### 2.3 Withholding Tax (الضريبة المحجوزة)

Clients (especially government clients and large private clients) are required to withhold a percentage of payments and remit it directly to the Tax Authority on the contractor's behalf. The current rate is 1% of contract value for resident contractors (verify current rate). The withholding is creditable against the contractor's annual corporate income tax. The contractor must account for withholding in cash flow planning — the contractor receives, say, 99% of invoice value, and the 1% is held until the annual tax filing.

### 2.4 Payroll Taxes and Social Insurance (التأمينات الاجتماعية)

The largest non-obvious cost of formal employment in Egypt. For each employee on the formal payroll, the employer contributes 11% of the employee's insured wage (capped at a maximum insured wage — currently around EGP 9,000-12,000/month — verify) to the Social Insurance Authority, and the employee contributes 14% (capped similarly). The employer's contribution is a direct cost of employment — adding roughly 11% to the wage bill above the gross wage. For the contractor, this means: an employee at EGP 15,000/month gross actually costs the company EGP 15,000 + EGP 1,650 (11% of the insured portion) + end-of-service indemnity accrual + paid leave accrual. The total cost of a formal employee is roughly 125-135% of the gross wage.

The contractor must decide, for each hire, whether to formalize (with social insurance, contracts, full benefits) or to engage as a day-laborer or subcontractor. The halal answer leans toward formalization: the Quran commands justice in treatment of workers ("and do not withhold from people what is rightfully theirs" — Al-A'raf 7:85, and the famous hadith "pay the worker his wage before his sweat dries" — Ibn Majah no. 2443, narrated by Abdullah ibn Umar, grade: hasan). Informal employment is not strictly haram, but formal employment better fulfills the Islamic obligation of just treatment of workers.

**Application to the contractor**: For Stages 1-3, day-laborers and a single junior electrician (paid informally or formally depending on cost-benefit). For Stage 4 onward, formalize the core team (project manager, senior electricians, accountant) and use subcontractors for variable labor. The cost of formalization (social insurance + accounting) is offset by the credibility, retention, and risk reduction.

## Section 3: The Accounting Foundation

A contracting business cannot be run on a checkbook. It must be run on a proper accounting system. The accounting foundation has five components:

### 3.1 The Chart of Accounts

A standard chart of accounts for a contracting business has six sections: assets (cash, receivables, inventory, equipment, work-in-progress); liabilities (payables, advances from clients, accrued expenses, equity); equity (paid-in capital, retained earnings, current-period profit); revenue (by service line — residential electrical, commercial electrical, general contracting, development); cost of sales (by project — materials, labor, subcontractor, equipment); and operating expenses (salaries, rent, utilities, marketing, travel, professional fees).

The chart must be set up by a CPA at company formation. The chart is not creative work; it follows the Egyptian Accounting Standards (which are aligned with IFRS for SMEs). The contractor's job is to use the chart, not to design it.

### 3.2 The Project Ledger

The single most important accounting document for a contracting business is the project ledger — a record, for each project, of all costs (materials, labor, subcontractors, equipment, overhead allocation) and all revenue (contract value, variations, advances collected). The project ledger shows, at any moment, whether the project is profitable or losing money. Without a project ledger, the contractor is flying blind: the business may show overall profit on the income statement while losing money on every individual project (because the profitable projects are subsidizing the loss-making ones, and the contractor cannot tell which is which).

The project ledger is updated weekly (at minimum) by the accountant. The contractor reviews it weekly. A project that shows a -10% margin at week 3 of a 10-week project is a project the contractor can save (by renegotiating scope, by changing the labor mix, by escalating to the client). A project that shows a -10% margin at week 9 cannot be saved. The project ledger is the contractor's early warning system.

### 3.3 The Cash Flow Forecast

Profit is not cash. A contractor can be profitable on the income statement and unable to make payroll, because the receivables have not been collected. The cash flow forecast (a 13-week rolling forecast) shows when cash will come in and when it will go out. The contractor updates the forecast weekly and uses it to decide: which invoices to chase, which suppliers to delay, which projects to push for advance payments.

### 3.4 The Receivables Aging

The receivables aging shows, for each client invoice, how long it has been outstanding. Standard buckets: 0-30 days, 31-60, 61-90, 91-180, 180+. Any invoice in the 91-180 bucket requires active collection (phone call, formal letter, meeting). Any invoice in the 180+ bucket requires legal action or write-off decision.

### 3.5 The Audit

For an LLC, an annual external audit is required. The auditor reviews the books, issues an audit opinion, and signs the tax return. The audit is not a formality; a good auditor catches errors, identifies tax-saving opportunities, and provides a credibility signal to clients and partners. The contractor should not select the cheapest auditor; select the auditor with the most relevant contracting experience.

## Section 4: The HR Foundation

The transition from "I have helpers" to "I have employees" is one of the largest psychological and operational shifts in company building. The five components:

### 4.1 The Employment Contract

Every formal employee must have a written contract. The contract specifies: the position, the wage, the working hours, the leave entitlement, the social insurance arrangements, the termination conditions, the notice period, the confidentiality obligations. The contract must be in Arabic and must comply with Egyptian Labor Law (Law 12 of 2003, as amended). The contractor must use a labor lawyer to draft the first contract; subsequent hires can reuse the template.

### 4.2 The Social Insurance Registration

Every formal employee must be registered with the Social Insurance Authority within 30 days of hiring. The registration triggers the 11% employer contribution. The contractor who delays registration (a common informal practice in Egypt) accumulates liability that becomes due if the employee files a claim or if the authority audits.

### 4.3 The Payroll System

Payroll is not just paying wages. It is: calculating gross wage, deducting employee social insurance, deducting income tax (PAYE), calculating employer social insurance, calculating end-of-service accrual, calculating leave accrual, making the payment, issuing the payslip, filing the monthly social insurance return, filing the monthly payroll tax return. The contractor's accountant does this; the contractor's job is to ensure it is done correctly and on time.

### 4.4 The Performance Management Cycle

A small company does not need a complex performance management system, but it does need a quarterly conversation: the contractor sits with each employee, reviews the last quarter's work, discusses the next quarter's goals, identifies development needs, and acknowledges accomplishments. The conversation is documented in a brief note (one page) signed by both. This is the Islamic principle of shura (consultation) applied to management — not a vote, but a structured exchange of views that informs the contractor's decisions and signals to the employee that their work is observed and their input is heard.

### 4.5 The Employee Handbook

A short document (10-20 pages) that explains: the company's mission, the company's values (including the halal commitment), the working hours, the leave policy, the expense policy, the grievance procedure, the safety rules (critical for electrical work — see below), the disciplinary process. The handbook is signed by each new hire. The handbook is the contractor's defense in any labor dispute — it establishes that the employee was informed of the rules.

### 4.6 The Safety System

For an electrical contractor, safety is not optional. Egyptian Labor Law and the Social Insurance Authority require a documented safety system. The minimum: a written safety policy, regular safety briefings (weekly at minimum), personal protective equipment (PPE) issued and tracked, incident reporting and investigation, and insurance for the team (the social insurance covers work injuries; additional commercial coverage is available and recommended). The Islamic principle: the Prophet ﷺ said, "There should be neither harming nor reciprocating harm" (la darar wa la dirar — narrated by Ibn Majah, Ahmad; graded hasan by some, sahih by others). The contractor who exposes workers to avoidable risk violates this principle directly.

## Section 5: The Operational Foundation

A company's operations are the systems that turn contracts into delivered work and collected cash. The four core systems:

### 5.1 Project Tracking

For each project, a single document (the "project file") contains: the contract, the scope of work, the schedule, the budget, the change orders, the correspondence with the client, the daily logs (work done, materials used, labor hours), the invoices issued, the payments received, the safety incidents, the photos (before, during, after). The project file is digital (a shared folder or a simple project management tool) and physical (a binder for the original signed documents). The project file is the contractor's evidence in any dispute and the contractor's reference for future bids on similar work.

### 5.2 Invoicing

Invoices must be issued on time, in the correct format (including the VAT registration number, the client's VAT registration number, the invoice date, the description of goods or services, the amount, the VAT, the total), and tracked in the receivables aging. The contractor's invoicing discipline determines the cash flow. Many contractors do excellent work and go bankrupt because they fail to invoice promptly or fail to chase the unpaid invoices. The rule: invoice within 7 days of the milestone being reached. Chase any unpaid invoice at 30 days. Escalate at 60 days. Engage legal at 90 days (or write off — the contractor's choice based on the client's payment history and the relationship).

### 5.3 Procurement

Procurement is the second largest cost (after labor) for a contracting business. The procurement system has three components: a supplier list (3-5 suppliers for each major material category, with negotiated terms), a purchase order system (every purchase above a stated threshold requires a written PO, with the supplier's confirmation), and a receiving process (materials delivered are inspected, counted, and signed for). The system prevents two of the most common contractor losses: paying for materials not received and overpaying for materials (because the contractor did not check the price against the negotiated terms).

### 5.4 The Project Closing

Every project must be formally closed: the punch list completed and signed, the final invoice issued, the final payment collected, the project file closed, the lessons learned documented (what went well, what went badly, what to do differently next time), the warranty period started (typically 1 year for electrical work under Egyptian practice), the team reassigned. The project closing is the discipline that prevents the "long tail" of unfinished work that drains a contractor's cash and reputation.

## Section 6: The Management Foundation

The hardest transition in company building is from "I make every decision" to "the company makes decisions through systems and people." The four pillars:

### 6.1 Decision-Making Authority

For each significant decision type (bid submission, hire, fire, purchase above EGP X, contract change above EGP Y), specify who has authority. The contractor holds authority for strategic decisions (new service line, new partner, large purchase). The project manager holds authority for operational decisions within a project. The accountant holds authority for cash management within stated limits. The authorities are written in a one-page "authority matrix" that every senior team member sees and signs. The matrix prevents two failures: the contractor making decisions the team should make (slowing the business and demoralizing the team), and the team making decisions the contractor should make (exposing the contractor to uncontrolled risk).

### 6.2 Delegation

Delegation is not abdication. To delegate a task: (a) define the task specifically, (b) define the expected outcome, (c) define the constraints (budget, deadline, quality standard), (d) define the reporting requirement (what the contractor needs to see and when), (e) confirm the delegate understands, (f) step back and let the delegate work, (g) review at the agreed checkpoints. The contractor who delegates without defining the task and the constraints sets the delegate up to fail. The contractor who delegates and then intervenes in the detail sets the delegate up to quit. The discipline is in the up-front definition and the checkpoint review, not in the moment-to-moment supervision.

### 6.3 Accountability

Accountability is the discipline of confronting non-performance. When a team member misses a commitment (a deadline, a quality standard, a budget), the contractor's response has three steps: (a) the conversation — what happened, why, what is the recovery plan; (b) the documentation — a brief note in the team member's file recording the conversation and the agreed recovery; (c) the consequence — for a first minor failure, the consequence is the conversation itself; for repeated failures, the consequence escalates (formal warning, performance plan, termination). The Islamic principle: the Prophet ﷺ said, "Help your brother if he is the oppressor or the oppressed." When asked how to help the oppressor, he replied, "By stopping him from oppression" (Sahih al-Bukhari, no. 2444, narrated by Anas ibn Malik — grade: sahih). Accountability IS helping the team member — by refusing to let the team member's non-performance compound into a career-destroying pattern.

### 6.4 The Founder's Discipline

The most difficult accountability is to oneself. The contractor, as founder, has no boss. The contractor's discipline is the company's discipline. The contractor who arrives late, leaves early, takes personal expenses through the company account, hires relatives without standards, signs contracts without reading them, or makes commitments the company cannot keep — that contractor builds a company that does the same. The contractor's discipline is the company's ceiling. The Islamic principle of muhasabah (self-accountability) applies: Umar ibn al-Khattab said, "Hold yourself to account before you are held to account" (recorded by Ibn Abi Dunya, *Muhasabat al-Nafs*; attributed also to various tabi'un). The contractor who holds himself to a standard higher than the company's standard lifts the company; the contractor who holds himself to a lower standard drags the company down.

## Section 7: The Egyptian Legal and Regulatory Framework — The Full Checklist

A contracting LLC in Egypt in 2026 must maintain, at minimum:
1. Commercial registration (سجل تجاري) — annual renewal.
2. Tax registration (بطاقة ضريبية) — with the Egyptian Tax Authority.
3. VAT registration (if revenue above threshold) — monthly or quarterly returns.
4. Social Insurance Authority registration — monthly returns.
5. Industrial Security registration (for contracting businesses operating in certain industrial zones) — verify applicability.
6. Membership in the Egyptian Federation of Contractors — annual fee, grade renewal.
7. Chamber of Commerce membership — annual fee.
8. Municipal licenses for any permanent office or warehouse.
9. Environmental compliance (for projects above certain scale) — verify with each project.
10. Labor Office registration — annual update of employee count.
11. Trade union registration for workers (Syndicate of Construction Workers) — verify current requirements.
12. Health and safety documentation per Labor Law.
13. Annual audit filed with the Companies Authority.
14. Annual general assembly meeting of partners (for LLC) — documented in minutes.

The contractor's lawyer and accountant maintain this checklist. The contractor reviews it annually. A single missed filing can lead to penalties that compound into license suspension — the formal framework is unforgiving of neglect.

## Where Experts Disagree

There is a genuine disagreement among practitioners and consultants about the timing of the LLC conversion. The "early formalization" school argues that the contractor should formalize in year 2 — the credibility, tax savings, and discipline pay off. The "late formalization" school argues that the contractor should remain a sole proprietorship through year 4-5 — the formalization cost (accounting, audit, social insurance) drains capital that is better invested in the business, and the credibility can be built through the federation membership and personal reputation.

The synthesis: the contractor should formalize when ANY of these triggers fire: (a) the contractor hires the first employee on a fixed contract, (b) the contractor's annual revenue exceeds EGP 5M, (c) the contractor seeks a partner, (d) the contractor bids on work that requires an LLC (some government work, some large private developer work), (e) the contractor begins to plan the first real estate development (which requires a separate development entity — typically an LLC). The triggers are roughly equivalent; when the first one fires, formalize within 90 days.
