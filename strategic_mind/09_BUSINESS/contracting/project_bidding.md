## Why this file exists

The bid is the contractor's primary commercial act — the moment the contractor commits to a price for a defined scope of work. Every other commercial act (the negotiation, the variation order, the payment chase) is downstream of the bid. The contractor who bids well wins the work the firm can profitably deliver; the contractor who bids badly wins work the firm cannot deliver or fails to win work the firm could have delivered. This file is the discipline of the bid: which bids to pursue, how to investigate before bidding, how to construct the technical and commercial proposals, how to bid against stronger and weaker competitors, how to handle the post-bid negotiation, and how to bid differently for government and private work.

---

## The core principle

The contractor's bid win rate should be 20-35% — high enough that the firm wins enough work to fill the pipeline, low enough that the firm is not bidding too cheaply. A win rate above 35% indicates the contractor is under-pricing (the market would pay more); a win rate below 20% indicates the contractor is either over-pricing or bidding the wrong work. The bid discipline is the discipline of *choosing* the bids the firm should win and *declining* the bids the firm should not pursue. The contractor who bids every bid is the contractor who wins the wrong work and loses the right work. The contractor who bids selectively wins the work the firm can deliver at the margin the firm needs.

## The Principles

### Principle 1: The Bid Decision Framework — Which Bids to Pursue, Which to Decline

The bid decision is not "can we do this work" — it is "should we do this work, at the price we can win it, given the firm's current pipeline, capacity, and strategic objectives." The framework has six filters:

**Filter 1 — Strategic fit.** Does the bid align with the firm's strategic direction (the work types, the client types, the geographic area the firm is targeting)? If the bid is for work the firm does not want to do again, the bid is a distraction. The contractor who bids work the firm does not want to repeat is the contractor who wins work that pulls the firm off-strategy.

**Filter 2 — Capacity fit.** Does the firm have the crew, the equipment, and the management bandwidth to do the work without compromising the existing projects? If the firm must pull crews from existing projects to do the new work, the new work damages the existing work. The contractor's capacity is the binding constraint; the bid that exceeds the capacity is the bid that fails execution.

**Filter 3 — Financial fit.** Does the firm have the working capital (or the advance payment and credit that the bid will provide) to finance the work through the cash cycle? The bid that the firm cannot finance is the bid that fails mid-project. The financial filter is the cash flow discipline (see `cash_flow_zero_capital.md`).

**Filter 4 — Competitive fit.** Does the firm have a competitive advantage in this bid — a relationship with the client, a specialized capability, a cost advantage, a reference the competitors lack? If the firm has no competitive advantage, the firm is one of many bidders, and the bid is a price competition the firm is unlikely to win at a profitable margin.

**Filter 5 — Risk fit.** Is the risk profile of the bid acceptable — the client's payment discipline, the project's scope definition, the schedule's realism, the consultant's quality, the contractual terms? If the risk profile is poor, the bid is a risk the firm should not take. The contractor who bids high-risk work without a risk premium is the contractor who absorbs the risk at the firm's expense.

**Filter 6 — Pipeline fit.** Does the bid fit the firm's project pipeline — the timing of the start, the duration, the cash flow pattern? If the bid is a 12-month project that starts in 2 months and the firm's pipeline already has 3 projects starting in 2 months, the bid overloads the firm's mobilization capacity. The pipeline fit is the contractor's timing discipline.

The contractor who applies all six filters before every bid will decline 60-70% of the bids he is offered. The contractor who bids 30-40% of the bids he is offered, and who wins 20-35% of those, has a sustainable bid pipeline. The contractor who bids every bid wins more bids but loses more money per bid won.

### Principle 2: Pre-Bid Investigation — Client Financial Capacity, Project History, Competitor Presence

The pre-bid investigation is the contractor's defense against the bid that should not have been submitted. The investigation has three components:

**Component 1 — Client financial capacity.** Who is the client, what is the client's track record on payment, what is the client's current financial position? For government clients: the relevant ministry or authority's budget allocation for the project (is the project funded? is the funding secure?). For private developers: the developer's track record on completed projects (have they paid their contractors? have they completed the projects they started?). For private individuals: the individual's source of funds, the size of the project relative to the individual's apparent wealth (a 20 million EGP project for an individual with no visible business is a payment risk).

The investigation methods:
- Direct inquiry with other contractors who have worked for the client (the contractor's network, the Federation of Contractors' informal information, the supplier's information).
- Public records (the government's tender portal for government clients; the developer's website and project portfolio for private developers; the court records for litigation history).
- Site visits to the client's existing projects (the completed project that was paid on time is the best evidence; the incomplete project that stopped mid-construction is the warning).
- The consultant's report (the consultant who has worked with the client before can give an informal view of the client's payment discipline — but the consultant's view is biased by the consultant's relationship with the client).

**Component 2 — Project history.** Is this project a new build or a re-tender of a project that was previously awarded and failed? Was the project previously awarded to a contractor who defaulted? Was the project previously bid and cancelled (a sign of a client who changes scope or budget mid-bid)? The project history is the contractor's defense against the project that no one wants — the project that was previously bid by 6 contractors and was won by a contractor who walked off after 3 months is the project the contractor should not bid without a substantial risk premium.

**Component 3 — Competitor presence.** Who else is likely to bid? What are their competitive advantages (relationship, cost, specialization)? Is the competitor desperate for work (a competitor with an empty pipeline will bid aggressively and may underprice the contractor)? Is the competitor likely to be the "preferred" bidder (a relationship that the contractor cannot match)? The competitor analysis is the contractor's defense against the bid where the contractor cannot win at a profitable price.

The pre-bid investigation takes 2-5 days of the contractor's time per bid. The cost is low relative to the cost of bidding (the bid preparation is 5-15 days of the estimator's time, plus the bid bond, plus the management time). The contractor who investigates before bidding saves the bid cost on the bids he should not submit.

### Principle 3: The Technical Proposal vs the Commercial Proposal

The Egyptian bid (especially for government work and larger private developer work) typically has two components: the technical proposal and the commercial proposal. The two are evaluated separately, often by different committees, and the contractor must do both well to win.

**The technical proposal** demonstrates that the contractor can do the work. The components:

- **Company profile:** the firm's history, registration, classification, certifications, references.
- **Project understanding:** the contractor's narrative of the project, the scope, the challenges, the approach.
- **Method statement:** the contractor's plan for executing the work — the sequence, the methods, the equipment, the labor, the quality control, the safety.
- **Program:** the contractor's schedule, with milestones, critical path, and resource histogram.
- **Organization:** the contractor's project team, with CVs of the key personnel (project manager, project engineer, QA/QC engineer, safety officer).
- **References:** the contractor's track record of similar projects (3-5 references, with project name, client, value, year, scope).
- **Compliance matrix:** the contractor's point-by-point compliance with the technical specification (every specification requirement addressed, with the contractor's compliance or his proposed alternative).
- **Material schedule:** the contractor's proposed materials, with brands, models, datasheets, and country of origin.
- **Quality and safety plans:** the contractor's QA/QC plan and HSE plan, with the procedures, the records, the responsibilities.

The technical proposal is evaluated on a points system (typical: 100 points, with 30 for company profile and references, 30 for method statement and program, 20 for organization and personnel, 20 for compliance and material). The technical pass mark is typically 70-80 points — the contractor who scores below the pass mark is disqualified regardless of the commercial price.

**The commercial proposal** is the contractor's price. The components:

- **Priced bill of quantities:** the contractor's unit rate and total for each item in the bill of quantities.
- **Lump sum items:** the contractor's price for items that are not unit-rated (the mobilization, the testing, the commissioning).
- **Provisional sums:** the client's allowance for items not yet defined (the contractor marks these up if applicable, but typically the provisional sums are the client's items).
- **The total bid price:** the sum of the priced bill of quantities and the lump sum items.

The commercial proposal is evaluated on price (the lowest compliant bid wins, in the standard government tender). For private developer work, the evaluation is more nuanced — the technical points and the commercial price may be combined into a "most economically advantageous tender" score, where the lowest price does not automatically win.

The discipline: prepare both proposals with the same care. The contractor who prepares a strong technical proposal and a careless commercial proposal (or vice versa) loses the bid on the weak side. The contractor who prepares both well wins on the technical pass and competes on the commercial price.

### Principle 4: The Egyptian Bidding Culture — Open Tender, Negotiated, Selective

The three bidding modes (see `egypt_electrical_market.md` Principle 8) have different strategic implications:

**Open tender (مزايدة علنية):** published, open to any qualified bidder, lowest compliant price wins. The open tender is the most competitive mode — the contractor competes with every qualified bidder in the market. The strategic posture: bid only when the contractor has a clear competitive advantage (cost, capability, speed); otherwise, the open tender is a price war the contractor is unlikely to win at a profitable margin. The bid validity is typically 90 days (the contractor's price risk is high); the bid bond is typically 1-3% of bid value (the contractor's commitment cost is moderate). The open tender is the bid for the contractor who is building a reference list or who has a cost advantage; the contractor without these should not bid open tenders as the primary pipeline.

**Selective tender (مزايدة محدودة):** invitation to a pre-qualified list of 3-7 bidders. The selective tender is the relationship mode — being on the list is the precondition, the bid is the price competition among the listed. The strategic posture: invest in the pre-qualification (the documentation, the references, the relationship with the developer's procurement team) to get on the list, then bid competitively within the list. The bid validity is typically 30-60 days; the bid bond is typically 1-2% of bid value. The selective tender is the bid for the contractor with the relationship but not the cost advantage; the contractor who wins the selective tenders is the contractor who has the relationship and the price.

**Negotiated (ترسا بالتكي):** direct negotiation with a single contractor. The negotiated contract is the relationship-driven end of the market — the developer has decided on the contractor and is negotiating the price. The strategic posture: maintain the relationships that produce the negotiated work; the negotiated work is the highest-margin work the contractor can win because there is no price competition. The negotiated contract is the reward for years of consistent execution and relationship discipline.

The contractor's bid pipeline should be a mix: 30-50% selective tender (the relationship work, the moderate margin, the stable pipeline), 20-30% negotiated (the high-margin, low-volume relationship work), 10-20% open tender (the market test, the reference builder, the capacity fill). The contractor who relies solely on open tender is in a price war; the contractor who relies solely on negotiated is dependent on a small number of relationships; the mix is the sustainable posture.

### Principle 5: Bid Strategy Against Stronger Competitors — Differentiate, Niche, Partner

The contractor competing against a stronger competitor (a larger firm, a firm with better relationships, a firm with lower cost) cannot win on the stronger competitor's terms. The strategy:

**Strategy 1 — Differentiate on specialization.** The stronger competitor is a generalist; the contractor is a specialist in a niche the stronger competitor does not prioritize. The contractor who bids the niche scope (MT jointing, BMS integration, generator commissioning) as a subcontractor to the main contractor competes with the stronger competitor's subcontractor, not with the stronger competitor directly. The differentiation is the contractor's path to the work the stronger competitor does not want.

**Strategy 2 — Differentiate on responsiveness.** The stronger competitor has a slow bid process (the large firm's bid requires approval from the head office, the regional manager, the technical department). The contractor who can submit a complete bid in 7 days (the smaller firm's bid requires only the contractor's own decision) wins the bid that the larger competitor could not submit in time. The responsiveness is the contractor's path to the urgent work.

**Strategy 3 — Differentiate on flexibility.** The stronger competitor has a rigid process (the specification is the specification, the price is the price, the schedule is the schedule). The contractor who can offer alternatives (a different material that meets the spec at lower cost, a phased delivery that meets the client's cash flow, a value engineering proposal that reduces the total project cost) wins the bid that the larger competitor's rigidity cannot match. The flexibility is the contractor's path to the work the client values the alternatives on.

**Strategy 4 — Partner with the stronger competitor.** The contractor who cannot win the bid as the prime can partner with the stronger competitor as a subcontractor. The partnership gives the contractor the work (at a subcontractor's margin, not a prime's margin) and the relationship with the stronger competitor (a relationship that may produce future joint bids or future direct awards). The partnership is the contractor's path to the work the contractor cannot win alone — and the path to the relationship that may produce the work the contractor can win alone in the future.

[Sun Tzu, *Art of War* ch. 3 — Tier 1 on avoiding direct engagement when outmatched; Porter — Tier 2 on differentiation strategy]

### Principle 6: Bid Strategy Against Weaker Competitors — Do Not Engage in Price War

The contractor competing against a weaker competitor (a smaller firm, a firm with weaker relationships, a firm with a higher cost base) faces a different strategic problem. The weaker competitor may underprice the contractor — sometimes deliberately (the loss-leader), sometimes out of error (the underestimation), sometimes out of desperation (the bid that the weaker competitor must win to survive). The contractor's strategy:

**Strategy 1 — Do not match the price.** The contractor who matches the weaker competitor's price competes on the weaker competitor's terms, and the weaker competitor's terms are not sustainable for the contractor (the contractor's cost base is higher, the contractor's overhead must be covered, the contractor's margin must be earned). The contractor who matches the price erodes his own margin and trains the client to expect the lower price on future bids.

**Strategy 2 — Differentiate the proposal.** The contractor's bid at the higher price must be visibly different — a stronger technical proposal, a better material specification, a longer warranty, a stronger reference list, a more experienced project team. The differentiation is the contractor's argument that the higher price buys a better project. The client who sees the differentiation will pay the higher price; the client who does not see it will choose the lower price, and the contractor accepts the loss.

**Strategy 3 — Let the weaker competitor win the bid and learn.** The weaker competitor who wins the bid at the unsustainably low price learns (when the project loses money) that the low price was wrong. The contractor who lets the weaker competitor learn is the contractor who faces a more disciplined competitor on the next bid. The discipline is the patience to let the weaker competitor fail — and to be the alternative the client calls when the weaker competitor fails.

**Strategy 4 — Bid the next bid, not this one.** The contractor who declines the bid the weaker competitor wins is the contractor who is available for the next bid the weaker competitor cannot deliver. The patience is the discipline — the contractor who chases every bid the weaker competitor chases is the contractor who is busy on the wrong work when the right work arrives.

The contractor who engages in the price war with the weaker competitor loses twice — the margin is eroded on the work the contractor wins, and the competitor's discipline is not improved by the competition. The contractor who declines the price war preserves the margin and the discipline.

[Sun Tzu ch. 10 — Tier 1 on the discipline of not engaging when the cost of engagement exceeds the value of victory]

### Principle 7: The Loss-Leader Bid — When Strategic, When Destructive

The loss-leader bid (see `pricing_methodology.md` Principle 7) is a strategic tool in the bid context. The principles applied to bidding:

**When the loss-leader bid is strategic:**
- The bid is the entry to a 3-5 project relationship with a high-value client (the developer the contractor wants for repeat work).
- The bid is the reference project that opens a new segment (the first grade-A commercial bid that, if won at break-even, gives the contractor the reference for the next 10 grade-A bids).
- The bid is the capacity fill (the 2-3 month gap in the pipeline that the loss-leader keeps the crews together for).
- The bid is the competitor block (the specific competitor whose loss weakens them strategically — the loss-leader is the bid that the competitor must lose).

**When the loss-leader bid is destructive:**
- The contractor bids at break-even because the contractor is desperate for cash flow (the contractor will win the bid, finance the work, and run out of cash before the milestone payment).
- The contractor bids at break-even as a habit (the contractor's pricing is undisciplined, and the "loss-leader" is the default).
- The contractor bids at break-even to win volume (the contractor's overhead grows with the volume, and the margin does not cover the overhead).
- The contractor bids at break-even to block a competitor the contractor cannot actually afford to block (the competitor survives the block, the contractor doesn't).

The discipline: the loss-leader bid is acceptable no more than once per year, with a documented strategic objective, with a post-project review of whether the objective was achieved. The contractor who is doing more than one loss-leader bid per year is no longer doing loss-leaders — he is doing loss-making as a business model.

[Drucker, *The Effective Executive* — Tier 2 on disciplined resource allocation]

### Principle 8: Bid Timing — Last Minute vs Early

The bid submission timing is a strategic choice:

**Early submission advantages:**
- The client's procurement team has more time to review the bid and ask clarifying questions — the contractor who submits early can clarify ambiguities before the bid closes.
- The contractor's bid is the first the client sees, and the first bid can set the price expectation (the client's reference price for the bid evaluation).
- The contractor avoids the last-minute rush (the bid preparation errors that come from the last 24 hours of pressure).

**Early submission disadvantages:**
- The competitor who submits later can see the contractor's bid team at the submission, can estimate the contractor's bid volume, and can adjust the competitor's bid in response (the late submitter has the information advantage).
- The contractor commits to the price early; if the market moves (a copper price spike, a currency move), the contractor cannot adjust.

**Last-minute submission advantages:**
- The contractor has the latest material prices (the bid reflects the current market, not the market of 3 weeks ago).
- The competitor does not see the contractor's bid team until the last minute (the competitor cannot adjust).
- The contractor can incorporate the latest information (the client's clarifications, the site visit findings, the pre-bid meeting outcomes).

**Last-minute submission disadvantages:**
- The last-minute rush produces errors (the wrong price, the wrong scope, the missing document).
- The contractor's bid may be disqualified for late submission (the bid closing time is strict; a 5-minute late submission is rejected).

The strategic discipline: prepare the bid early (have the bid ready 3-5 days before the close), submit in the last 24-48 hours (the latest prices, the latest information, no time for competitors to adjust). The discipline combines the early preparation advantage with the late submission advantage.

### Principle 9: The Post-Bid Negotiation Phase

The post-bid negotiation (see `pricing_methodology.md` Principle 8 for the negotiation principles) is the bid's second phase. The principles applied:

**Principle 1 — Know your floor.** The contractor must know the absolute lowest price he can accept without losing money. Any negotiation below the floor is a no-go, regardless of the relationship pressure.

**Principle 2 — Trade, don't give.** Every concession is traded for something — a higher advance payment, a shorter payment cycle, a scope reduction, a longer schedule, a variation clause. The contractor who gives a price concession without a trade loses twice.

**Principle 3 — Walk-away credibility.** The contractor must be willing to walk away. The client who senses the contractor will not walk away has no incentive to stop pressing. The contractor who has walked away from a previous negotiation has stronger credibility in the next one.

**Principle 4 — Slow the negotiation.** The client's negotiation leverage increases with speed. The contractor's leverage increases with time. The contractor who responds to "I need a lower price by tomorrow" with "I will review and respond in a week" shifts the dynamic.

**Principle 5 — Document the negotiation.** Every concession and every trade is documented in writing. The contractor's memory of the negotiation differs from the client's; the document is the protection.

The post-bid negotiation is the contractor's opportunity to recover margin that the bid competition eroded. The contractor who does not negotiate (who accepts the bid price as the contract price) leaves 2-5% of contract value on the table — the value the client would have conceded in the negotiation. The discipline is to negotiate on every bid, even the winning bid.

[Fisher & Ury, *Getting to Yes* — Tier 2; practitioner interviews — Tier 3]

### Principle 10: Government Tenders — Special Considerations

The government tender has specific features the contractor must account for:

**Feature 1 — Lowest compliant bid wins.** The Egyptian government tender (Law 89 of 1998) typically awards to the lowest compliant bidder — the contractor with the lowest price among the bidders who passed the technical evaluation. The "compliant" is the technical threshold; the "lowest" is the price competition. The contractor who bids the government tender must price to win on price, not on value — the government evaluation does not reward value differentiation.

**Feature 2 — The tender document is the contract.** The government tender's terms and conditions are fixed; the contractor cannot negotiate them. The contractor's bid is an acceptance of the tender's terms, not a counter-offer. The contractor must read the tender's terms carefully — the payment terms, the variation procedures, the dispute resolution, the liquidated damages — and price the risk into the bid.

**Feature 3 — The bid bond and performance guarantee are mandatory.** The bid bond (1-3% of bid value) is submitted with the bid; the performance guarantee (10% of contract value) is submitted on award. The contractor's bank must be ready to issue both; the contractor's banking line of credit must accommodate.

**Feature 4 — The payment cycle is slow.** The government's payment cycle is typically 60-90 days from certification (longer than the private sector's 30-60). The contractor must finance 60-90 days of work-in-progress on the government work; the cash flow plan must reflect this.

**Feature 5 — The political risk is real.** The government's priorities shift; the project that was a priority this year may not be a priority next year. The contractor with a substantial government portfolio is exposed to the political risk — the project that is paused, the budget that is cut, the new administration that re-prioritizes.

[Law 89 of 1998 — Tier 4; practitioner interviews — Tier 3]

### Principle 11: Private Developer Contracts — Special Considerations

The private developer contract has different features:

**Feature 1 — The relationship is the bid.** The private developer does not run open tenders; the developer invites the contractors he has relationships with. The contractor who is not on the developer's pre-qualified list does not bid; the contractor who is on the list is one of 3-7 bidders competing on a mix of price and relationship.

**Feature 2 — The terms are negotiable.** The private developer's contract is negotiable — the payment terms, the variation procedures, the liquidated damages, the dispute resolution. The contractor must negotiate the terms that protect his position; the developer's standard contract is biased toward the developer.

**Feature 3 — The payment cycle is faster.** The private developer's payment cycle is typically 30-45 days from certification (faster than the government's 60-90). The contractor's cash flow is improved on the private developer work.

**Feature 4 — The financial risk is real.** The private developer's payment depends on the developer's cash flow, which depends on the developer's sales. The developer whose project is selling slowly may delay the contractor's payment; the developer whose project is selling well pays on time. The contractor must investigate the developer's sales position before bidding.

**Feature 5 — The repeat business is the prize.** The private developer with multiple projects is the contractor's repeat client — the developer who is satisfied with the contractor's first project will invite the contractor to bid the second, the third, the fourth. The relationship is the contractor's most valuable asset on the private developer work; the discipline is to deliver the first project at a fair margin to earn the right to the next.

## Where experts disagree

**On whether to bid every bid or to bid selectively.** Some contractors argue the contractor should bid every bid he is offered (the bid pipeline is the firm's lifeblood; the contractor who bids selectively misses opportunities). Others argue the contractor should bid only the bids the firm can win at a profitable margin (the bid cost — estimator time, bid bond, management time — is real; the bid that the firm cannot win is a wasted cost). The user's correct posture is selective — the 6-filter framework (Principle 1) applied to every bid decision.

**On whether to bid low to win and recover on variations.** Some contractors argue the contractor should bid low to win the bid and recover the margin on the variations (the "loss-leader plus variation" strategy). Others argue the strategy is dangerous (the client who sees the contractor's pattern refuses the variations, and the contractor is left with the low-price contract and no recovery). The user's correct posture is to bid at the profitable margin and to recover on variations only when the variations are properly priced and approved — the bid-low-recover-on-variation strategy is the contractor's path to the disputed contract and the eroded relationship.

## Applied to the user's situation

Three specific applications:

**1. The bid decision log.** The user should maintain a bid decision log — every bid offered, the 6-filter decision, the bid outcome, the post-bid review. The log is the contractor's discipline — the contractor who reviews the log quarterly sees the patterns (which bids the firm wins, which the firm loses, which the firm should have declined) and adjusts the bid strategy. The log is the firm's bid learning system.

**2. The pre-qualification investment.** The user should invest in the pre-qualification with 2-3 target developers — the documentation, the references, the relationships. The pre-qualification is the contractor's path to the selective tender invitations that produce the profitable work. The investment is multi-year; the return is the bid pipeline that does not depend on open tender price wars.

**3. The bid-no-bid discipline.** The user should establish the bid-no-bid discipline: every bid is a 6-filter decision, with the decision documented in the bid decision log. The discipline is the contractor's defense against the bid that the firm should not submit — and the discipline is the contractor's path to the 20-35% win rate that signals a healthy bid strategy.
