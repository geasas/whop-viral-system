## Why this file exists

The user has limited capital. The Egyptian electrical contracting market is structured so that the contractor must finance 60-150 days of work-in-progress (see `egypt_electrical_market.md` Principle 7). The combination — limited capital plus a long cash cycle — is the central strategic problem of the user's business. This file is the discipline of running the business with minimal working capital: how to use the client's money, the supplier's credit, the subcontractor's deferral, and the milestone structure to operate a contracting business that does not require the contractor to have a large bank balance. The discipline is not free — it has costs (the supplier credit premium, the subcontractor relationship cost, the bank guarantee fee) and risks (the late payment shock, the supplier cutoff, the subcontractor walk-off). The contractor who masters the discipline operates with minimal capital; the contractor who does not master it operates with the maximum capital the work demands.

---

## The core principle

The zero-capital model uses the cash of three parties — the client, the supplier, and the subcontractor — to finance the contractor's work-in-progress. The client pays an advance; the supplier provides credit; the subcontractor defers his payment. The contractor's own cash is the smallest possible reserve, sized to cover the gap between the client's expected payment and the actual payment, not to cover the work itself. The model is sustainable only when the contractor's payment discipline to the supplier and the subcontractor is impeccable — the contractor who pays late destroys the supplier credit and the subcontractor deferral that the model depends on. The zero-capital contractor is, in effect, a coordinator of cash flows: he receives the client's money, he pays the supplier and the subcontractor, he keeps the margin. The cash that passes through the contractor's bank account is many multiples of the contractor's own capital. The discipline is to manage that flow without the flow overwhelming the contractor.

## The Principles

### Principle 1: The Client's Money Model — Advance Payment → Supplier Credit → Labor on Completion

The zero-capital model is built on three cash flow moves, sequenced per project:

**Move 1 — Advance payment.** The contractor negotiates an advance payment of 15-30% of contract value on contract signature, against a bank guarantee of equivalent value. The advance payment is the contractor's working capital for the first phase — the procurement of long-lead material (cable, distribution boards, transformers, generators) and the mobilization of the site.

**Move 2 — Supplier credit.** The contractor uses the advance payment to pay the supplier deposits (10-30% of material cost) and negotiates supplier credit terms (15-60 days from delivery) for the balance. The supplier credit is the contractor's financing of the material that is on site but not yet paid for.

**Move 3 — Labor on completion.** The contractor pays the labor weekly, but the labor cost is recovered from the first milestone payment (typically at 15-25% of project completion). The labor is, in effect, financed by the milestone payment, not by the contractor's cash.

The sequence:
- Day 0: contract signed, advance payment received, bank guarantee issued.
- Day 0-30: material procured (advance payment used for deposits), supplier credit terms negotiated.
- Day 15-30: site mobilized, labor engaged, work begins.
- Day 30-60: work progresses, labor paid weekly from the cash reserve, material delivered on supplier credit.
- Day 45-60: first milestone measured and certified, milestone payment received (typically 20-25% of contract value).
- Day 60-75: supplier credit matures, supplier paid from the milestone payment.
- Day 75+: subsequent milestones fund subsequent work; the project runs on the client's money, not on the contractor's cash.

The contractor's own cash requirement for the project, in the ideal case, is the bank guarantee fee (1-2% of advance payment value, typically 15,000-60,000 EGP per 10 million EGP of contract value) plus the cash reserve to cover the gap between expected and actual milestone payment (typically 30-60 days of labor cost, 200,000-800,000 EGP per 10 million EGP of contract value).

The total cash requirement, in the ideal case, is 250,000-1,000,000 EGP per 10 million EGP of contract value — 2.5-10% of contract value. The contractor with 2-5 million EGP of cash can run a 20-50 million EGP project portfolio if the model is executed perfectly.

The model fails when any of the moves fail: the client delays the advance payment, the supplier demands cash on delivery, the milestone payment is late. The contractor must plan for each failure mode.

### Principle 2: Egyptian Advance Payment Norms and How to Negotiate Them

The Egyptian advance payment norms (2026):

| Segment | Typical advance payment | Bank guarantee requirement |
|---|---|---|
| Government tender | 10-15% | 100% of advance, valid through contract |
| Private developer (small) | 15-20% | 100% of advance, valid through contract |
| Private developer (large) | 15-25% | 100% of advance, valid through contract |
| Private individual (small) | 25-40% | None (personal trust) |
| Private individual (large) | 30-50% | None (personal trust) |
| Negotiated/emergency | 25-50% | Varies |

The negotiation principles for a higher advance:

**1 — Justify the advance with the procurement.** The contractor's case for a higher advance is the long-lead material (the cable that takes 4-6 weeks to deliver, the distribution board that takes 8-12 weeks, the transformer that takes 12-20 weeks, the generator that takes 16-24 weeks). The contractor who presents the procurement schedule and the supplier deposit requirements makes the case that the advance is necessary for the project schedule, not for the contractor's cash flow.

**2 — Offer a higher bank guarantee in exchange.** The client's risk on the advance is the contractor's default before the work is done. The bank guarantee is the client's protection. The contractor who offers a guarantee of 100-120% of the advance (instead of the typical 100%) reduces the client's risk and can negotiate a higher advance in exchange.

**3 — Phase the advance with the procurement.** The contractor can negotiate a phased advance — 10% on contract, 10% on delivery of long-lead material, 5% on site mobilization. The phasing aligns the advance with the contractor's actual cash needs and reduces the client's risk of paying the full advance before any work is done.

**4 — Trade a higher advance for a shorter payment cycle.** The contractor can offer a 25-day payment cycle (instead of the typical 30-45) in exchange for a 25% advance (instead of the typical 15%). The shorter cycle reduces the client's cash flow advantage but increases the contractor's, and the trade is sometimes acceptable to the client.

**5 — Trade a higher advance for a price discount.** The contractor can offer a 1-2% price discount in exchange for a 30% advance. The discount is the cost of the cash; the higher advance is the contractor's reduced working capital requirement. The trade is sometimes profitable for the contractor even with the discount.

The contractor who does not negotiate the advance payment is leaving working capital on the table. The Egyptian market accepts negotiation on the advance; the contractor who asks gets more than the contractor who doesn't.

### Principle 3: Supplier Credit Terms — 15, 30, 60 Days, and How to Negotiate

The Egyptian electrical supplier (the wholesaler, the manufacturer's distributor, the importer) offers credit terms that vary by the contractor's relationship, the supplier's policy, and the order size. The typical terms (2026):

| Supplier type | Credit terms | Discount for cash |
|---|---|---|
| Elsewedy Electric (direct) | 30-60 days for established customers | 1-2% for cash |
| Schneider distributor | 15-30 days for established customers | 1-3% for cash |
| ABB distributor | 15-30 days | 1-2% for cash |
| Local wholesaler (general) | 15-30 days | 2-5% for cash |
| Cable distributor (Elsewedy/Turkish/Chinese) | 15-60 days | 1-3% for cash |
| Importer (for non-stock items) | 0-15 days (often cash on delivery) | Often no discount |
| Manufacturer direct (for orders) | 30-90 days for established relationships | Varies |

The negotiation principles:

**1 — Build the credit over time.** The supplier starts with cash or short credit (15 days) and increases the credit (to 30, then 60 days) as the relationship develops. The contractor who pays on time, every time, builds the credit over 6-18 months. The contractor who pays late is restricted to cash terms and loses the credit benefit.

**2 — Concentrate the purchases with 2-4 suppliers.** The supplier gives better credit terms to the contractor who concentrates his purchases. The contractor who buys from 10 suppliers gets 15-day credit from all of them; the contractor who buys from 3 suppliers gets 30-60 day credit from all of them. The concentration is the contractor's leverage.

**3 — Negotiate the credit terms at the order, not at the payment.** The contractor who negotiates the credit terms when placing the order (and gets the supplier's confirmation in writing) is the contractor who controls the cash flow. The contractor who negotiates the credit terms when the payment is due is the contractor who is in crisis and has no leverage.

**4 — Use the discount for cash strategically.** The supplier's 2% discount for cash is, in effect, a 24% annual return on the cash (2% for 30 days of credit). The contractor who has the cash and can take the discount earns a 24% annual return — substantially better than the bank. The contractor who does not have the cash pays the 2% premium and uses the credit. The strategic discipline is to use the credit when cash is tight and to take the discount when cash is available — and to track which suppliers offer the best terms for the work the contractor is bidding.

**5 — Maintain the supplier credit even when paid by the client.** The contractor who is paid by the client on time and who then delays the supplier payment "to be safe" is destroying the credit relationship that the zero-capital model depends on. The supplier is paid on the agreed date, regardless of the contractor's cash position. The discipline is non-negotiable.

### Principle 4: The Milestone Structure That Protects Cash Flow

The milestone structure is the contractor's cash flow blueprint. The Egyptian convention is monthly milestones, certified by the consultant, paid 30-60 days from certification. The structure that protects cash flow has four characteristics:

**Characteristic 1 — Front-loaded milestones.** The early milestones (mobilization, first-fix, first material delivery) carry higher percentages of contract value than the work-in-place would justify. The front-loading gives the contractor cash early in the project, when the cash need is highest. The Egyptian market accepts modest front-loading (15-20% for mobilization and first-fix against a work-in-place value of 10-15%) but resists aggressive front-loading (30%+ for mobilization, which the consultant will not certify).

**Characteristic 2 — Short milestone intervals.** The monthly milestone is the Egyptian convention. The contractor can sometimes negotiate bi-weekly milestones for smaller projects (the consultant's certification cost is higher, but the contractor's cash flow is much smoother). The bi-weekly milestone is particularly valuable for residential and small commercial work where the contractor's cash need is continuous.

**Characteristic 3 — Defined measurement criteria.** The milestone is measured against defined criteria (units installed, meters pulled, points completed) that the consultant can certify quickly. The ambiguous milestone (the "substantial progress" milestone) is the milestone the consultant certifies late, because the criteria are debatable. The contractor's discipline is to define the milestone criteria in the contract, with the measurement standard, so the certification is quick.

**Characteristic 4 — Retention release schedule.** The retention is held through the defects liability period (typically 12 months). The contractor should negotiate a partial retention release at substantial completion (typically half the retention) so the cash is not entirely locked up for the full liability period. The partial release is the Egyptian convention; the contractor who does not negotiate it is leaving 2.5-5% of contract value locked up for an extra 6-12 months.

The milestone structure is negotiated at the contract, not adjusted mid-project. The contractor who accepts a back-loaded milestone structure (the milestones weighted toward the end of the project) is the contractor who finances the first 60-70% of the project from his own cash — and who, with limited capital, will run out of cash before the project completes.

### Principle 5: The Subcontractor Deferral Technique and Its Risks

The subcontractor deferral is the contractor's deepest source of working capital relief. The subcontractor's labor is paid weekly; the subcontractor's payment from the contractor is on milestone (30-60 days from the principal's payment). The subcontractor is, in effect, financing 60-150 days of labor cost. The contractor with 5-10 subcontractors and a 20 million EGP project portfolio is using 3-8 million EGP of subcontractor financing — the most valuable cash flow relief the model provides.

The risks of the deferral:

**Risk 1 — Subcontractor failure.** The subcontractor who is financing 60-150 days of labor cost can fail if his own cash runs out. The failure is the contractor's problem (see `subcontractor_management.md` Principle 7). The contractor must vet the subcontractor's financial capacity to absorb the deferral (Principle 4 of the subcontractor file) and must intervene early when the subcontractor's cash is tight (the partial payment, the direct labor payment, the emergency advance).

**Risk 2 — Subcontractor quality erosion.** The subcontractor who is financing the work may under-invest in labor quality (cheaper electricians) or material (substitutions) to manage his own cash flow. The quality erosion is the contractor's problem (the snag list grows, the rework cost grows, the client relationship erodes). The contractor must monitor the subcontractor's quality continuously and must intervene when the quality erodes (the inspection, the rejection, the demand for replacement).

**Risk 3 — Subcontractor walk-off.** The subcontractor who is being deferred too long may walk off the site and refuse to return. The walk-off is the contractor's most expensive problem (the recovery cost is 50-200% of the remaining subcontract value). The contractor must read the early signals (the subcontractor's foreman becomes hard to reach, the subcontractor's labor thins out, the subcontractor's invoicing becomes aggressive) and must respond before the walk-off (the partial payment, the relationship conversation).

**Risk 4 — Subcontractor reputation cost.** The Egyptian subcontractor market is informal and reputation-based. The contractor who defers his subcontractors too long gets the reputation of "the contractor who pays late," and the better subcontractors become unavailable to him. The contractor must protect his payment reputation as the most valuable asset the zero-capital model depends on.

The discipline: defer the subcontractor only as much as the subcontractor's financial capacity allows, and only as much as the contractor's payment reputation can sustain. The aggressive deferral that saves the contractor 200,000 EGP in working capital costs the contractor 2,000,000 EGP in the next bid's subcontractor availability. The discipline is to defer within the relationship's tolerance, not to defer maximally.

### Principle 6: Handling Late Client Payments

The late client payment is the most common cash flow shock. The Egyptian market's late payment norms (2026):

| Client type | Typical payment delay (from contracted date) |
|---|---|
| Government (small) | 30-60 days |
| Government (large infrastructure) | 60-120 days |
| Private developer (small) | 30-60 days |
| Private developer (large) | 30-45 days |
| Private individual | 0-30 days (often faster) |

The contractor's response to the late payment:

**Response 1 — Communicate early.** The contractor's project manager contacts the client's project manager within 5 days of the contracted payment date, with a written reminder and a request for the expected payment date. The communication is professional, not adversarial — the contractor is asking for the status, not demanding the payment.

**Response 2 — Escalate if the delay exceeds 30 days.** The contractor's senior management contacts the client's senior management, with a formal letter, requesting the payment and the cause of the delay. The escalation is the contractor's signal that the delay is now a relationship issue, not a routine matter.

**Response 3 — Use the contractual remedies if the delay exceeds 60 days.** The contract typically provides for interest on late payment (often 5-10% above the central bank rate, currently 22-28% in Egypt, so 27-38% effective annual rate) and for suspension of work after a defined period of late payment. The contractor who uses the contractual remedies is the contractor who is paid before the contractor who does not.

**Response 4 — Protect the supplier and subcontractor payments even when the client is late.** The contractor's payment discipline to the supplier and the subcontractor is not contingent on the client's payment discipline to the contractor. The contractor who delays the supplier and the subcontractor when the client delays him is the contractor who destroys the model. The contractor must pay the supplier and the subcontractor from the cash reserve, even when the client is late.

**Response 5 — Negotiate the recovery, not just the payment.** When the client is in genuine financial trouble (not just slow processing), the contractor negotiates the recovery — a partial payment now, a payment schedule for the balance, a security (a post-dated check, a bank guarantee, a lien on the property). The recovery negotiation is the contractor's protection against the client's eventual default.

The contractor who survives the Egyptian market is the contractor who has been through 5-10 late payment cycles and has learned the discipline: communicate early, escalate appropriately, use the contractual remedies, protect the supplier and subcontractor, negotiate the recovery when needed.

### Principle 7: Bank Guarantees — When Needed, Costs, Alternatives

The bank guarantee is the contractor's standard security instrument in the Egyptian market. The two main types:

**Advance payment guarantee:** the contractor's bank guarantees to repay the advance payment if the contractor does not perform. The guarantee is typically 100% of the advance payment value, valid through the contract period, and released when the work-in-place value exceeds the advance. The cost: 1-2% of the guarantee value per year, payable to the bank.

**Performance guarantee:** the contractor's bank guarantees to compensate the client for the contractor's default. The guarantee is typically 10% of contract value, valid through the contract and the defects liability period. The cost: 1-2% of the guarantee value per year.

**Retention bond (alternative to retention):** the contractor's bank guarantees the contractor's performance during the defects liability period, in lieu of the client holding the cash retention. The retention bond frees the contractor's cash from being locked up in retention. The cost: 1-2% of the bond value per year. The retention bond is not universally accepted in Egypt; the larger developers and the government typically accept it, the smaller developers and individuals often do not.

**Bid bond (for tender bids):** the contractor's bank guarantees the contractor will enter into the contract if his bid is accepted. The bid bond is typically 1-3% of bid value, valid for the bid validity period (30-90 days). The cost: 0.5-1% of the bond value.

The total bank guarantee cost for a 10 million EGP project (15% advance, 10% performance, 1% bid):
- Advance payment guarantee: 1.5 million EGP × 1.5% = 22,500 EGP/year
- Performance guarantee: 1 million EGP × 1.5% = 15,000 EGP/year
- Bid bond (one-time): 100,000 EGP × 0.75% = 750 EGP
- Total annual cost: ~38,000 EGP for a 10 million EGP project, or 0.38% of contract value per year.

**The alternatives to bank guarantees:**
- **Cash deposit:** the contractor deposits cash with the client in lieu of the bank guarantee. The cost is the opportunity cost of the cash (the 22-28% annual interest the contractor could earn elsewhere) — typically much higher than the bank guarantee fee. The cash deposit is the most expensive form of security and should be used only when the bank guarantee is not available.
- **Parent company guarantee:** for contractors with a parent company, the parent company can guarantee the contractor's performance. The cost is the parent company's internal cost of capital allocation. Not available to the user (no parent company).
- **Insurance bond:** an insurance company bond (instead of a bank guarantee). The cost is similar to the bank guarantee (1-2% per year). Less common in Egypt but increasingly available.

The discipline: use the bank guarantee for the advance and performance; use the retention bond when the client accepts it (the cash freed up is the contractor's working capital); avoid the cash deposit except in cases of necessity. The total bank guarantee cost is 0.3-0.5% of contract value per year — a small price for the working capital the guarantees unlock.

### Principle 8: Letter of Credit for Imported Materials

For material that is imported (Schneider, ABB, Siemens equipment not stocked locally; specialized cable; specialized fixtures), the supplier may require a Letter of Credit (LC) — the contractor's bank guarantees payment to the supplier's bank upon shipment. The LC is the international equivalent of the local bank guarantee.

The LC types and costs:
- **Sight LC:** the supplier is paid on presentation of shipping documents. The cost: 0.5-1.5% of LC value (bank fees plus the letter issuance fee).
- **Usance LC (deferred payment):** the supplier is paid at a defined date after shipment (30, 60, 90 days). The cost: 0.5-1.5% of LC value plus the interest for the deferred period.
- **Confirmed LC:** a third-party bank (typically an international bank) confirms the LC, providing additional security to the supplier. The cost: 1-3% of LC value (the confirmation fee is the additional cost).

The LC ties up the contractor's bank line of credit (the bank will limit the contractor's total LC exposure based on the contractor's financial position). The contractor who has a 5 million EGP line of credit can issue up to 5 million EGP of LCs at any time. The contractor who needs more must either increase the line (with additional collateral or financial strengthening) or stagger the LCs (issue one, wait for shipment, issue the next).

The strategic discipline: avoid the LC when possible by sourcing from local stock (Elsewedy stocks most electrical equipment in Egypt; the local Schneider distributor stocks most Schneider products in Egypt). Use the LC only for the imported material that is not locally stocked. The LC is a financing cost and a banking complexity that the local sourcing eliminates.

### Principle 9: The Smallest Possible Cash Reserve Calculation

The cash reserve is the contractor's insurance against the gap between expected and actual cash flow. The reserve is the smallest amount of cash the contractor must hold to operate without running out.

The calculation:
1. **Identify the worst-case payment delay.** The contractor's largest client (or the largest 2-3 clients combined) is delayed by 60-90 days. What is the contractor's monthly cash outflow during that delay? (Labor + subcontractor + supplier payments that cannot be deferred further + bank guarantee fees + office overhead.)
2. **Identify the worst-case supplier cutoff.** The contractor's largest supplier (or 2-3 suppliers combined) cuts the credit and demands cash on delivery. What is the contractor's monthly material cost that must then be paid in cash? (Typically 50-70% of monthly project cost.)
3. **Identify the worst-case subcontractor walk-off.** One or two of the contractor's strategic subcontractors walk off. What is the cost of the replacement labor (direct labor or emergency subcontractor) for the work the walked-off subcontractors were doing?
4. **Sum the worst cases.** The sum is the cash reserve that covers all three simultaneously (the perfect storm scenario).

For a contractor with a 30 million EGP annual revenue:
- Worst-case payment delay (60 days): labor 1.5M/month × 2 = 3.0M; subcontractor 1.0M/month × 2 = 2.0M; supplier 0.5M/month × 2 = 1.0M; bank fees 0.05M/month × 2 = 0.1M; office 0.4M/month × 2 = 0.8M. Subtotal: 6.9M.
- Worst-case supplier cutoff (additional cash for material): 1.5M/month × 2 = 3.0M.
- Worst-case subcontractor walk-off (replacement labor): 0.5M/month × 2 = 1.0M.
- **Total worst-case reserve: 10.9 million EGP** (about 36% of annual revenue, or 4 months of total operating cost).

This is the perfect storm reserve — the reserve that covers all three worst cases simultaneously. The contractor with 10.9 million EGP of cash survives a 60-day payment delay combined with a supplier cutoff and a subcontractor walk-off. The contractor with less survives a subset of these.

The realistic reserve is smaller — the contractor typically targets 2-3 months of total operating cost (50-75% of the perfect storm reserve), accepting that the perfect storm is rare and that the contractor can manage the individual shocks with the smaller reserve plus the supplier and subcontractor relationships.

The user's specific reserve target, for a contractor at the user's current scale (15-25 million EGP annual revenue), is 2-4 million EGP. This is the smallest cash reserve that allows the zero-capital model to operate without the contractor running out of cash in a routine 30-60 day client delay.

### Principle 10: Warning Signs of Cash Flow Crisis

The warning signs of an emerging cash flow crisis, in order of severity:

**Early warning signs (act now, before the crisis):**
- The contractor's cash reserve falls below the 2-month operating cost target.
- The contractor's largest client's payment delay exceeds 30 days.
- A supplier requests cash on delivery where credit was previously offered.
- A subcontractor's foreman becomes hard to reach.
- The contractor's monthly bank balance is below the monthly operating cost (the contractor is consuming reserve).

**Mid warning signs (act urgently, the crisis is approaching):**
- The contractor's cash reserve falls below the 1-month operating cost target.
- Two or more clients' payments are delayed simultaneously.
- A supplier cuts the credit limit (refuses further credit, demands cash).
- A subcontractor formally requests early payment (in writing).
- The contractor's bank line of credit is fully drawn.

**Late warning signs (the crisis is here, emergency action required):**
- The contractor cannot meet payroll.
- A supplier files a legal action for unpaid invoices.
- A subcontractor walks off a site.
- A bank calls the contractor's line of credit (demands repayment).
- The contractor's checks are returned for insufficient funds (a criminal offense under Egyptian law — the bounced check is prosecuted under Articles 534-536 of the Penal Code).

The contractor's response to each stage:

**Early stage:** reduce new commitments (decline or delay new bids), accelerate collections (escalate late client payments), defer discretionary spending (delay equipment purchases, defer non-essential overhead).

**Mid stage:** partial liquidation of assets (sell equipment the contractor can lease back, factoring of receivables — selling the client's invoices to a bank or factoring company at a discount for immediate cash), emergency borrowing (the short-term bridge from a bank or from the contractor's personal network).

**Late stage:** emergency recovery (the contractor's last-resort actions — selling equity in the firm, restructuring the debt, declaring insolvency under the Egyptian Commercial Code). The late stage is the contractor's survival mode; the recovery is uncertain and the firm's reputation is damaged regardless of the outcome.

The discipline: act on the early warning signs. The contractor who acts early manages the crisis; the contractor who waits for the mid or late signs is managed by the crisis.

## Where experts disagree

**On the right size of the cash reserve.** Some contractors argue for the perfect-storm reserve (36% of annual revenue, 4 months of operating cost); others argue for the minimal reserve (1 month of operating cost, with the relationship discipline covering the rest). The user's correct posture is the middle — 2-3 months of operating cost — combined with the relationship discipline that prevents the perfect storm from materializing. The minimal reserve is too risky for a contractor who is still building the relationships; the perfect-storm reserve is too expensive for a contractor with limited capital.

**On whether to use factoring (selling receivables at a discount).** Some contractors use factoring aggressively (it converts the late client payment into immediate cash, at a 5-15% discount); others avoid it (the discount erodes the margin, and the client may see the factoring as a sign of contractor weakness). The user's correct posture is to use factoring as an emergency tool, not a routine practice — the discount is too expensive for routine use, but the cash flow relief is valuable in a crisis.

## Applied to the user's situation

Three specific applications:

**1. The cash reserve target.** The user should target a 2-3 month operating cost reserve, segregated from the working capital account, replenished as the client payments arrive, used only for the late payment shocks. For a 20 million EGP annual revenue, the target is 2-4 million EGP. The user should build this reserve over 12-24 months, by retaining 10-15% of profit in the reserve account until the target is reached.

**2. The supplier concentration.** The user should concentrate the purchases with 2-4 suppliers (1-2 cable, 1-2 distribution board and breaker, 1 lighting, 1 miscellaneous) and build the credit terms with each over 12-24 months. The concentration is the user's leverage for the 30-60 day credit terms the zero-capital model depends on.

**3. The advance payment discipline.** The user should negotiate the advance payment on every contract — present the procurement schedule, justify the advance with the long-lead material, offer the bank guarantee, trade the higher advance for the shorter payment cycle or the price discount where the trade is profitable. The discipline of asking for the advance on every contract is the discipline that the zero-capital model requires.
