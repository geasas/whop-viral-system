## Why this file exists

The Egyptian electrical contractor does not — and should not — do all the work himself. The Egyptian construction market is built on a layered subcontracting structure (see `egypt_electrical_market.md` Principle 4), and the contractor's ability to manage subcontractors is the difference between the contractor who scales and the contractor who drowns in direct labor. This file is the discipline of subcontractor management: when to use them, how to vet them, how to contract them, how to pay them, and how to handle the inevitable failures.

---

## The core principle

The subcontractor is the contractor's extension into work types or volumes the contractor cannot or should not do directly. The subcontractor is not a competitor, not a servant, not a friend — he is a partner in a transactional relationship governed by a written agreement, fair payment, and mutual interest. The contractor who treats subcontractors as servants loses them when the market tightens; the contractor who treats them as friends is exploited when the relationship is tested; the contractor who treats them as partners with written agreements, fair payment, and consistent work builds a network that becomes the firm's most durable competitive advantage. The strategic value of a long-term subcontractor relationship is, in the Egyptian market, comparable to the strategic value of a long-term client relationship — and the contractor who builds both, in parallel, has the strongest position the market allows.

## The Principles

### Principle 1: When to Use Subcontractors vs Direct Labor — The Decision Framework

The decision to use a subcontractor or direct labor is not a default; it is a strategic decision with seven criteria:

**Criterion 1 — Volume stability.** If the work volume is stable and predictable across projects (e.g., the contractor consistently has 5,000-15,000 light points per year), direct labor is preferable (the crew is always deployed, the productivity is high, the cost is controlled). If the volume is sporadic (e.g., the contractor has 20,000 light points in one quarter and 2,000 in the next), a subcontractor is preferable (the subcontractor absorbs the volume variance, the contractor does not carry idle labor).

**Criterion 2 — Skill specialization.** If the work requires a specialized skill the contractor does not have in-house (MT jointing, generator commissioning, BMS programming), a subcontractor is preferable (the contractor pays for the skill when needed, not for the skill to be idle). If the work requires a skill the contractor has in-house, direct labor is preferable (the contractor controls the quality and the productivity).

**Criterion 3 — Equipment cost.** If the work requires specialized equipment the contractor does not own (scissor lifts for high-bay lighting, MT test equipment, generator load banks), a subcontractor with the equipment is preferable (the contractor does not invest in equipment for sporadic use). If the contractor would use the equipment across multiple projects, owning is preferable.

**Criterion 4 — Cash flow impact.** Subcontractors absorb the cash-flow cycle of their portion of the work — they pay their labor weekly while the contractor pays them on milestone. The cash-flow benefit to the contractor is substantial (the contractor's working capital is reduced by the subcontractor's labor cost). Direct labor consumes working capital. The contractor with limited capital should lean toward subcontractors; the contractor with strong capital can afford direct labor.

**Criterion 5 — Quality control.** Direct labor is easier to control (the contractor's foreman is on site, the contractor's standard is enforced). Subcontractor quality varies (the subcontractor has his own foreman, his own standard). The contractor who values quality above cost should lean toward direct labor for the visible work (lighting, finishing) and toward subcontractors for the hidden work (conduit, cable pulling).

**Criterion 6 — Risk allocation.** Subcontractors absorb risk — the risk of labor shortage, the risk of productivity variance, the risk of rework. Direct labor concentrates risk in the contractor. The contractor who wants to concentrate risk (and the corresponding reward) should use direct labor; the contractor who wants to spread risk should use subcontractors.

**Criterion 7 — Strategic relationship.** Some subcontractors are strategic — the subcontractor who has been with the contractor for 5+ years, who knows the contractor's standards, who is available on call. The strategic subcontractor should be retained even when direct labor could do the work (the relationship is the asset). The transactional subcontractor should be used only when direct labor cannot do the work.

The default decision rule: **direct labor for the work the contractor does repeatedly, subcontractors for the work the contractor does occasionally or for the work that requires specialized skill or equipment.** The contractor who subcontractors everything is a coordinator, not a contractor; the contractor who does everything directly is a craftsman, not a business. The balance is the strategic decision.

### Principle 2: The Three Types of Subcontractors — Labor-Only, With-Materials, With-Design

The Egyptian subcontractor market segments into three types:

**Type 1 — Labor-only (مقاولة بالك).** The subcontractor provides labor (skilled, semi-skilled, laborer) for a defined scope, with the contractor providing all material. Pricing is per point (for lighting/power) or per meter (for conduit/cable) or per day (for general labor). This is the most common subcontractor type in Egyptian electrical work.

Pricing norms (2026):
| Work type | Unit | Labor-only rate (EGP, 2026) |
|---|---|---|
| Light point | per point | 80-180 |
| Socket point | per point | 100-220 |
| Power point (A/C) | per point | 150-350 |
| PVC conduit (15-25mm) | per meter | 25-50 |
| PVC conduit (32-50mm) | per meter | 40-75 |
| Cable pulling (LV, 1.5-6 mm²) | per meter | 8-18 |
| Cable pulling (LV, 10-25 mm²) | per meter | 15-35 |
| Cable pulling (LV, 35-95 mm²) | per meter | 30-80 |
| Cable tray installation | per meter | 60-150 |
| Cable trunking installation | per meter | 50-120 |
| Distribution board wiring (per way) | per way | 80-180 |
| Lighting fixture installation | per fixture | 30-100 |
| Generator commissioning | per day | 2,500-6,000 |
| MT cable jointing | per joint | 2,500-6,500 |

**Type 2 — With-materials (مقاولة بالخامات).** The subcontractor provides both labor and material for a defined scope. The contractor provides the design, the supervision, and the integration. This is common for the standardized scopes (PVC conduit, cable pulling, fixture installation) where the subcontractor has supplier relationships that allow him to source material at competitive prices.

Pricing norms (with-materials, 2026):
| Work type | Unit | With-materials rate (EGP, 2026) |
|---|---|---|
| Light point (standard PVC + cable + fitting) | per point | 250-450 |
| Socket point (standard) | per point | 350-650 |
| Power point (A/C) | per point | 500-1100 |
| PVC conduit installation (conduit + fittings) | per meter | 60-110 |
| Cable pulling (cable + labor) — 2.5 mm² | per meter | 60-95 |
| Cable pulling — 6 mm² | per meter | 95-150 |
| Cable pulling — 16 mm² | per meter | 180-300 |

**Type 3 — With-design (مقاولة بالتصميم).** The subcontractor provides design, labor, and material for a defined scope. This is common for specialized scopes (BMS, smart home, fire alarm, generator integration) where the subcontractor has the engineering capability the contractor does not have. The with-design subcontractor is the contractor's partner in technical scope the contractor cannot deliver directly.

The with-design subcontractor's pricing is typically a turnkey price for the scope, plus a design fee, plus a commissioning fee. The contractor's margin on the with-design subcontract is typically 5-12% (lower than the labor-only margin because the subcontractor carries the design risk and the material risk).

### Principle 3: The Egyptian Subcontractor Culture — Loyalty, Payment Expectations, Problems

The Egyptian subcontractor culture has specific patterns the contractor must understand:

**Pattern 1 — Personal relationship dominates the formal contract.** The Egyptian subcontractor works with the contractor he trusts; the contract documents the trust but does not create it. The contractor who tries to manage subcontractors purely through contracts (without the personal relationship) is the contractor who loses subcontractors when the market tightens.

**Pattern 2 — Payment expectations are weekly or per milestone, not on completion.** The Egyptian subcontractor's labor is paid weekly; the subcontractor must pay his labor weekly regardless of when the contractor pays him. The contractor who pays the subcontractor monthly or quarterly is forcing the subcontractor to finance the work, and the subcontractor will eventually refuse the work or demand a premium.

**Pattern 3 — The subcontractor's first response to a problem is to absorb it, not to escalate.** The Egyptian subcontractor's culture of *sabr* (patience) and *karam* (generosity) means he will absorb a small payment delay, a small scope change, a small material substitution — once or twice. The third time, the subcontractor begins to disengage (slower mobilization, lower productivity, eventual refusal of further work). The contractor who reads the early absorption as "no problem" misses the signal that the relationship is eroding.

**Pattern 4 — The subcontractor's loyalty is to the contractor's payment discipline, not to the contractor's personality.** The contractor who pays on time and fair retains the subcontractor through market downturns. The contractor who pays late or short loses the subcontractor to the competitor who pays on time, regardless of how much the subcontractor "likes" the contractor personally.

**Pattern 5 — The subcontractor market is informal and reputation-based.** Subcontractors are vetted by reputation, not by audited financials. A subcontractor who has a reputation for late completion, poor quality, or labor disputes cannot hide that reputation; the contractor who asks around will hear it within a day. The contractor who relies on the subcontractor's own claims (rather than the market reputation) is exposed.

The problems that arise from these patterns:
- The subcontractor who is paid late begins to underperform before he refuses the work.
- The subcontractor who is overworked (the contractor's volume exceeds the subcontractor's capacity) produces poor quality on the contractor's work, even though the subcontractor's other work is fine.
- The subcontractor whose key electrician leaves has a quality drop the contractor may not detect until the snag list.
- The subcontractor who is in financial trouble (the contractor's payment delay pushed him over) may go bankrupt mid-project, leaving the contractor with half-finished work and a complicated recovery.

### Principle 4: How to Vet a Subcontractor — Track Record, Financial Stability, Team Size, Current Workload

The subcontractor vetting process is the contractor's defense against the failed subcontractor. The vetting has four dimensions:

**Dimension 1 — Track record.** The contractor asks for 3-5 references from past projects, with project name, client, scope, value, and year. The contractor calls the references (does not email — the phone call is harder to fake) and asks:
- Did the subcontractor complete on time?
- Did the subcontractor's quality meet the spec?
- Did the subcontractor's labor stay through the project?
- Would you use this subcontractor again?
- (If not, why not — the negative reference is the most informative.)

The contractor who does not call the references is relying on the subcontractor's claims. The contractor who calls the references knows the subcontractor's actual performance.

**Dimension 2 — Financial stability.** The contractor asks for:
- Bank reference (a letter from the subcontractor's bank confirming the account and the relationship duration — not the balance, which the bank will not disclose).
- Supplier references (2-3 suppliers who confirm the subcontractor pays on time).
- A balance sheet or financial summary (the small subcontractor may not have audited financials, but the contractor can ask for a summary).

The financially unstable subcontractor is the subcontractor who will fail mid-project when the contractor's payment is late. The contractor must assess the subcontractor's capacity to absorb 30-60 days of payment delay without failing.

**Dimension 3 — Team size and skill mix.** The contractor visits the subcontractor's current project (unannounced if possible) and observes:
- How many electricians are on the crew?
- Are they skilled (do they work with confidence) or unskilled (do they wait for instructions)?
- Does the foreman manage the crew or work alongside them?
- Is the site organized (materials staged, tools accounted for) or chaotic?

The team size tells the contractor whether the subcontractor can absorb the contractor's scope. The skill mix tells the contractor whether the subcontractor can deliver the contractor's quality standard. The site organization tells the contractor whether the subcontractor is a professional or a labor broker.

**Dimension 4 — Current workload.** The contractor asks the subcontractor how many projects he is currently working on and how many crews he has. The contractor cross-checks by asking the references the same question. The subcontractor who claims 3 crews and is actually working 6 projects with 2 crews is the subcontractor who will underperform on the contractor's work because his crews are spread thin.

The vetting takes 2-5 days of the contractor's time per subcontractor. The cost is low (a few days of the contractor's time) relative to the cost of a failed subcontractor (the recovery cost can be 50-200% of the subcontract value). The contractor who skips the vetting to save the time is the contractor who pays the recovery cost later.

### Principle 5: The Subcontractor Agreement Essentials

The Egyptian subcontractor agreement should include, at minimum:

**1. Scope of work:** a detailed description of the work the subcontractor will perform, with reference to the project drawings and specifications. The scope is the subcontractor's defense against scope creep and the contractor's defense against scope evasion. Vague scope ("the electrical work") is the root cause of most subcontractor disputes.

**2. Price:** the price per unit (point, meter, day) or the lump sum, with the basis of measurement (the standard for measuring completed work — typically the Egyptian standard or the project-specific measurement rule). The price is the subcontractor's revenue and the contractor's cost; the agreement must specify both clearly.

**3. Payment terms:** the trigger for payment (milestone, monthly measurement, completion of defined scope), the payment cycle (e.g., 30 days from measurement), the retention (typically 5-10% until defects liability), and the mechanism for disputes (escalation to the contractor's project manager, then to the contractor, then to mediation, then to arbitration).

**4. Completion schedule:** the start date, the completion date, the intermediate milestones, and the liquidated damages for delay (typically 0.1-0.5% of subcontract value per day of delay, capped at 10% of subcontract value). The schedule is the contractor's defense against the subcontractor who falls behind.

**5. Quality standard:** the reference standard (Egyptian code, IEC, project specification), the inspection regime (the contractor's right to inspect, the consultant's right to inspect, the rejection right), the rework obligation (the subcontractor's obligation to rework at his cost), and the warranty period (typically aligned with the main contract's defects liability period).

**6. Material responsibility:** who supplies what material, with the procedure for material substitution (typically, substitution requires the contractor's written approval and the consultant's written approval). The material responsibility is the most contested element of the agreement — the subcontractor who substitutes material without approval is the subcontractor who delivers a different quality than agreed.

**7. Insurance and safety:** the subcontractor's obligation to insure his labor (workers' compensation, social insurance registration — required by Egyptian law) and to comply with the project's safety program (PPE, scaffolding, fall protection). The subcontractor who does not carry social insurance is exposing the contractor to liability for the subcontractor's labor (the contractor can be held responsible under Egyptian labor law).

**8. Termination:** the contractor's right to terminate for cause (delay, quality failure, safety violation, financial failure) and the procedure for termination (written notice, opportunity to cure, handover of work in progress). The termination clause is the contractor's last-resort protection against the failed subcontractor.

**9. Dispute resolution:** the procedure for resolving disputes — typically negotiation, then mediation by a respected industry figure, then arbitration (Cairo Regional Centre for International Commercial Arbitration or the Egyptian Arbitration Act 27 of 1994). The Egyptian court system is slow and unpredictable for subcontractor disputes; arbitration is the preferred forum.

### Principle 6: The Payment Cycle Problem and Its Management

The central problem of Egyptian subcontractor management is the payment cycle. The chain:

- The principal pays the contractor in 30-90 days from milestone.
- The contractor pays the subcontractor in 30-60 days from the principal's payment.
- The subcontractor pays his labor weekly.

The subcontractor is financing 60-150 days of labor cost while waiting for the contractor's payment. The subcontractor's capacity to finance this varies — the larger subcontractor with a balance sheet can absorb it; the smaller subcontractor cannot. When the smaller subcontractor runs out of cash, he either:
- Stops work and demands a payment (the contractor's emergency payment, often at a discount).
- Borrows from the informal market at 30-50% annual interest (the cost of which he recovers by under-investing in labor quality or material).
- Walks off the site and refuses to return (the most damaging outcome).

**The management discipline:**
1. **Pay the subcontractor on the agreed schedule, even if the principal has not paid the contractor.** The contractor who pays his subcontractor on time even when his own payment is late builds the relationship that survives the downturn. The contractor who delays the subcontractor's payment because his own is late is the contractor who loses the subcontractor when the market tightens.
2. **If the contractor cannot pay on time, communicate early.** The subcontractor who is told on day 25 that the payment will be 15 days late can plan; the subcontractor who is told on day 35 that the payment is delayed is in crisis. The communication is the management discipline.
3. **Pay the labor directly if the subcontractor is in trouble.** When the subcontractor's financial trouble threatens the project, the contractor can pay the subcontractor's labor directly (with the subcontractor's agreement) to keep the labor on site. This is the emergency measure that prevents the project from failing; it is not the routine practice.
4. **Build a subcontractor payment reserve.** The contractor should hold a cash reserve equal to 30-60 days of subcontractor payments, separate from the working capital, to cover the gap between the principal's payment and the subcontractor's payment. The reserve is the contractor's insurance against the payment cycle shock.

### Principle 7: Handling the Subcontractor Who Fails Mid-Project

The subcontractor failure mid-project is the contractor's most expensive problem. The recovery cost is typically 50-200% of the remaining subcontract value, and the schedule delay is typically 30-90 days. The contractor's response:

**Step 1 — Diagnose the failure.** Is the subcontractor in financial trouble (cannot pay his labor)? In managerial trouble (his foreman left, his crew quit)? In technical trouble (the work is beyond his capability)? In relational trouble (he is angry about a payment or a perceived unfairness)? The diagnosis determines the response.

**Step 2 — Stabilize the project.** If the subcontractor's labor is on site and willing to continue with direct payment, the contractor pays the labor directly and keeps the project moving. If the labor has left, the contractor mobilizes a replacement crew (from another subcontractor, from direct labor, from an emergency labor supplier) and accepts the schedule delay.

**Step 3 — Document the failure.** The contractor documents the subcontractor's failure (the unpaid labor, the uncompleted work, the defects) with photos, written statements, and the consultant's confirmation. The documentation is the contractor's defense against the subcontractor's claim for payment for the uncompleted work.

**Step 4 — Negotiate the exit.** The contractor and the subcontractor negotiate the exit — the payment for the work completed (the subcontractor is entitled to payment for work done, even if he failed to complete), the cost of the recovery (the contractor's additional cost to complete is deducted from the subcontractor's payment), and the release of the subcontractor's retention (the contractor may retain a portion for the defects liability period).

**Step 5 — Replace the subcontractor.** The contractor finds a replacement subcontractor (or completes the work with direct labor) and proceeds. The replacement is more expensive than the original (the recovery cost), but the project must continue.

**Step 6 — Learn from the failure.** The contractor asks: what did the vetting miss? What signal was ignored? What relationship discipline was absent? The contractor who learns from each failure improves the vetting and the management; the contractor who does not learn repeats the failure.

### Principle 8: The Relationship Discipline — Regular Check-Ins, Fair Treatment, On-Time Payment

The strategic value of a long-term subcontractor relationship is, in the Egyptian market, comparable to the strategic value of a long-term client relationship. The relationship discipline has three components:

**Component 1 — Regular check-ins.** The contractor meets with each strategic subcontractor (the subcontractor the contractor wants to retain for 5+ years) at least quarterly, even when there is no active project. The check-in is a 30-60 minute conversation: how is the subcontractor's pipeline, what are the subcontractor's concerns, what is the contractor doing well or poorly. The check-in maintains the relationship when the work is not active — the contractor who only calls the subcontractor when he has work is the contractor who finds the subcontractor unavailable when the work arrives.

**Component 2 — Fair treatment.** The contractor treats the subcontractor fairly:
- The agreed price is honored; the contractor does not retroactively discount.
- The agreed scope is honored; the contractor does not impose scope changes without variation orders.
- The subcontractor's labor is treated with respect; the contractor's site manager does not abuse the subcontractor's electricians.
- The subcontractor's payment is on time; the contractor does not use the subcontractor's payment as a cash-flow management tool.

The fair treatment is not generosity — it is the investment that pays in the subcontractor's loyalty when the market tightens.

**Component 3 — On-time payment.** The on-time payment is the single most important relationship discipline. The subcontractor who is paid on time is the subcontractor who is available when the contractor needs him. The subcontractor who is paid late is the subcontractor who is "too busy" when the contractor calls next. The 30-day payment delay that saves the contractor 200,000 EGP in working capital costs the contractor 2,000,000 EGP in the next bid's subcontractor availability.

The contractor who builds the relationship discipline with 5-10 strategic subcontractors has, in the Egyptian market, a structural advantage that no competitor can replicate in less than 5 years. The relationship is the contractor's most durable competitive asset.

## Where experts disagree

**On whether to use a single subcontractor per scope or multiple subcontractors per scope.** Some contractors argue for a single subcontractor per scope (the relationship is deeper, the quality is more consistent, the management is simpler). Others argue for 2-3 subcontractors per scope (the competitive pressure keeps prices down, the contractor has backup if one fails). The user's correct posture is 2-3 subcontractors per major scope, with one preferred and the others as alternates. The single-subcontractor posture is a relationship risk (if the subcontractor fails, the contractor has no backup); the multiple-subcontractor posture is a management burden (the contractor must maintain the relationships with all of them).

**On whether to subcontract with the contractor's relatives or close friends.** The Egyptian cultural pattern of family-and-friend subcontracting is strong. Some contractors argue the family/friend subcontractor is the most reliable (the social bond enforces performance). Others argue the family/friend subcontractor is the most difficult to manage (the contractor cannot enforce the contract without damaging the social bond, and the subcontractor knows it). The user's correct posture is to use family-and-friend subcontractors only with the same written agreements as for any other subcontractor — and to be willing to enforce the agreements. The family subcontractor without an agreement is the relationship that ends in a courtroom or a severed family tie.

## Applied to the user's situation

Three specific applications:

**1. The strategic subcontractor portfolio.** The user should identify 5-10 subcontractors who will be the strategic portfolio over the next 3-5 years: 1-2 PVC conduit subcontractors, 1-2 cable pulling subcontractors, 1-2 lighting fixture installation subcontractors, 1 MT specialist subcontractor, 1 generator specialist subcontractor, 1 BMS/smart home specialist subcontractor. The portfolio should be built deliberately — not by accumulation, but by selection. The user should vet each one per the Principle 4 process, contract each one per the Principle 5 essentials, and maintain each per the Principle 8 discipline.

**2. The payment reserve.** The user should build a subcontractor payment reserve equal to 30-60 days of the subcontractor payments. The reserve is the firm's insurance against the payment cycle shock — when the principal delays the milestone, the reserve covers the subcontractor. The reserve is not working capital; it is a segregated cash account that is replenished as soon as the principal pays.

**3. The variation order discipline extended to subcontractors.** The user should require written variation orders from the subcontractors (the subcontractor who wants to charge extra must submit a written variation order before doing the work) and should issue written variation orders to the subcontractors (the contractor who wants the subcontractor to do extra work must issue a written order with a price). The discipline is symmetric — the contractor cannot demand it from the subcontractor without giving it to the subcontractor.
