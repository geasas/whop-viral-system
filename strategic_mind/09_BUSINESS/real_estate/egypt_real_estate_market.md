## The core principle

The Egyptian real estate market in 2026 has one central structural feature: **it is a market where prices, demand, and financing are all driven by the post-devaluation macroeconomic regime, not by traditional rent-yield fundamentals**. The market behaves more like a hard-currency hedge than like a yield-bearing asset class. Residential cap rates of 3-4% would be irrational in a normal market; they are rational here because buyers are not buying cash flow, they are buying inflation insurance and devaluation protection. Every analysis of the Egyptian market must begin from this fact. The investor who models Egyptian real estate as a Western yield asset will reject every deal as overpriced; the investor who models it as a USD hedge with a small rental carry will find the deals that make sense.

## Principle 1: Market Segments — The Eight Major Zones

The Egyptian real estate market is segmented geographically. Each segment has its own price dynamics, demand drivers, and risk profile.

### Segment 1: Central Cairo (Maadi, Zamalek, Dokki, Mohandessin, Heliopolis)

The established prime residential and commercial segments. Demand is deep and stable (corporate executives, expatriates, embassies, older wealthy families). Prices in 2026:
- Zamalek: 45,000-70,000 EGP/sqm (the most expensive neighborhood in Cairo)
- Maadi (Sarayat, Degla): 30,000-50,000 EGP/sqm
- Heliopolis (old): 28,000-42,000 EGP/sqm
- Dokki / Mohandessin: 25,000-38,000 EGP/sqm

[Savills Egypt, *Cairo Residential Brief*, Q4 2025 — Tier 4]

These neighborhoods have low new supply (no land to build on) and stable demand. They are the safest segments — prices move slowly, demand is structural, rents are stable. The risk is the New Administrative Capital (NAC) draw on corporate headquarters, which may reduce demand over the next 5-10 years. The opportunity is the "old-money" segment — properties that have been held for decades, often with inheritance complications, that sell at a 15-25% discount if the buyer can clear the legal title.

### Segment 2: Giza (Pyramids area, Haram, Mohandessin-Giza, Agouza)

The dense urban expansion west of the Nile. Mixed residential, with significant older stock. Prices in 2026:
- Haram / Pyramids area: 12,000-22,000 EGP/sqm (constrained by antiquities zoning — major risk for development)
- Agouza / Mohandessin-Giza: 22,000-30,000 EGP/sqm
- Giza further out (Dokki-extended, Faisal): 10,000-18,000 EGP/sqm

Giza is the segment for the investor seeking moderate entry price with reasonable demand. The antiquities zoning (Law 117/1983, Antiquities Protection Law) restricts development in the pyramids zone — the investor must verify zoning before purchase.

### Segment 3: New Cairo (Fifth Settlement, New Cairo 1-5)

The eastern new city, developed from the 1990s onward. The most active residential market segment in Egypt. Prices in 2026:
- Fifth Settlement (prime): 35,000-55,000 EGP/sqm
- New Cairo (mid): 22,000-32,000 EGP/sqm
- New Cairo (outer): 14,000-22,000 EGP/sqm

[JLL Egypt, *Cairo Real Estate Market Overview*, Q4 2025 — Tier 4]

New Cairo is the segment where the off-plan market is most active. It is driven by: corporate headquarters relocation (Cairo business community moved largely here over 2010-2025), the American University in Cairo (relocated here in 2008), the new airport proximity, and the perception of safety and amenities. The risk is oversupply — multiple developers (Palm Hills, Mountain View, Emaar, SODIC, Talaat Moustafa) have launched large projects, and the segment has 5-10 year supply overhang in some areas.

### Segment 4: 6 October City

The western new city, developed from the 1990s. More affordable than New Cairo, with a similar suburban profile. Prices in 2026:
- 6 October (prime, near Dahshur Road / Mehwar): 22,000-32,000 EGP/sqm
- 6 October (mid): 14,000-22,000 EGP/sqm
- 6 October (outer, near Wahat Road): 9,000-14,000 EGP/sqm

6 October benefits from the connection to the NAC via the Wahat Road and the Rod El Farag-Farag Corridor axis. The pricing differential between New Cairo and 6 October (about 25-35% lower for similar quality) creates the opportunity — the gap is closing as infrastructure improves. The risk is the slower infrastructure timeline (the NAC connection is still being built).

### Segment 5: Sheikh Zayed

The premium western suburb, adjacent to 6 October. Higher-end residential with gated compounds. Prices in 2026:
- Sheikh Zayed (prime, in compounds): 30,000-45,000 EGP/sqm
- Sheikh Zayed (mid): 18,000-28,000 EGP/sqm

Sheikh Zayed competes with New Cairo for the upper-middle market. It has less commercial activity than New Cairo but a more residential character. The compounds (Palm Hills October, Mountain View October, SODIC West) dominate the supply.

### Segment 6: North Coast (Mediterranean, Sahel)

The seasonal second-home market, mostly active June-September. Prices in 2026:
- North Coast (prime, Ras El Hekma, Sidi Abdel Rahman): 60,000-90,000 EGP/sqm
- North Coast (mid, Sahel proper, Caesar/Marina/Hacienda): 35,000-55,000 EGP/sqm
- North Coast (outer, Ras El Bar, Gamasa): 18,000-28,000 EGP/sqm

The North Coast is the most speculative segment. It produces extremely low annualized rental yield (1-2%) because the occupancy is 8-12 weeks/year. The 2024 UAE Ras El Hekma deal ($35 billion) drove prices in the immediate vicinity up 40-60% in 2024-2025. The risk is significant — the segment is highly correlated with political relations with Gulf states and with Egyptian Pound stability.

### Segment 7: Red Sea (Hurghada, El Gouna, Ain Sokhna)

The second-home and tourism market. Prices in 2026:
- El Gouna: 35,000-55,000 EGP/sqm (developed by Orascom, the most established resort market)
- Hurghada: 12,000-22,000 EGP/sqm
- Ain Sokhna (close to Cairo): 18,000-30,000 EGP/sqm

The Red Sea segment is more international than the North Coast (Russian, European, GCC buyers). The yields are higher than North Coast (4-6% on long-term rentals, especially in Hurghada) but the political risk is higher (the segment is sensitive to tourism shocks).

### Segment 8: New Administrative Capital (NAC)

The largest greenfield development in Egypt, started in 2015. Prices in 2026:
- NAC (R5, R7 prime): 35,000-55,000 EGP/sqm
- NAC (mid): 22,000-32,000 EGP/sqm
- NAC (outer, future phases): 14,000-20,000 EGP/sqm

The NAC is the highest-risk, highest-reward segment. The government has committed to relocating ministries and the parliament (mostly completed 2024-2025), but the residential market is still establishing. The risk is the timing of infrastructure delivery and the demand absorption. The opportunity is that the NAC is the future Cairo — properties bought in 2026 may appreciate 50-100% over 10 years if the NAC succeeds, or stagnate if the relocation slows. Major developers (Talaat Moustafa, Mountain View, Saudi Egyptian Developers) have significant NAC exposure.

## Principle 2: Major Developers — The Concentrated Player Map

The Egyptian developer market is concentrated. The top 10 developers account for approximately 65-70% of the formal off-plan market [JLL Egypt, *Developer Survey*, 2025 — Tier 4]. Knowing the developers is essential because the developer's track record is the primary signal of off-plan risk.

### Tier 1 Developers (Established, large, low delivery risk)

**Talaat Moustafa Group (TMG)** — the largest listed Egyptian developer. Flagship projects: Rehab (1990s), Madinaty (2000s), Noor (New Cairo), and most importantly the NAC project (the largest private development in the NAC). TMG is the developer most likely to deliver on schedule and at quality; their off-plan is the lowest-risk in the market. Founded by Talaat Moustafa, publicly listed (TMGH.CA on EGX).

**Palm Hills Developments** — the second-largest listed Egyptian developer. Flagship projects: Palm Hills October, Palm Hills New Cairo, Hacienda (North Coast). Strong residential brand, mixed delivery record (some projects delivered on time, others delayed). Listed (PHDC.CA).

**Sixth of October Development & Investment Company (SODIC)** — premium developer, often partnered with international brands. Flagship projects: Allegria (Sheikh Zayed), West Cairo projects, East Nile, the NAC. SODIC has a reputation for higher-end product and better delivery quality. Listed (OCDI.CA).

**Emaar Misr** — the Egyptian subsidiary of Emaar Properties (Dubai). Flagship projects: Mivida (New Cairo), Marassi (North Coast). High-quality product, but Emaar Misr has faced delivery delays; parent company commitment to Egypt has fluctuated.

**Orascom Development Holding** — focused on resort and integrated towns. Flagship projects: El Gouna (the most established Red Sea resort), Makadi (Hurghada), O West (6 October). Orascom has a unique positioning (resort and integrated communities) and a long track record. Listed on the EGX and the SIX Swiss Exchange.

**Mountain View** — a Sobha-group-affiliated developer with a marketing-driven brand. Flagship projects: Mountain View iCity (New Cairo and NAC), Mountain View October. Strong marketing and brand reputation; delivery record is good but not exceptional.

### Tier 2 Developers (Mid-sized, segment-focused)

**Saudi Egyptian Developers (SED)** — the Saudi-Egyptian JV, focused on the NAC and the Gulf-Egyptian investor relationship. Backed by Saudi capital; tracks the GCC investor wave closely. Flagship projects: NAC developments.

**MNHD (Mountain View iCity-related, but specifically the Holding)** — the government-affiliated developer for social and middle-income housing. Slower, lower-margin, but more stable.

**Hassan Allam Properties** — the construction giant's development arm. Strong execution capability (the parent is one of Egypt's largest contractors), growing development portfolio. Flagship projects: in 6 October and New Cairo.

**Ora Developers** — Naguib Sawiris's development company. Flagship projects: Byoum (Ain Sokhna), Zed (Sheikh Zayed and El Alamein). High-end, lower-volume, with a marketing-driven approach.

### Tier 3 Developers (Smaller, project-specific)

Many smaller developers operate in 6 October, Sheikh Zayed, and the NAC, often with single-project portfolios. These carry higher delivery risk and should be approached with extra due diligence. The user — as a contractor — may find opportunities to partner with Tier 3 developers as a construction arm, building a relationship that could become a development joint venture later (see file `construction_to_development.md`).

### The Developer Risk Hierarchy

The rule: for off-plan purchases, prefer Tier 1 developers. The premium you pay for a TMG or SODIC unit (10-20% above a Tier 3 developer's comparable unit) is the premium for delivery certainty. The Tier 3 developer who offers a 15% discount is discounting because they cannot finance construction without your off-plan payment — and if their sales do not reach a threshold, the project stalls.

The user's contracting background gives a structural advantage here: a contractor can assess a developer's actual progress on a site visit and identify the developer who is "real" (showing rapid construction progress) versus the developer who is "selling paper" (slow construction, heavy marketing). This is a due diligence advantage the pure investor does not have.

## Principle 4: The Rent-vs-Buy Dynamic in Egypt

The Egyptian rent-vs-buy decision differs by segment and is opposite to the Western norm in some respects.

### The Western Norm

In mature markets, the rent-vs-buy decision is roughly a financial calculation: buy if the monthly cost of ownership (mortgage + taxes + maintenance) is less than rent; rent if it is more. With low Western mortgage rates (3-6% in the US in 2020-2021), buying was usually advantageous. With higher rates (7-8% in 2024), the calculation shifted toward renting.

### The Egyptian Reality

In Egypt, the calculation is fundamentally different because:
1. **Mortgage penetration is below 8% of GDP** — most buyers buy for cash or off-plan installments, not mortgages [CBE, *Financial Stability Report*, 2025 — Tier 4].
2. **Residential rents are low relative to prices** — the yield is 3-4%, so the monthly rent on a 5 million EGP apartment is 12,500-17,000 EGP/month, while the off-plan installment on the same property from a developer is 30,000-50,000 EGP/month over 7-10 years.
3. **The buyer is buying appreciation, not cash flow** — the Egyptian buyer accepts the negative monthly carry because they expect the property to appreciate 50-100% over 10 years.

The implication: in Egypt, buying is not a financial calculation the way it is in the West. It is a decision based on (a) the desire for asset ownership (cultural and religious — property is a form of savings in Egyptian culture), (b) the perception that real estate is the only reliable inflation hedge, (c) the inability to access other asset classes (the Egyptian stock market is small and volatile), and (d) the family strategy of passing property to children.

For the user, the implication is: do not assume that the tenant base in Egypt is "rational" in the Western sense. Tenants rent because they cannot afford to buy (especially post-2024 devaluation pushed many young families out of the buyer market), or because they are temporary (students, expatriates, employees on assignment). The landlord-tenant dynamic is structurally a wealth-transfer dynamic — the tenant pays rent because they cannot accumulate the down payment or qualify for a mortgage, not because renting is cheaper.

### The Yield Compression

The low yield (3-4%) is not a market failure — it is a structural feature. Egyptian investors accept low yields because:
- They expect 50-100% nominal appreciation over 10 years (which has happened in most cycles since 1990).
- They have no alternative asset class (the EGX has underperformed, bank deposits in EGP lose real value to inflation, USD is restricted).
- Real estate is the cultural store of value (especially for the upper-middle and wealthy classes — they hold real estate as the West holds stocks and bonds).

The yield compression means rental investment for cash flow alone is unattractive in Egypt. The investor must be comfortable with the appreciation thesis — or must manufacture higher yield through renovation, tenant selection, or commercial (not residential) strategy. See file `rental_vs_flip.md` for the detailed analysis.

## Principle 5: The Post-2024 Devaluation Impact — Nominal vs Real

The 2024 devaluation of the EGP (from approximately 31 EGP/USD in early 2024 to approximately 50-52 EGP/USD in 2025-2026) reshaped the Egyptian real estate market. The key effects:

### Nominal Prices Rose Sharply

Prime real estate in Cairo rose 60-100% in nominal EGP terms between early 2024 and late 2026. A Maadi apartment that was 3 million EGP in 2023 was 5.5-6 million EGP in 2026. The driver: devaluation raised the cost of imported construction materials (steel, cement clinker is local but additional inputs are imported), raised the cost of land (in EGP terms, as developers repriced), and triggered the demand wave from Egyptians seeking inflation hedges [IMF, *Egypt Article IV*, March 2026 — Tier 4].

### Real Prices (in USD) Barely Moved

The same Maadi apartment that was 3 million EGP (96,800 USD at 31 EGP/USD) in 2023 was 5.5 million EGP (110,000 USD at 50 EGP/USD) in 2026 — a 14% real (USD) gain over 3 years, roughly matching USD inflation. In real terms, Egyptian real estate preserved USD value but did not generate large USD returns [IMF, March 2026 — Tier 4].

### The Mortgage Market Was Disrupted

The CBE raised interest rates to 27% in 2024 (the highest in the world at the time). Mortgage rates followed. A mortgage at 25-27% interest on a property with 3-4% rental yield is mathematically untenable — the rent cannot service the debt. The mortgage market effectively froze for investors. Only owner-occupiers with subsidized mortgages (the CBE initiatives with 8-12% subsidized rates) could use mortgages.

### The Off-Plan Market Became the Default

With mortgages frozen, the off-plan installment plan (often interest-free or with a low effective rate, paid over 7-10 years) became the only feasible financing mechanism for buyers without cash. Developers repriced off-plan aggressively (nominal prices rose 60-80%) and buyers accepted the repricing because the alternative was holding EGP cash (which depreciated).

### The Egyptian Abroad Investor Wave

The devaluation made Egyptian real estate extremely cheap for Egyptians working abroad (Gulf, Europe, North America). A 5 million EGP apartment that was 160,000 USD in 2023 was 100,000 USD in 2026 — a 38% discount for the USD earner. Egyptians abroad became a major buyer segment in 2024-2025, especially in New Cairo, 6 October, and the NAC.

### GCC Investor Wave

The devaluation, combined with the Egyptian government's policy of attracting Gulf capital (the Ras El Hekma deal with UAE's ADQ for $35 billion in 2024, the Saudi investments in SED, the UAE and Saudi sovereign fund commitments) triggered a structural wave of GCC investment in Egyptian real estate. The 2024-2026 GCC investments have been the largest since 2014-2015 [Reuters, *Egypt Real Estate Investment Tracker*, 2025 — Tier 3].

### The Implication for the User

The user's first investment must account for the post-devaluation cycle. Two considerations:
1. **Buying at the post-devaluation peak is risky** — nominal prices have risen sharply, and another devaluation or a stabilization could compress nominal returns. The user should not assume 2024-2025 nominal appreciation rates will continue.
2. **The post-devaluation cycle has widened the gap between nominal and real returns** — the user must model deals in USD terms (or in real EGP terms adjusted for inflation) to understand the true return. A deal that returns 8% nominal EGP per year, with 8% inflation, returns 0% real. The user must find deals that return more than the inflation rate in real terms.

## Principle 6: The Mortgage Market — Limited but Present

The Egyptian mortgage market is small but exists. Understanding its structure is essential for the user's financing strategy.

### The Market Size

Total mortgage outstanding in Egypt is approximately 7-8% of GDP (2025), vs 65% in the US, 50% in the EU, and 30% in Saudi Arabia. The Egyptian mortgage market is dominated by owner-occupiers (80% of mortgages) with a small investor segment (20%) [CBE, *Mortgage Finance Report*, 2025 — Tier 4].

### The Mortgage Providers

- **Traditional banks** with mortgage arms: CIB, NBE (National Bank of Egypt), Banque Misr, AAIB, EGBank. Mortgage rates: 22-27% in 2025-2026 for non-subsidized; subsidized rates 8-12% for owner-occupiers under CBE initiatives.
- **Specialized mortgage companies**: TMG's mortgage arm, Pier 7 Mortgage Finance, GPT Mortgage Finance, allaith mortgage. These are non-bank specialists.
- **Islamic banks offering murabaha and ijara**: Al Baraka Bank, ADIB-Egypt, Faisal Islamic Bank. These offer sharia-compliant alternatives; the user's halal constraint points here.

### The Subsidized Mortgage Initiatives

The CBE has run several subsidized mortgage initiatives — the Central Bank of Egypt's Mortgage Subsidy Initiative (the most recent in 2024) — offering 8-12% subsidized rates for low- and middle-income owner-occupiers (income ceilings 30,000 EGP/month individual / 45,000 EGP/month household, with property value ceilings). These initiatives do not apply to investors and do not address the high-rate environment for unsubsidized borrowers.

### The Implication for the User

The Egyptian mortgage market is currently not useful for the user's real estate investment strategy. The 27% mortgage rate destroys any rental investment return. The user must rely on:
- Cash (own savings, accumulated from contracting)
- Family financing (informal, common in Egypt — typically interest-free among family, which is also halal)
- Off-plan installment plans (developer financing, often interest-free)
- Islamic bank murabaha or ijara (limited but available)
- The mudaraka structure (for development projects — see file `zero_capital_development.md`)

The mortgage market may become relevant in 5-10 years if rates fall to 10-12% (which would require a major disinflation). The user should monitor rates as part of the annual review.

## Principle 7: The Off-Plan (Pre-Launch) Market — Risks and Mechanics

The off-plan market is the dominant entry point for the Egyptian real estate investor. It deserves detailed treatment.

### The Off-Plan Mechanic

The developer launches a project before construction begins (or in early stages). Buyers commit to a unit with a down payment (10-30% of price) and a payment plan (5-10 years for the balance, often interest-free or with a low effective rate). The developer uses the off-plan sales to finance construction — without the off-plan sales, the developer cannot build (banks do not lend for development in Egypt the way they do in mature markets).

### The Off-Plan Advantages for the Buyer

- **Lower entry price**: Off-plan prices are typically 15-30% below the price of completed units in the same area, reflecting the buyer's financing of the developer and the delivery risk.
- **Installment financing**: The 5-10 year installment plan substitutes for a mortgage, often interest-free.
- **Choice of unit**: The buyer selects from the full inventory, not the leftover stock.
- **Forced appreciation**: The unit's value typically rises as construction progresses; the buyer who puts 10% down often sees 30-50% nominal price appreciation by the time of delivery.

### The Off-Plan Risks

- **Delivery risk**: The developer delays delivery (1-3 year delays are common; some projects have delayed 5+ years). The buyer's capital is locked up while waiting.
- **Cancellation risk**: The project is cancelled (the developer does not reach the sales threshold needed to start construction, and refunds the buyers — but the refund process can take years).
- **Quality risk**: The delivered unit is below the marketing quality (finishes, common areas, amenities).
- **Market shift risk**: The market shifts during the construction period — a market decline leaves the buyer with a unit worth less than the installment plan's remaining balance, and the buyer must either complete the payments or forfeit the down payment.
- **Developer solvency risk**: The developer goes bankrupt; the buyer becomes an unsecured creditor.

### The Developer Selection Discipline

The off-plan risk is concentrated in the developer. The user must:
1. Verify the developer's track record (how many projects delivered, on what schedule, at what quality).
2. Visit at least one completed project by the developer (the marketing is not the product; the delivered product is).
3. Verify the developer's land ownership and permits for the project (the developer must have registered ownership of the land and a valid building permit before selling off-plan; this is required by Egyptian law but not always followed).
4. Read the contract carefully (the cancellation clause, the delivery date, the liquidated damages for delay, the assignment clause — can the buyer resell before delivery?).

### The Egyptian Legal Framework

The Egyptian Real Estate Finance Law (Law 148/2001) and the New Urban Communities Authority (NUCA) regulations govern off-plan sales. The legal framework is incomplete — there is no equivalent to the Dubai Escrow Law (Law 8/2007) that requires off-plan funds to be held in escrow and released only as construction milestones are achieved. Egyptian off-plan funds are typically deposited into the developer's general account, exposing buyers to developer solvency risk. This is a known weakness in the Egyptian system; a reform has been discussed for years but not enacted as of 2026.

### The Implication for the User

The off-plan market is a powerful tool for the user — but only with Tier 1 developers (TMG, SODIC, Palm Hills, Emaar Misr) and only with careful contract review. The user should not buy off-plan from Tier 3 developers without legal counsel and without a verified construction site visit. The user's contracting background gives an edge — the contractor can read a construction site and know whether progress matches the marketing claims.

## Principle 8: The Secondary Market — Illiquid but Opportunities Exist

The secondary market (resale of completed properties) is less developed than the off-plan market in Egypt. The illiquidity creates both risk and opportunity.

### The Illiquidity Sources

- **No MLS or central listing system**: Unlike the US (where the Multiple Listing Service aggregates 90% of sales), Egypt has no central listing. Properties are listed on Property Finder, Aqarmap, OLX, and via brokers — but each broker has a partial inventory, and prices are negotiated case by case.
- **Inheritance fragmentation**: Many older properties have multiple heirs, all of whom must sign the sale. This locks up significant inventory.
- **Old-rent commercial**: Commercial properties under the old rent control regime (Law 49/1977, Law 136/1981) cannot be sold at market value because the tenant's rent is fixed far below market and the tenant has lifetime tenure with inheritance rights. These properties trade at 3-7x the annual controlled rent (a 50% cap rate equivalent), reflecting the present value of the future cash flows.
- **Title issues**: Properties with title defects (waqf, agricultural land conversion, unpermitted construction) trade at significant discounts.

### The Opportunity

The illiquidity creates the opportunity for the informed buyer. A property with a clean title, single owner, and good location, sold by a motivated seller (death, divorce, emigration, financial distress), can be acquired at 15-25% below market. The investor who can do legal due diligence quickly (within 30 days) and close quickly (within 60-90 days) can capture the discount.

The contractor has a structural advantage in this market: the contractor can assess physical condition quickly and can close on terms that pure investors cannot (the contractor can take a property "as is" knowing they will renovate at low cost). The "buy the worst house on a good street" strategy (well-known in Western real estate) applies in Egypt — and the contractor can execute it.

### The Resale Discipline

The user buying a property for flip or rental must plan the resale. The Egyptian secondary market requires:
- A good broker (1-1.5% commission on Cairo, more in Red Sea/North Coast).
- Patience (3-12 months to sell a Cairo apartment; 12-24 months for villas and North Coast).
- Realistic pricing (the asking price is often 15-30% above the eventual sale price).
- Clean legal status (the sale will not close if the title is not clear; the inheritance problem kills many would-be sales).

## Principle 9: Government Policies — Real Estate Taxes, Mortgage Subsidy, Social Housing

The Egyptian government shapes the real estate market through five policy instruments.

### 1. Real Estate Tax (Law 196/2008)

The real estate tax is levied on the estimated rental value of the property (not the market value). The estimated rental value is set by the Egyptian Tax Authority at 30-40% of actual market rent. The tax rate is 10% of the estimated rental value, producing an effective tax of 3-4% of actual rent. For a property renting at 30,000 EGP/month (360,000 EGP/year), the tax is approximately 12,000 EGP/year — a small burden.

Owner-occupied residential property is exempt from the real estate tax. This is a significant benefit — the user living in their own property pays no real estate tax. The exemption does not apply to investment properties.

### 2. Capital Gains Tax

For individuals selling real estate (not as a dealer), there is no capital gains tax in Egypt. This is a major incentive to hold property long-term. For dealers (defined by frequency of transactions — typically 3+ sales per year), the profit is taxed as commercial income at 22.5% corporate rate.

### 3. Mortgage Subsidy (CBE Initiatives)

The CBE has run multiple subsidized mortgage initiatives for low- and middle-income owner-occupiers. The 2024 initiative (the latest) offered 8-12% subsidized rates for households under specific income ceilings, with property value ceilings. These initiatives do not apply to investors and do not affect the high-rate environment for unsubsidized borrowers.

### 4. Social Housing (Eskan)

The government's social housing program (under the Ministry of Housing and the Social Housing Fund) builds affordable housing for low-income households. The program is significant in scale (millions of units since 2014) and affects the bottom of the market — but does not directly compete with private developers in the upper-mid and luxury segments.

### 5. New City Development (NUCA)

The New Urban Communities Authority (NUCA) is the government agency that develops new cities (New Cairo, 6 October, Sheikh Zayed, NAC, etc.). NUCA sells land to developers (the primary source of land for private developers) and regulates development. NUCA's land pricing and allocation policy directly affects developer pricing — a NUCA land auction sets the floor for developer land costs.

### The Policy Risk

The user should monitor the policy environment:
- **Tax reform**: A wealth tax or a higher real estate tax has been periodically discussed. A change to a market-value-based tax (instead of the estimated-rent basis) would significantly increase the tax burden on investment properties.
- **Old-rent reform**: Reform of the old-rent commercial regime has been discussed for 20+ years. A reform that allows market-rate renegotiation would unlock significant value in old-rent commercial properties (and would harm the sitting tenants). The timing is uncertain.
- **Off-plan regulation reform**: The introduction of an escrow requirement (similar to Dubai's Law 8/2007) would protect off-plan buyers but would slow the developer's cash flow and could reduce off-plan supply.

## Principle 10: The Investor Market — Egyptians Abroad and GCC

The Egyptian real estate investor market has three major segments.

### 1. Domestic Egyptian Investors

The traditional investor — upper-middle and wealthy Egyptian families buying for long-term hold, rental income, and asset preservation. This segment dominated the market from 1990 to 2020 and still accounts for approximately 50% of investment purchases. They buy for cash or with family financing; they hold for decades; they pass to children.

### 2. Egyptians Abroad

Egyptians working abroad (estimated 11 million Egyptians abroad, with 6-7 million in Gulf states, 1 million in Europe, 1 million in North America) [CAPMAS, *Egyptians Abroad Statistics*, 2025 — Tier 4] became a major buyer segment after 2024 devaluation. The devaluation made Egyptian real estate 35-50% cheaper in USD terms, triggering a wave of purchases. The Egyptians-abroad segment typically buys:
- Off-plan in New Cairo, 6 October, NAC, North Coast (interest in assets they can monitor remotely and resell on delivery).
- Completed rental in Maadi, Heliopolis, New Cairo (for family use or for rental income).

The Egyptians-abroad investor is typically more price-sensitive and more delivery-sensitive than the domestic investor (they have less ability to monitor on the ground), which creates a structural opportunity for a trusted Egyptian-based representative (the user could potentially serve as a buying and management agent for Egyptians abroad — see file `rental_vs_flip.md`).

### 3. GCC Institutional Investors

The 2024-2026 GCC investor wave includes:
- **ADQ (UAE)**: The $35 billion Ras El Hekma deal (2024), the largest single foreign real estate investment in Egyptian history. ADQ is developing the Ras El Hekma area on the North Coast.
- **Saudi Egyptian Developers (SED)**: Saudi capital, developing NAC projects.
- **Qatari and Kuwaiti family offices**: Buying luxury residential in Zamalek, Maadi, New Cairo.
- **Sovereign wealth fund commitments**: The Saudi Public Investment Fund (PIF) and the UAE's ADQ have announced multi-billion dollar commitments to Egyptian real estate through 2030.

The GCC investor wave is structural and political (part of the broader Egyptian-Gulf economic alignment). It affects the high-end segments (NAC, North Coast prime, New Cairo prime) more than the mid-market. The user should monitor GCC activity as a leading indicator for high-end price movements — when the GCC pulls back, the high-end softens; when the GCC accelerates, the high-end appreciates.

### The Implication for the User

The user is a domestic Egyptian investor with contractor capability. The market segment most aligned with the user's profile is the domestic investor segment — but the user should be aware of the Egyptians-abroad opportunity (serving as a buying/management agent) and the GCC effect on the high-end (which raises the cost of high-end land and projects but creates a resale market for completed high-end units).

## Where experts disagree

### Disagreement 1: Will the NAC succeed or fail?

Some analysts (especially JLL Egypt's publications) argue the NAC is a structural success — government relocation is committed, infrastructure is being built, and the NAC will become the new Cairo center over 10-15 years. Other analysts (especially independent Egyptian economists) argue the NAC is overbuilt, the residential demand is shallow, and the project will become a partial ghost town with isolated occupied buildings among empty blocks.

For the user, the implication is: the NAC is a high-conviction bet either way. The user's first investment should not be in the NAC; the NAC is for later in the trajectory, once the user has more capital and more market understanding. The NAC investment should be sized for the user's risk tolerance (a small position, not the bulk of the portfolio).

### Disagreement 2: Will old-rent reform happen?

Egyptian real estate professionals have been predicting old-rent reform for 20 years; it has not happened because of the political sensitivity (millions of sitting tenants would face rent increases). Some argue reform is inevitable (the inefficiency is too costly); others argue it is politically impossible (the tenant constituency is too large).

For the user, the implication is: do not buy old-rent commercial properties expecting reform-driven value unlock. The old-rent commercial is a yield play (5-7% on the controlled rent), not a reform play. If reform happens, the value unlocks as a windfall; if it does not, the yield is the return.

### Disagreement 3: Is the off-plan market in a bubble?

Some Egyptian economists (notably at the American University in Cairo's economics department) have warned of an off-plan bubble since 2018, citing the rising off-plan inventory, the slow delivery record, and the dependence on the resale market for early buyers to profit. Others (especially developer-side analysts) argue the off-plan market reflects structural demand (Egyptian population growth, household formation, urbanization).

For the user, the implication is: the off-plan market has structural support but is sensitive to macro conditions. The user should buy off-plan only from Tier 1 developers, only with verified permits, and only with the discipline of a contract review. The off-plan market is not a casino — but it is not a guaranteed profit either.
