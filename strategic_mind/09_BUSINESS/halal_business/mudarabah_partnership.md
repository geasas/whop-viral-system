# Mudarabah Partnership — The Capital-Provider-and-Contractor Structure

## The Core Premise

The mudarabah (also called qirad in the Muwatta of Imam Malik, and muqaradah in some classical sources) is one of the oldest Islamic contract forms. It was practiced in the time of the Prophet ﷺ — the Prophet himself engaged in a qirad-like arrangement with Khadija before their marriage, where Khadija provided capital and the Prophet managed the trade caravans. The companions continued the practice after the Prophet's death — Umar ibn al-Khattab, as caliph, administered the public treasury's investments through qirad contracts with merchants. The classical manuals of all four Sunni schools treat mudarabah as a recognized and well-regulated contract.

The contract's distinctive feature — the feature that distinguishes it from both conventional debt and conventional equity — is the asymmetry of risk and labor. The capital provider contributes 100% of the capital and bears 100% of the loss (if the loss occurs without the mudarib's negligence). The mudarib contributes 0% of the capital and 100% of the labor and management, and bears 0% of the loss (again, absent negligence). The profits are shared in an agreed ratio — typically 50/50 or 60/40 in favor of the capital provider for higher-risk ventures, or 40/60 in favor of the mudarib for ventures where the mudarib's labor is the dominant value-creator.

The asymmetry is the contract's genius. The capital provider's loss exposure is limited to the capital contributed; the capital provider does not risk additional assets. The mudarib's exposure is limited to the labor contributed; the mudarib does not risk capital the mudarib does not have. The two parties are aligned in interest (both want the venture to profit) without the conflict that conventional debt creates (the lender wants the loan repaid regardless of the borrower's outcome) or the conflict that conventional equity creates (the equity investor wants the manager to take less risk than the manager's career incentives suggest).

For the Egyptian contractor, mudarabah is the natural structure for Stage 5 — the first real estate development project. The contractor (as mudarib) has the operational capability (project management, contractor relationships, sales) but lacks the EGP 3-10 million required for a small development. A capital provider (as rab al-mal) has the capital but lacks either the operational capability or the time to manage a construction project. The mudarabah aligns the two — the capital provider's capital meets the contractor's labor, the profit share motivates both, and the risk allocation matches the parties' capacities.

This file is the operator's manual for the mudarabah. It covers: the contract's definition and types, the conditions of validity, the mudarib's responsibilities, the capital provider's rights, the profit distribution (and loss absorption), the termination conditions, the Egyptian legal framework, and a practical application — the structuring of a mudarabah for the contractor's first real estate development.

## Section 1: The Mudarabah Contract — Definition and Types

### 1.1 Definition

The classical definition (Ibn Rushd, *Bidayat al-Mujtahid*, Book of Mudaraba): "The mudarabah is a contract in which one of the parties contributes capital and the other contributes labor, and the profit is shared between them in an agreed ratio, with the loss borne by the capital provider in proportion to his capital, and the laborer bearing no loss except the loss of his effort." The definition is agreed across the four Sunni schools, with minor terminological variations (Imam Malik uses "qirad"; the other schools use "mudarabah"; the meaning is the same).

### 1.2 Types

The classical jurists distinguish two types of mudarabah:

**Type 1 — Restricted (muqayyadah) Mudarabah**: The capital provider specifies the type of business, the geography, the time period, or the suppliers with whom the mudarib may transact. The mudarib is bound by the restrictions; transgressing them makes the mudarib liable for any loss resulting from the transgression. The restricted form is the dominant form in classical practice (the capital provider, having specified the venture, retains some control over its execution).

**Type 2 — Unrestricted (mutlaqah) Mudarabah**: The capital provider gives the capital to the mudarib without restrictions; the mudarib may invest in any halal business, in any geography, at any time. The unrestricted form is rare in classical practice (the capital provider's exposure to unspecified ventures is high) and is the form most associated with the Islamic banks' mudarabah investment accounts (the depositor gives capital to the bank without restriction on the bank's investments, in exchange for a share of the bank's overall profits).

For the contractor, the restricted form is appropriate. The capital provider specifies the project (a specific plot, a specific development plan, a specific budget, a specific timeline); the mudarib (the contractor) executes within the specifications. The restriction protects the capital provider from the mudarib's speculative temptation; the restriction also protects the mudarib (the contractor) by making clear what the mudarib's mandate is.

### 1.3 The Term

The mudarabah may be:
- **Single-project**: tied to the completion of a specific project (a development, a construction job). The mudarabah ends at project completion, with profit distribution and capital return.
- **Time-bound**: for a specified period (e.g., 24 months), regardless of project completion. The mudarabah ends at the period's end, with the venture's profit or loss calculated and distributed.
- **Open-ended**: until the parties mutually agree to terminate. The open-ended form is rare; the absence of an end creates ambiguity that the fiqh discourages.

For the contractor, the single-project form is appropriate for the first mudarabah. The specific scope (the development project) provides a clear termination point, a clear profit calculation, and a clear return of capital. The parties can negotiate a new mudarabah for the next project, with the experience of the first informing the terms.

## Section 2: The Conditions of Validity — Pillars and Conditions

The classical fiqh of mudarabah identifies the contract's pillars (arkan, sing. rukn — the essential elements without which the contract does not exist) and its conditions (shurut, sing. shart — the qualifications of the pillars). The four schools agree on the pillars; they differ on some conditions. The summary here follows the majority view, with notes on the disagreements.

### 2.1 The Pillars

**Pillar 1 — The Parties (al-'aqidan)**: The capital provider (rab al-mal) and the mudarib (the laborer/manager). Both must have legal capacity (baligh, rashid) — the contract of a minor or someone under guardianship is not valid without the guardian's permission.

**Pillar 2 — The Capital (al-mal)**: The capital contribution must be known in quantity, exist at the time of contract, and be in a form acceptable to the schools (the majority view: cash; some schools accept goods at market value, but the majority view is safer for the contractor).

**Pillar 3 — The Labor (al-'amal)**: The mudarib's management of the venture. The mudarib's labor is the consideration for the profit share; the labor must be real (the mudarib must actually manage the venture, not merely hold the title).

**Pillar 4 — The Profit Ratio (al-mabhah)**: The agreed profit-sharing ratio. Must be a percentage of profit (not a fixed amount, not a percentage of capital, not a percentage of revenue). The percentage must be agreed at the time of contract (not determined after the fact).

**Pillar 5 — The Offer and Acceptance (al-ijab wa al-qabul)**: The contract must be expressed in an offer (from one party) and an acceptance (from the other). The classical fiqh accepts both verbal and written expression; modern practice requires the written contract.

### 2.2 The Conditions of the Capital

The capital must satisfy four conditions (agreed across the four schools):

**Condition 1 — The capital must be known in quantity and type.** The capital provider cannot say "some of my money"; the capital provider must specify the amount and the currency (or, in the case of goods, the quantity and the description). The condition prevents disputes at the time of profit distribution.

**Condition 2 — The capital must exist at the time of contract.** A mudarabah on capital to be provided in the future is not valid (the future capital does not exist to be invested). The capital provider must transfer the capital to the mudarib's possession at the time of contract (or shortly after, in a specified period).

**Condition 3 — The capital must be in cash (in the majority view).** Goods at market value are accepted by some schools (the Hanafis, in particular), but the majority view (the Malikis, Shafi'is, and Hanbalis) requires cash. The contractor, structuring the mudarabah, should use cash to be safe across the schools.

**Condition 4 — The capital must be halal in source.** The capital provider's wealth must be from halal sources (trade, halal investment, halal inheritance). The contractor is not strictly required to investigate the capital provider's entire income, but the contractor should not knowingly accept capital from a haram source (e.g., capital from a known interest-based lending business). The condition protects the contractor from participating, even unknowingly, in the circulation of haram wealth.

### 2.3 The Conditions of the Profit Ratio

The profit ratio must satisfy three conditions (agreed across the four schools):

**Condition 1 — The ratio must be a percentage of profit.** A fixed amount ("EGP 200,000 to the capital provider, the rest to the mudarib") is not valid — the ratio must be a percentage (e.g., 50/50, 60/40). The condition prevents the capital provider from being guaranteed a return (which would make the contract a disguised loan). The condition also prevents the mudarib from being guaranteed a return (which would make the contract a disguised employment contract rather than a partnership).

**Condition 2 — The ratio must be agreed at the time of contract.** A ratio determined after the fact is not valid (the parties cannot know, at the time of distribution, whether the ratio they are applying is the one they would have agreed to at the time of contract).

**Condition 3 — The ratio must apply to net profit, not gross revenue.** The expenses of the venture (materials, labor, overhead) are deducted from revenue to arrive at net profit; the profit ratio applies to net profit. The capital is returned first; the profit ratio applies to the remainder. If the venture's revenue is less than the capital, there is no profit — the capital provider takes the remaining revenue (less than the capital contributed) as a partial return of capital, and the mudarib takes no profit share (and bears no liability for the capital shortfall, absent negligence).

### 2.4 The Conditions of the Mudarib's Conduct

The mudarib's conduct must satisfy four conditions (agreed across the four schools, with variations in detail):

**Condition 1 — The mudarib must act within the agreed restrictions.** If the mudarabah is restricted (Section 1.2 above), the mudarib must act within the restrictions. Transgressing the restrictions makes the mudarib liable for any loss resulting from the transgression.

**Condition 2 — The mudarib must act with the diligence of a prudent manager.** The mudarib is the "amin" (trustee) of the capital; the mudarib must manage the venture with the diligence that a prudent owner would apply to the mudarib's own venture. The mudarib's negligence (failure to inspect materials, failure to obtain required permits, failure to ensure safety) makes the mudarib liable for any loss resulting from the negligence.

**Condition 3 — The mudarib must not commingle the mudarabah capital with the mudarib's own capital.** The mudarabah capital must be held in a separate account, used only for the mudarabah venture, and accounted for separately from the mudarib's other ventures. The commingling of capital is a breach that, depending on the school, either voids the mudarabah or makes the mudarib liable for any loss.

**Condition 4 — The mudarib must not engage in prohibited transactions.** The mudarib may not use the capital for haram purposes (gambling, interest-bearing transactions, haram goods). Any profit from a haram transaction is not part of the mudarabah profit; it must be removed from the distribution and (per the dominant view) given to charity.

## Section 3: The Mudarib's Responsibilities

The mudarib (the contractor, in the contractor's mudarabah) has the following operational responsibilities (synthesizing the classical fiqh with the modern AAOIFI standards):

### 3.1 The Capital Preservation Responsibility

The mudarib must preserve the capital with the diligence of a prudent owner. The mudarib does not guarantee the capital against business losses (a loss that occurs in the ordinary course of the venture's business is borne by the capital provider), but the mudarib is liable for capital losses caused by the mudarib's negligence, breach, or transgression of the agreed restrictions.

For the contractor's mudarabah for a real estate development, the capital preservation responsibility means: the mudarib must obtain the necessary permits, must use qualified subcontractors, must inspect the materials, must ensure the safety of the site, must follow the agreed budget (with documented variations only for agreed scope changes), must maintain the project on the agreed timeline (with documented extensions only for agreed scope changes or force majeure).

### 3.2 The Management Responsibility

The mudarib must manage the venture actively, not passively. The mudarib cannot delegate the management to a third party without the capital provider's permission (in the majority view; the Hanafis are more permissive). The mudarib's active management is the consideration for the mudarib's profit share; the absence of active management voids the mudarib's claim to the profit.

For the contractor's mudarabah, the management responsibility means: the contractor (as mudarib) must personally manage the project, with the contractor's team, using the contractor's established operational systems (see `company_building.md` for the operational foundation). The contractor cannot "outsource" the management to another contractor; the contractor must be the operator.

### 3.3 The Reporting Responsibility

The classical fiqh requires the mudarib to report to the capital provider at the end of the venture (or at agreed intervals). The modern AAOIFI standards (Standard No. 13 on Mudaraba) require periodic reporting — typically quarterly, with annual audited financial statements.

For the contractor's mudarabah, the reporting responsibility means: the contractor provides the capital provider with a monthly written report (project status, financial position, variances from the budget, risks identified, decisions taken) and a quarterly in-person meeting. The reporting is the mudarib's defense against any subsequent claim of negligence or breach — the documented reporting shows that the mudarib managed the venture with diligence and transparency.

### 3.4 The Distribution Responsibility

At the end of the mudarabah (or at agreed intervals during a longer-term mudarabah), the mudarib must distribute the profits in the agreed ratio, after returning the capital to the capital provider. The distribution is documented in a written statement (showing the calculation of profit, the distribution to each party, the return of capital to the capital provider) signed by both parties.

For the contractor's mudarabah, the distribution responsibility means: at project completion (after the units are sold and the proceeds collected), the contractor calculates the profit (revenue from sales less capital less operating expenses), distributes the profit in the agreed ratio (e.g., 50/50), and returns the original capital to the capital provider. The distribution is documented in a written closing statement signed by both parties.

## Section 4: The Capital Provider's Rights

The capital provider (the rab al-mal) has the following rights (synthesizing the classical fiqh with the modern AAOIFI standards):

### 4.1 The Right to Specify the Venture

In a restricted mudarabah, the capital provider may specify the venture (the project, the budget, the timeline, the suppliers, the geography, etc.). The mudarib is bound by the restrictions. The capital provider's right to specify is the capital provider's protection against the mudarib's speculative temptation.

For the contractor's mudarabah, the capital provider's specification typically includes: the specific plot (the capital provider may require the mudarib to acquire a specific plot, or may acquire the plot and provide it as part of the capital), the specific development plan (the number and size of units, the quality standard, the design), the specific budget (with line items for land, design, materials, labor, sales, overhead), the specific timeline (with milestones for foundation, structure, finishing, handover), the specific sales approach (the listing price, the sales agent, the marketing budget).

### 4.2 The Right to Monitor and Audit

The capital provider may monitor the venture's progress and may audit the venture's books at any reasonable time. The classical fiqh recognizes this right (Ibn Rushd, *Bidayat al-Mujtahid*); the modern AAOIFI standards codify it. The capital provider may not interfere in the mudarib's day-to-day management (the management is the mudarib's responsibility and consideration for the profit share), but the capital provider may inspect, ask questions, and require reporting.

For the contractor's mudarabah, the capital provider's monitoring and audit rights typically include: a monthly site visit (the capital provider or a representative), a quarterly audit by the capital provider's accountant (in addition to the mudarib's own accounting), and the right to inspect any contract, invoice, or document related to the venture.

### 4.3 The Right to Profit in the Agreed Ratio

The capital provider's right to the agreed share of profit is the capital provider's primary right. The right arises only if there is profit (no profit, no distribution). The right is to the agreed percentage of net profit (not a fixed amount, not a guaranteed return). The right is realized at the agreed distribution date (project completion for a single-project mudarabah).

### 4.4 The Right to Terminate Under Specified Conditions

The mudarabah may be terminated by the capital provider under the conditions specified in Section 6 below. The capital provider's right to terminate is the capital provider's protection against the mudarib's breach or material change in circumstances.

### 4.5 The Right to Return of Capital (Less Any Loss)

At the termination of the mudarabah, the capital provider has the right to the return of the capital, less any loss (the loss being the amount by which the venture's net assets at termination fall short of the original capital). The capital provider's loss exposure is limited to the capital contributed; the capital provider has no claim against the mudarib's personal assets for the loss (absent the mudarib's negligence or breach).

## Section 5: Profit Distribution and Loss Absorption

### 5.1 The Profit Distribution

The profit distribution follows a specific order:

1. **Recover the operating expenses**: All expenses of the venture (materials, labor, subcontractors, equipment, overhead, sales costs) are deducted from the revenue to arrive at the gross profit.
2. **Recover the capital**: The original capital is returned to the capital provider. (In a single-project mudarabah, the capital is returned at project completion; in a longer-term mudarabah, the capital is returned at the agreed interval or at termination.)
3. **Distribute the net profit**: The net profit (gross profit less any expenses after the recovery of capital, though in the standard structure all expenses are deducted before capital recovery) is distributed in the agreed ratio.

Example calculation for the contractor's mudarabah for a 6-unit residential development:
- Capital provided by the rab al-mal: EGP 5,000,000.
- Revenue from sales of 6 units: EGP 9,000,000.
- Operating expenses (land, materials, labor, sales, overhead): EGP 7,500,000.
- Gross profit: EGP 1,500,000.
- Capital returned to the rab al-mal: EGP 5,000,000.
- Net profit: EGP 1,500,000 (after expenses; the capital return does not affect the net profit calculation — the capital return is a balance-sheet item, not an income statement item).
- Profit distribution at 50/50: EGP 750,000 to the rab al-mal; EGP 750,000 to the mudarib (the contractor).
- Total return to the rab al-mal: EGP 5,750,000 (the original capital plus the profit share).
- Total return to the mudarib: EGP 750,000 (the profit share; the mudarib's labor is the consideration).

### 5.2 The Loss Absorption

If the venture's net assets at termination are less than the original capital, the loss is borne by the capital provider. The capital provider receives the remaining net assets (less than the original capital); the mudarib receives nothing (the mudarib's loss is the loss of the labor invested).

Example: if the development's revenue from sales is only EGP 6,000,000 (the market turned down, the units sold at lower prices), and the operating expenses are EGP 7,500,000, the venture has a net loss of EGP 1,500,000. The rab al-mal's capital of EGP 5,000,000 has been reduced by the loss; the rab al-mal receives EGP 3,500,000 back (the original capital less the loss share, which is 100% for the rab al-mal). The mudarib receives EGP 0 (no profit; the mudarib's labor has been lost).

### 5.3 The Exception: The Mudarib's Negligence or Breach

The capital provider bears the loss ONLY in the absence of the mudarib's negligence or breach. If the loss is caused by the mudarib's negligence (failure to inspect materials that turned out to be defective; failure to obtain a required permit that resulted in regulatory penalties; failure to enforce safety standards that resulted in an incident) or breach (transgression of the agreed restrictions; commingling of capital; engagement in prohibited transactions), the mudarib is liable for the loss. The capital provider has a claim against the mudarib for the amount of the loss caused by the mudarib's negligence or breach.

The determination of negligence or breach is a question of fact, not a question of contract — the parties cannot, by contract, allocate the loss from the mudarib's negligence to the capital provider (any such contract term is void as contrary to the fiqh of mudarabah). The parties can, however, agree in advance on the standards of care, the reporting requirements, and the audit procedures that will be used to determine whether negligence or breach has occurred.

For the contractor's mudarabah, the contractor's protection against unjustified negligence claims is the documentation discipline: the contractor maintains a complete project file (see `company_building.md` on the operational foundation), with documented decisions, inspections, permits, and reporting. The documentation shows that the contractor managed the venture with the diligence of a prudent owner. The capital provider's claim of negligence, in the absence of specific documented failures, is weak; the contractor's documentation is the contractor's defense.

## Section 6: Termination Conditions

The mudarabah may be terminated by:

### 6.1 Completion of the Venture

For a single-project mudarabah, the completion of the project (the units are sold, the proceeds are collected, the books are closed) terminates the mudarabah. The profit is distributed, the capital is returned, the contract ends.

### 6.2 Expiration of the Term

For a time-bound mudarabah, the expiration of the term terminates the mudarabah. The venture's net assets are calculated, the profit or loss is distributed, the contract ends.

### 6.3 Mutual Agreement

The parties may mutually agree to terminate the mudarabah at any time. The venture's net assets are calculated (the work-in-progress is valued at the lower of cost or market), the profit or loss is distributed, the contract ends.

### 6.4 The Capital Provider's Unilateral Termination (the Majority View)

The majority view (the Malikis, Shafi'is, and Hanbalis, and the dominant modern view) holds that the capital provider may unilaterally terminate the mudarabah at any time, provided that the termination does not cause foreseeable loss to the mudarib's work in progress. The Hanafis disagree (they hold that the mudarabah, once begun, cannot be unilaterally terminated by the capital provider until the venture is complete or the term expires). The majority view prevails in modern Islamic finance practice (the AAOIFI standards follow the majority view).

For the contractor's mudarabah, the capital provider's right to unilateral termination is a risk the contractor must price into the profit ratio. If the capital provider terminates at a moment when the project is half-complete, the contractor's labor invested is lost (the contractor has no claim to a profit share on an uncompleted venture). The contractor's protection is: (a) a contract term that requires the capital provider to compensate the mudarib for the work in progress at the time of termination (the work valued at the lower of cost or market, with a profit share on the work completed); (b) a contract term that restricts the capital provider's right to terminate to specific circumstances (the mudarib's breach, the venture's failure, force majeure). The Egyptian Islamic banks that offer mudaraba typically include such terms in their standard contracts; the contractor's lawyer should ensure that the contractor's private mudarabah includes them as well.

### 6.5 The Mudarib's Unilateral Termination

The mudarib may unilaterally terminate the mudarabah, but with consequences: the mudarib's termination before the venture's completion is treated as a withdrawal from the venture, and the mudarib's labor invested is lost (no profit share on an uncompleted venture). The mudarib's termination is, in practice, rare (the mudarib has nothing to gain from termination); the more common mudarib termination is the mudarib's death or incapacity, which terminates the mudarabah by operation of law.

### 6.6 Death or Incapacity of Either Party

The mudarabah terminates on the death or incapacity of either party. The venture's net assets are calculated, the profit or loss is distributed, the capital is returned (or, in the case of the capital provider's death, returned to the capital provider's estate). The heirs of a deceased mudarib do not inherit the mudarib's role (the mudarib's role is personal, based on the mudarib's skill and labor); the heirs inherit only the mudarib's right to the profit share on work completed before death.

For the contractor's mudarabah, the contractor's death or incapacity is a critical risk. The contractor should have life insurance (takaful) that provides for the contractor's family in the event of death — the mudarabah's termination on the contractor's death means the family does not inherit the contractor's profit share on uncompleted work. The takaful coverage is a separate arrangement from the mudarabah, but the two should be coordinated (the takaful sum insured should be at least equal to the contractor's expected profit share on the current project).

## Section 7: The Egyptian Legal Framework for Mudarabah

### 7.1 The Civil Code

The Egyptian Civil Code (Law 131 of 1948, articles on partnership and agency) recognizes the mudarabah as a contract form. The Civil Code's treatment is general (it does not provide the operational detail of the classical fiqh), but the recognition is sufficient for the enforcement of a mudarabah contract in the Egyptian courts.

### 7.2 The Companies Law

The Egyptian Companies Law (Law 159 of 1981, as amended through 2024) does not specifically recognize the mudarabah as a company form, but it does not prohibit the use of the mudarabah within an LLC structure. The contractor's mudarabah is typically structured as: (a) a private contract between the contractor (as mudarib) and the capital provider (as rab al-mal), establishing the mudarabah terms; (b) the establishment of a special-purpose LLC (or a special-purpose vehicle within an existing LLC) for the specific project, in which the capital provider holds a non-managing equity stake (corresponding to the rab al-mal's capital) and the contractor holds a managing equity stake (corresponding to the mudarib's labor). The LLC structure provides the limited liability (the capital provider's loss exposure is limited to the LLC's capital; the contractor's loss exposure is limited to the contractor's personal assets in case of negligence claim); the private mudarabah contract provides the profit-share and loss-borne-by-capital-provider terms that the LLC statute alone does not provide.

The dual structure (LLC + private mudarabah contract) requires the lawyer to ensure that the two are consistent (the LLC's articles of association should reference the mudarabah contract for the profit-share and loss-allocation terms; the mudarabah contract should reference the LLC for the operational terms). The Egyptian Islamic banks' standard mudaraba contracts provide a template for this dual structure; the contractor's lawyer should use the template and adapt it to the specific project.

### 7.3 The Tax Treatment

The Egyptian tax treatment of mudarabah (as of the 2024 amendments — verify current treatment with a tax accountant) generally follows the LLC treatment: the mudarabah's profits are taxed at the corporate level (if structured through an LLC), and the distributions to the capital provider and the mudarib are taxed as personal income (subject to the dividend tax rate). The specific treatment depends on the structure (whether the mudarabah is a standalone contract or an LLC-embedded structure); the contractor's tax accountant should model both structures and recommend the more efficient one for the specific project.

### 7.4 The Regulatory Framework

The Egyptian Islamic banks (Faisal Islamic Bank, Al Baraka Bank, ADIB-Egypt, etc.) operate under the regulatory framework of the Central Bank of Egypt (CBE) and the Financial Regulatory Authority (FRA), with the sharia supervisory boards of each bank providing the religious compliance. The contractor's private mudarabah (with an individual capital provider) is not subject to the Islamic banking regulations, but the contractor's lawyer and accountant should ensure that the contract and its execution comply with both the Egyptian law and the AAOIFI standards.

## Section 8: Practical Application — Structuring a Mudarabah for the Contractor's First Real Estate Development

### 8.1 The Scenario

The contractor (in Stage 5, year 8 of the expansion arc) has identified a 300 m² plot in a residential district for a 6-unit residential building. The estimated development cost is EGP 6 million (land: EGP 2.5M, materials: EGP 2.5M, labor: EGP 0.8M, sales and overhead: EGP 0.2M). The estimated sales revenue is EGP 9 million (6 units at average EGP 1.5M each). The estimated net profit is EGP 3 million (50% return on capital). The contractor has EGP 1 million of retained earnings available for the project; the contractor needs to raise EGP 5 million.

### 8.2 The Capital Provider

The contractor identifies a capital provider: a successful merchant in the contractor's network, with EGP 10 million of savings, looking for a halal investment with a higher return than the bank's murabaha products. The merchant has no construction expertise and no time to manage a project; the merchant is interested in the contractor's project because of the contractor's track record and the merchant's trust in the contractor's integrity.

### 8.3 The Mudarabah Structure

The contractor and the merchant structure the mudarabah as follows:

**Type**: Restricted (muqayyadah), single-project.

**Term**: From contract signing to project completion (estimated 24 months), with extension possible by mutual agreement for force majeure.

**Capital**: EGP 5 million cash from the merchant (the rab al-mal), transferred to a dedicated bank account in the contractor's name (or, better, in the name of a special-purpose LLC established for the project). The contractor's EGP 1 million of retained earnings is contributed separately as the contractor's equity in the special-purpose LLC (this is the contractor's "skin in the game" — the contractor's equity contribution is at risk if the project loses money, but the contractor's equity contribution also earns a profit share proportionate to the contribution). The dual structure (mudarabah capital + equity capital) is more complex but more aligned: the contractor has some capital at risk (which motivates the contractor's diligence), and the merchant's mudarabah capital is the larger pool that the merchant bears at risk.

**Restrictions**: The merchant specifies: (a) the specific plot (the plot identified by the contractor); (b) the specific development plan (6 units, the design approved by the merchant); (c) the specific budget (the line-item budget agreed by the parties); (d) the specific timeline (24 months, with milestones); (e) the specific sales approach (the listing price range, the sales agent — the merchant may require a specific agent, or may delegate to the contractor).

**Profit ratio**: 50% to the merchant (the rab al-mal) and 50% to the contractor (the mudarib), applied to the net profit of the mudarabah portion of the project (the EGP 5 million capital). The contractor's separate equity contribution (the EGP 1 million) earns its own profit share proportionate to the contribution (in this simplified example, the contractor's equity earns 1/6 of the project's net profit; the mudarabah portion earns the other 5/6, distributed 50/50 between the merchant and the contractor).

In a simplified calculation (assuming the project's net profit is EGP 3 million):
- The contractor's equity portion (EGP 1 million of the EGP 6 million total capital, or 1/6) earns 1/6 of the net profit: EGP 500,000.
- The mudarabah portion (EGP 5 million of the EGP 6 million total capital, or 5/6) earns 5/6 of the net profit: EGP 2,500,000.
- The mudarabah profit is distributed 50/50: EGP 1,250,000 to the merchant; EGP 1,250,000 to the contractor.
- The merchant's total return: EGP 5,000,000 (capital) + EGP 1,250,000 (profit) = EGP 6,250,000.
- The contractor's total return: EGP 1,000,000 (capital) + EGP 500,000 (equity profit) + EGP 1,250,000 (mudarabah profit) = EGP 2,750,000.

The structure aligns the parties: the merchant's return (25% on the mudarabah capital in 24 months) is a fair return for a passive capital investment with a loss-absorption risk; the contractor's return (EGP 1.75 million in profit on EGP 1 million of capital and the contractor's labor) is a fair return for the labor and management contribution.

The mudarabah requires three documents:

1. **The Mudarabah Contract**: The private contract between the merchant and the contractor, specifying the type, term, capital, restrictions, profit ratio, termination conditions, and the dispute resolution mechanism. Drafted in Arabic by a lawyer familiar with Islamic finance; signed by both parties; notarized.

- The capital provider relationship requires regular, transparent reporting; the absence of reporting erodes the relationship faster than the presence of bad news.

- The mudarabah structure aligns the parties' interests — both parties want the project to succeed, and the alignment is the source of the relationship's durability.

## Where Experts Disagree

There is a genuine disagreement among contemporary Islamic finance practitioners on three questions:

**Question 1 — The mudarib's right to a management fee in addition to the profit share**. Some scholars hold that the mudarib's profit share IS the mudarib's compensation, and a separate management fee is not permissible (it would make the contract a disguised employment contract plus a profit share). Other scholars hold that a modest management fee (covering the mudarib's basic time cost) is permissible in addition to the profit share, provided the fee is disclosed and reasonable. The dominant view (and the one applied here) is the former — the mudarib's compensation is the profit share; if the mudarib wants a guaranteed income, the mudarib should structure a different contract (an ijara for the management services, plus a mudarabah for the profit share).

**Question 2 — The permissibility of the dual mudarabah-plus-equity structure**. Some scholars hold that the mudarib's separate equity contribution invalidates the mudarabah (the mudarib becomes a partner in the same venture, which changes the contract from mudarabah to musharaka for the equity portion). Other scholars hold that the dual structure is permissible, with the mudarabah terms applying to the mudarabah portion and the musharaka terms applying to the equity portion. The dominant view (and the one applied here) is the latter — the dual structure is permissible, with clear accounting separation between the two portions.

**Question 3 — The capital provider's right to unilaterally terminate in a single-project mudarabah**. The majority view (Section 6.4 above) holds that the capital provider may unilaterally terminate, with the consequences described. The Hanafi view holds that the capital provider may not. The dominant modern practice (AAOIFI Standard No. 13) follows the majority view, with the contract terms that protect the mudarib against unfair termination.

The contractor's religious advisor (a trusted scholar with finance expertise) is the reference for any specific question on the disagreements.
