## The Core Premise

Islamic commercial fiqh is not a list of prohibitions. It is a complete framework for understanding what makes a commercial transaction valid, what makes it void, what makes it defective, and what makes it disputed. The framework rests on three foundations: (1) the textual commands of the Quran and Sunnah (the prohibition of riba, gharar, and maysir; the commands of justice and mutual consent in trade); (2) the five maxims of Islamic jurisprudence (al-qawa'id al-fiqhiyya) that organize the application of the textual commands to specific cases; (3) the contract structures (the sales and partnership contracts) that the classical jurists developed as the halal alternatives to the prohibited forms.

For the contractor, the framework is not a constraint but an asset. The contractor who understands the framework can: structure any transaction to be halal (rather than improvising and risking an invalid contract); identify a problematic transaction before signing (rather than discovering the problem at the time of dispute); negotiate from a position of knowledge (rather than relying on the counterparty's drafting); and resolve disputes from the Islamic frame that the Egyptian courts and the Egyptian business community recognize.

This file is the operator's manual for the framework. It covers the five maxims, the prohibitions of gharar and maysir, the contract essentials, the major sales contracts (salam, istisna, murabaha, ijara), the major partnership contracts (musharaka, mudaraba), the Egyptian application (with the AAOIFI standards), and the ethical dimension. The mudarabah is treated in depth in `mudarabah_partnership.md`; the riba prohibition is treated in depth in `riba_free_finance.md`. This file is the broader frame in which those depth treatments sit.

## Section 1: The Five Maxims of Islamic Jurisprudence (Al-Qawa'id Al-Fiqhiyya)

The classical jurists identified five maxims that organize the entire body of fiqh — the maxims are the "shortcuts" that allow a jurist (and a contractor) to assess a new situation without researching every relevant primary source. The five maxims, in the canonical order (codified by Imam al-Suyuti in *Al-Ashbah wa al-Naza'ir* and by Ibn Nujaym in *Al-Ashbah wa al-Naza'ir*, on which the modern treatments rely):

### Maxim 1 — "Matters are determined by their purposes" (Al-umur bi-maqasidiha)

The maxim holds that the validity of an act depends on its purpose, not merely on its form. A contract that has the form of a sale but the substance of a loan (e.g., a "sale" in which the seller "sells" to the buyer with a deferred price, then immediately "buys back" at a lower cash price — a classical hiyal, or trick, to disguise a loan) is invalid because its purpose is the loan, not the sale. The form does not save the substance.

For the contractor, the maxim is the foundation of the "substance over form" principle in Islamic finance. The contractor does not ask only "is the contract document labeled a murabaha?" The contractor asks "does this contract actually function as a murabaha — i.e., is there a real sale of a real good at a real disclosed cost plus a real disclosed markup?" A contract labeled "murabaha" that does not have these substantive elements is not a murabaha, regardless of the label.

The maxim is also the foundation of the Islamic critique of the hiyal (legal tricks) — the classical jurists' opposition to the use of legal fictions to circumvent the prohibitions. The Hanafi school, in particular, developed a sophisticated literature on the hiyal (al-Sarakhsi, *Mabsut*, Book of Hiyal) and concluded that the hiyal are invalid because they violate the purpose of the prohibition, even if they technically comply with the form.

### Maxim 2 — "Certainty is not removed by doubt" (Al-yaqinu la yazulu bi-sh-shakk)

The maxim holds that an established fact (a certainty) cannot be set aside by a doubt. The application in commercial fiqh: a contract that is validly formed is presumed valid until it is proven invalid; a debt that is established is presumed owed until it is proven paid; a property that is owned is presumed owned until it is proven transferred. The party who challenges an established fact bears the burden of proof.

For the contractor, the maxim is the foundation of the contract documentation discipline. The contractor who documents every contract, every variation, every payment, every delivery, has established certainties that cannot be set aside by the counterparty's subsequent doubts. The contractor who does not document relies on the counterparty's good faith — and the counterparty's good faith is, in the absence of documentation, a doubt that can be set aside by the counterparty's claim.

### Maxim 3 — "The norm is that the matter is as it was" (Al-aslu baqa' ma kana 'ala ma kana)

The maxim holds that an established state continues until it is proven changed. The application in commercial fiqh: a partnership continues until it is proven dissolved; a debt continues to be owed until it is proven paid; a contract continues in force until it is proven terminated. The party who claims a change bears the burden of proof.

For the contractor, the maxim is the foundation of the continuing-obligation discipline. A contract the contractor has signed continues to bind the contractor until the contract is performed or terminated; the contractor cannot assume that the contract has "expired" or "been forgotten" simply because time has passed. The contractor who wants to terminate a contract must do so explicitly and in writing — the absence of an explicit termination leaves the contract in force.

### Maxim 4 — "Hardship begets facility" (Al-mashaqqatu tajlabu at-taysir)

The maxim holds that the law provides facility (exceptions, relaxations, alternatives) for cases of genuine hardship. The application in commercial fiqh: a contract that becomes impossible to perform due to a genuine hardship (a force majeure event, a fundamental change of circumstances) may be modified or terminated; a debtor in genuine hardship is entitled to a deferral of repayment (Quran 2:280); a traveler may shorten the prayers and break the fast.

For the contractor, the maxim is the foundation of the force majeure and hardship clauses in the contractor's contracts. A contract that imposes an obligation that becomes genuinely impossible (a project delayed by an unforeseeable regulatory change, a delivery prevented by a port closure) is not strictly enforced; the parties are expected to negotiate a fair accommodation. The maxim is also the foundation of the Islamic preference for settlement and negotiation over litigation (the Prophet ﷺ said, "Settlement among Muslims is permissible, except a settlement that makes the lawful unlawful or the unlawful lawful" — Sunan al-Tirmidhi, no. 1358, narrated by Aisha — grade: hasan sahih).

### Maxim 5 — "Custom is the basis of judgment" (Al-'adu huwa al-mu'tabar)

The maxim holds that custom (the prevailing practice in a given time and place) is the basis for interpreting contracts and determining obligations, in the absence of an explicit textual rule. The application in commercial fiqh: the meaning of contract terms that are not explicitly defined is determined by the prevailing custom; the prevailing practice in a trade is presumed to be part of the contract unless the parties have agreed otherwise; the prevailing market price is the reference for fair price disputes.

For the contractor, the maxim is the foundation of the Egyptian-context application of the fiqh. The Egyptian custom in contracting (the standard terms, the standard payment cycles, the standard dispute resolution mechanisms) is the basis for interpreting the contractor's contracts, unless the parties have explicitly agreed otherwise. The contractor who imports a contract from another jurisdiction (e.g., an FIDIC contract from the international construction practice) must adapt it to the Egyptian custom — the unadapted contract will be interpreted by the Egyptian courts in light of the Egyptian custom, which may produce results that surprise the contractor.

## Section 2: The Prohibition of Gharar (Excessive Uncertainty)

Gharar is the Arabic term for an excessive uncertainty or risk in a contract. The prohibition is rooted in the Hadith of the Prophet ﷺ: "The Prophet ﷺ forbade the sale of the pebble (hasah — a sale where a stone is thrown and the land where it lands is sold), the sale of gharar, and the sale of the miscarried camel" (Sahih Muslim, no. 1513, narrated by Abu Hurayra — grade: sahih). The hadith establishes the prohibition of gharar in commercial transactions; the classical jurists developed the prohibition into a comprehensive framework.

### 2.1 The Definition of Gharar

The classical jurists define gharar as "what is uncertain in its existence or its characteristics" (al-Sarakhsi, *Mabsut*). The definition covers two cases:
- **Gharar in existence (gharar al-wujud)**: the subject of the contract may or may not exist (e.g., the sale of a fish in the sea, the sale of a bird in the sky, the sale of an unborn animal whose sex and condition are unknown).
- **Gharar in characteristics (gharar al-sifah)**: the subject exists but its characteristics are uncertain (e.g., the sale of a hidden item, the sale of a quantity that is not specified, the sale of a quality that is not described).

### 2.2 The Levels of Gharar

The classical jurists distinguish:
- **Minor gharar (gharar yasir)**: a small uncertainty that is accepted by the parties and does not affect the validity of the contract. Example: the sale of a used car whose exact internal condition is not fully known to the buyer — the buyer accepts the uncertainty, the contract is valid. Most everyday contracts contain some minor gharar, and the fiqh does not require its elimination.
- **Major gharar (gharar fahish)**: a significant uncertainty that affects the validity of the contract. Example: the sale of an item whose existence is uncertain, the sale of a quantity that is not specified, the sale of a contract term that is open-ended. The major gharar invalidates the contract.

The modern AAOIFI standards and the contemporary jurists apply the distinction to modern contracts: a contract that contains major gharar is invalid; a contract that contains only minor gharar is valid. The determination of which is which is a question of degree and context — a small uncertainty in a small contract may be acceptable; the same uncertainty in a large contract may be excessive.

### 2.3 The Application to the Contractor

The contractor's contracts must avoid major gharar:
- **The subject of the contract must be specified**: the contractor's contract with a client must specify the scope of work, the materials, the labor, the timeline, and the price with sufficient precision that the parties know what is being exchanged. The contract that says "do the electrical work for a fair price" is invalid (gharar in price and scope); the contract that says "install 30 outlets, 12 light fixtures, 1 distribution board per the attached plan, for EGP 75,000, in 30 days" is valid.
- **The price must be specified**: the price must be a known amount (or, in the case of cost-plus, a known formula with a known cap or a known method of calculation). The contract that says "pay what is fair" is invalid; the contract that says "cost plus 20% markup, capped at EGP 100,000" is valid.
- **The timeline must be specified**: the timeline must be a known date (or a known period from a known event). The contract that says "finish when you can" is invalid; the contract that says "finish by 1 March 2027" or "finish within 60 days of the contract signing" is valid.

The contractor's contracts typically include minor gharar in the form of small uncertainties (the exact condition of a wall before rewiring, the exact route of a cable through a finished ceiling, the exact hours of labor required for an unusual site condition). The minor gharar is acceptable, provided the contract includes a clear mechanism for handling the unexpected (a variation clause, a site-condition clause, a force majeure clause).

## Section 3: The Prohibition of Maysir (Gambling)

Maysir is the Arabic term for gambling — the classical reference is to the pre-Islamic Arabian game of chance (maysir, named after the practice of dividing a slaughtered camel's meat by drawing arrows). The prohibition is rooted in the Quran: "O you who believe, indeed, intoxicants, gambling, stone altars, and divining arrows are an abomination of Satan's work, so avoid it that you may be successful" (al-Ma'idah 5:90).

### 3.1 The Definition of Maysir

The classical jurists define maysir as "the contract in which the gain or loss of either party depends on a chance event" (Ibn Rushd, *Bidayat al-Mujtahid*). The definition covers:
- **Pure gambling**: a contract in which one party pays and the other party pays, with the gain depending on a chance event (a wager, a bet, a lottery ticket).
- **Disguised gambling**: a contract in which the form is a sale but the substance is a wager (e.g., the classical contract of "if I sell this for X, you pay me Y; if I sell for less, I pay you" — the parties' gains depend on a future market event, which is maysir).
- **Insurance contracts with a wager element**: a contract in which the insured pays a known premium for an unknown payout triggered by a chance event (the conventional insurance contract — see `riba_free_finance.md` Section 3.4).

### 3.2 The Application to the Contractor

The contractor's contracts must avoid maysir:
- The contractor does not enter into wagering contracts (a contractor does not bet on the outcome of a project, on the price of materials, on the success of a development).
- The contractor does not enter into contracts with a wager element (a contract that pays the contractor a bonus if the project is delivered before a date and penalizes the contractor if the project is delivered after the date is, in the strict reading, a wager on the date — though the modern jurists generally accept liquidated damages and bonus clauses as legitimate contract terms, provided they reflect a genuine estimate of the loss or gain, not a wager).
- The contractor uses takaful (Islamic insurance — see `riba_free_finance.md` Section 3.4) rather than conventional insurance for property and liability coverage.

The maysir prohibition is less central to the contractor's daily operations than the riba and gharar prohibitions (the contractor's primary contracts are sales of services and goods, which do not typically involve maysir). The prohibition is most relevant for the contractor's insurance decisions and for any financial structures the contractor considers (e.g., the contractor does not invest in speculative derivatives that have a wager element).

## Section 4: The Contract Essentials

A valid Islamic contract requires four essentials (agreed across the four schools, with minor variations):

### 4.1 The Parties (al-'aqidan)

The parties must have legal capacity (ahl, ahliyyah) — they must be of age (baligh), of sound mind (rashid), and acting without duress (ghayr makrah). A contract by a minor, by an insane person, or by a person under duress is not valid (or, in some cases, is voidable at the option of the party lacking capacity).

For the contractor: the contractor's contract with a client requires the client (or the client's authorized representative) to have legal capacity. The contractor's contract with a partner requires the partner to have legal capacity. The contractor's contract with a minor (e.g., a young heir to a property) requires the minor's guardian.

### 4.2 The Offer and Acceptance (al-ijab wa al-qabul)

The contract requires an offer (from one party) and an acceptance (from the other party), on the same subject, at the same meeting (in the majority view) or within a reasonable time (in some modern views). The offer and acceptance may be verbal or written; modern practice requires the written contract.

For the contractor: the contractor's contract requires a clear offer (the contractor's bid or the client's request) and a clear acceptance (the client's award or the contractor's acceptance of the client's terms). The parties' mutual agreement is the contract; the written document is the evidence of the agreement (and, in modern practice, the standard form of the agreement). The "contract" that is verbally agreed but not written is valid in fiqh but difficult to enforce — the contractor's discipline is the written contract.

### 4.3 The Subject (al-ma'qud 'alayh)

The subject of the contract must be:
- **Exist (or be capable of existing at the time of delivery)**: the sale of a non-existent item is gharar (Section 2 above) and invalid. The exception is the salam contract (the sale of a described item to be delivered in the future, paid in advance) and the istisna contract (the manufacture of an item to be delivered in the future), which are valid specifically for future-existing items.
- **Be owned by the seller**: the seller cannot sell what the seller does not own. The sale of an item the seller does not own is invalid (the seller cannot transfer ownership the seller does not have). The exception is the salam contract (the seller does not own the agricultural product at the time of contract — the seller will produce or acquire the product for delivery).
- **Be capable of delivery**: the subject must be capable of being delivered to the buyer. The sale of an item that cannot be delivered (a fish in the sea, a bird in the sky) is gharar and invalid.
- **Be known to the parties**: the subject must be specified to the parties (the quantity, the quality, the description). The sale of an unspecified item is gharar and invalid.
- **Be valuable in Islamic law**: the subject must be something that is halal to possess and use. The sale of wine, pork, idols, or other haram items is invalid (the contract is void; the price, if paid, must be returned; the item, if delivered, must be returned).

For the contractor: the contractor's contracts must specify the subject (the materials, the labor, the scope of work, the timeline) with sufficient precision that the parties know what is being exchanged (Section 2.3 above). The contractor's contracts must be for halal subjects (the contractor does not contract to install electrical systems for a haram establishment — a bar, a casino, etc. — though the modern jurists differ on the degree to which the contractor is responsible for the client's end use of the contractor's work; the conservative view holds that the contractor should not contract for a known haram end use).

### 4.4 The Consideration (al-'iwad)

The consideration (the price in a sale, the wages in an employment contract, the profit share in a partnership) must be:
- **Known**: the consideration must be specified (or, in the case of a cost-plus contract, the formula must be specified). The contract that says "pay what is fair" is invalid (gharar).
- **Halal**: the consideration must be a halal item (in a sale, the price must be a halal currency or halal good; in an employment contract, the wages must be halal currency or halal goods; the consideration cannot be a haram item such as wine or pork).
- **Symmetric**: the consideration must be roughly equivalent to the subject (in a sale, the price must reflect the value of the good; a sale at a grossly inadequate price may be voidable as ghubn, deceit, in some schools).

For the contractor: the contractor's contracts must specify the price (or the formula), in a halal currency (Egyptian pounds, or a halal foreign currency), with a halal payment mechanism (no interest, no wager). The contractor's contracts should reflect the fair value of the work (the contractor who quotes grossly below cost to win a job and then discovers that the contract is unprofitable has, in some schools, a claim for ghubn — though the practical application is limited).

## Section 5: The Sales Contracts

The Islamic commercial fiqh developed a number of sales contract structures that serve specific purposes. The four most relevant to the contractor:

### 5.1 Salam (Forward Sale of Fungible Goods)

A salam contract is a sale in which the buyer pays in full in advance for a described fungible good to be delivered at a future date. The conditions (agreed across the four schools, with minor variations):
- The good must be fungible (interchangeable units of the same type — wheat, dates, standardized commodities). A non-fungible good (a specific car, a specific piece of land) cannot be the subject of a salam.
- The good must be described in detail (the type, the quality, the quantity) so that the parties know what is being exchanged.
- The delivery date must be specified.
- The price must be paid in full at the time of contract (the majority view; some schools accept partial payment with the rest at delivery, but the majority view is safer).
- The good must be capable of being delivered at the delivery date (the seller must be able to produce or acquire the good by the date).

For the contractor, salam is rarely used directly (the contractor's services are non-fungible). The salam structure is, however, the foundation for the contractor's understanding of istisna (the next contract) and may be relevant for the contractor's later expansion (e.g., the contractor may, in a real estate development, use a salam-like structure for the sale of pre-fabricated components).

### 5.2 Istisna (Manufacturing Contract)

An istisna contract is a contract in which a party commissions the manufacture of a described item, with payment in stages and delivery at a future date. The conditions:
- The item must be a manufactured item (a custom-built item — a building, a custom electrical panel, a custom distribution board). The istisna structure is the dominant Islamic contract for construction (the buyer commissions the contractor to build a described building, paying in stages against milestones, with delivery at a fixed date).
- The item must be described in detail (the design, the materials, the quality standard, the timeline).
- The price must be specified (a fixed price, or a fixed formula with a cap).
- The payment may be in stages (unlike salam, where the price is paid in full in advance — the istisna allows staged payment, which is essential for construction).
- The manufacturer (the contractor) must be capable of manufacturing the item.

For the contractor, istisna is the primary structure for the contractor's contracts with clients:
- For a custom-built electrical panel: the client commissions the contractor to build the panel per the specifications, paying in stages (advance, materials purchased, panel assembled, panel delivered), with delivery at a fixed date. The istisna structure applies.
- For a custom-built distribution board: same as above.
- For the contractor's real estate development in Stage 5: the buyers of the units commission the contractor to build the units per the specifications, paying in stages (advance, foundation, structure, finishing, handover), with delivery at a fixed date. The istisna structure is the halal form of off-plan sales — see `starting_broke.md` Section 4 (Model 4) and `riba_free_finance.md` Section 4.6.

### 5.3 Murabaha (Cost-Plus Sale)

A murabaha contract is a sale in which the seller discloses the cost of the good to the buyer, adds an agreed markup, and sells at the cost-plus-markup price, with payment either immediate or deferred. The conditions:
- The cost must be disclosed (the seller must tell the buyer the actual cost of the good). The disclosure is the defining feature of murabaha (without it, the contract is a regular sale, not a murabaha).
- The markup must be agreed (a fixed amount or a fixed percentage of the cost).
- The good must be owned by the seller at the time of contract (the seller cannot sell what the seller does not own — the seller must first acquire the good, then sell it to the buyer).
- The price (cost plus markup) may be paid immediately or deferred (the deferred payment is the standard form of murabaha in modern Islamic finance — the buyer pays the cost-plus-markup price in installments over an agreed period).

For the contractor, murabaha is the structure for the contractor's purchases of materials:
- The contractor buys materials from a supplier under a murabaha: the supplier discloses the cost (e.g., EGP 100 per meter of cable), adds an agreed markup (e.g., 10%), and sells to the contractor at EGP 110 per meter, with payment deferred 60 days. The structure is a halal form of supplier credit (see `capital_generation.md` Section 6).
- The contractor buys equipment or vehicles through a bank-mediated murabaha: the Islamic bank buys the equipment from the supplier, sells it to the contractor at cost plus an agreed markup, with payment deferred over an agreed period. The structure is a halal form of equipment financing (the alternative to a conventional interest-bearing loan).

The murabaha structure is the most commonly used Islamic finance product in the Egyptian market — the Egyptian Islamic banks offer murabaha for materials, equipment, and vehicles. The contractor's use of murabaha is straightforward once the structure is understood.

### 5.4 Ijara (Lease)

An ijara contract is a lease in which the lessor owns the asset, rents it to the lessee at an agreed rental, and (optionally) transfers ownership to the lessee at the end of the lease term. The conditions:
- The asset must be owned by the lessor at the time of contract (the lessor cannot lease what the lessor does not own).
- The asset must be known and specified (the description, the condition, the location).
- The rental must be specified (a fixed amount per period, or a fixed formula with a cap).
- The lease term must be specified.
- The asset must be capable of being used for the purpose of the lease (the asset must be in working condition, fit for the intended use).
- The lessor is responsible for the major maintenance of the asset (the lessee is responsible for the routine operating costs — fuel, consumables, daily care).

For the contractor, ijara is the structure for the contractor's equipment financing:
- The contractor leases a vehicle through an Islamic ijara: the Islamic bank (or a leasing company) buys the vehicle, leases it to the contractor at an agreed monthly rental, with ownership transferring to the contractor at the end of the lease term at an agreed price (often a nominal price, or a price equal to the final installment). The structure is a halal form of vehicle financing (the alternative to a conventional interest-bearing loan — see `riba_free_finance.md` Section 3.2).
- The contractor leases equipment (a generator, a scissor lift, a specialized tool) through an Islamic ijara, on a short-term or long-term basis. The short-term lease (a daily or weekly rental) is the standard form of equipment rental in the Egyptian market; the long-term lease (with ownership transfer) is the Islamic alternative to equipment financing.

The ijara structure is also the foundation for the Islamic home finance products (the "ijara muntahia bittamleek" or "lease ending in ownership" — the Islamic alternative to a conventional mortgage). The contractor's eventual purchase of a personal residence (or, more relevant, the contractor's eventual commercial property acquisition for the company's office or warehouse) can be structured through an ijara muntahia bittamleek.

## Section 6: The Partnership Contracts

The Islamic commercial fiqh developed two main partnership contracts:

### 6.1 Musharaka (Partnership in Capital and Labor)

A musharaka is a partnership in which two or more parties contribute capital and labor, and share in profit and loss in agreed proportions. The conditions (treated in detail in `capital_generation.md` Section 3 and `riba_free_finance.md` Section 4.1):
- The capital contribution of each partner must be known.
- The profit-sharing ratio must be agreed (it can differ from the capital ratio — a partner contributing 30% of capital can take 40% of profit if the partner also contributes labor).
- The loss must be borne in proportion to capital contribution (not in proportion to the agreed profit ratio — a partner contributing 30% of capital bears 30% of any loss).
- The management can be by one or all partners; if by one, the others are silent partners.
- The term can be fixed (a specific project musharaka, dissolved at project completion) or continuous (an ongoing business musharaka).
- The partnership agreement must be in writing.

For the contractor, musharaka is the structure for the contractor's partnership with another contractor (e.g., a general contractor who wants the contractor's electrical capability for a specific project, or for an ongoing general contracting business). The detailed application is in `capital_generation.md` and `company_building.md`.

### 6.2 Mudaraba (Partnership of Capital and Labor)

A mudaraba is a partnership in which one party (the rab al-mal) contributes all the capital, the other party (the mudarib) contributes all the labor and management, and profits are shared in an agreed ratio; losses are borne entirely by the capital provider (except in the case of the mudarib's negligence or breach). The mudaraba is the subject of the depth treatment in `mudarabah_partnership.md`; the summary here is brief.

For the contractor, mudaraba is the structure for the contractor's expansion into real estate development in Stage 5 — the contractor (as mudarib) partners with a capital provider (as rab al-mal) for a specific development project. The detailed application is in `mudarabah_partnership.md` Section 8.

## Section 7: The Egyptian Application — and the AAOIFI Standards

The Egyptian legal system applies the Islamic commercial fiqh through a specific set of legal instruments:

### 7.1 The Egyptian Civil Code

The Egyptian Civil Code (Law 131 of 1948) was drafted by the Egyptian jurist Abd al-Razzaq al-Sanhuri, who explicitly incorporated the Islamic fiqh into the Civil Code's provisions on contract, sale, partnership, and lease. The Civil Code's provisions on:
- Sales (Articles 418-446): general sales contracts.
- Salam (Articles 733-744): the forward sale of fungible goods.
- Construction contracts (Articles 651-681): the istisna-like construction contract.
- Partnership (Articles 505-557): the general partnership and limited partnership provisions — the framework that accommodates both musharaka and mudaraba.
- Lease (Articles 588-638): the general lease provisions — the framework that accommodates ijara.

The Civil Code's treatment is general (it does not provide the operational detail of the classical fiqh), but the recognition is sufficient for the enforcement of the Islamic contract structures in the Egyptian courts.

### 7.2 The Companies Law

The Egyptian Companies Law (Law 159 of 1981, as amended through 2024) provides the LLC and joint stock company structures that house the Islamic partnership contracts. The detailed application is in `company_building.md` and `mudarabah_partnership.md` Section 7.

### 7.3 The AAOIFI Standards

The Accounting and Auditing Organization for Islamic Financial Institutions (AAOIFI) is the international body that develops standards for Islamic finance. The AAOIFI standards are not Egyptian law, but they are widely followed by Egyptian Islamic banks and by Egyptian practitioners who structure Islamic finance contracts. The most relevant AAOIFI standards for the contractor:

- Shari'ah Standard No. 8: Istisna and Parallel Istisna (the standard for the construction contract structure the contractor uses for clients).
- Shari'ah Standard No. 9: Ijara and Ijara Muntahia Bittamleek (the standard for the lease structure the contractor uses for equipment financing).
- Shari'ah Standard No. 11: Salam and Parallel Salam (the standard for the forward sale structure, less relevant to the contractor but foundational).
- Shari'ah Standard No. 12: Sharika (Musharaka) and Modern Corporations (the standard for the partnership structure).
- Shari'ah Standard No. 13: Mudaraba (the standard for the capital-provider-and-contractor structure treated in `mudarabah_partnership.md`).
- Shari'ah Standard No. 26: Takaful (the standard for the Islamic insurance structure).

The contractor's lawyer (when drafting the contractor's Islamic finance contracts) and the contractor's accountant (when auditing the contracts' compliance) should follow the AAOIFI standards as the operational reference, in addition to the Egyptian Civil Code and Companies Law.

### 7.4 The Egyptian Islamic Banks

The Egyptian Islamic banks (Faisal Islamic Bank of Egypt, Al Baraka Bank, ADIB-Egypt, Abu Dhabi Islamic Bank — Egypt, Al Ahli Islamic Bank, etc.) operate under the regulatory framework of the Central Bank of Egypt (CBE) and the Financial Regulatory Authority (FRA), with the sharia supervisory boards of each bank providing the religious compliance. The banks offer the standard Islamic finance products (murabaha, ijara, mudaraba, musharaka) that the contractor can use. ## Section 8: The Ethical Dimension — Justice, Transparency, Mutual Consent

The Islamic commercial fiqh is not merely a set of contract structures. It is a framework for commercial ethics — the discipline of conducting business in a manner that the Quran and Sunnah command. The three foundational ethical principles:

### 8.1 Justice ('adl)

The Quran commands justice in commercial transactions: "And give full measure when you measure, and weigh with an even balance. That is the best [way] and best in result" (al-Isra 17:35). The command is to give the buyer the full measure of what was agreed; the command extends to all aspects of the contract (the contractor's materials must meet the agreed quality; the contractor's labor must meet the agreed standard; the contractor's timeline must be honored; the contractor's price must reflect the agreed scope).

For the contractor, justice is the foundation of the contractor's reputation (see `market_domination.md` Section 2 on the reputation flywheel). The contractor who gives the client the full measure — who delivers the agreed quality, the agreed timeline, the agreed scope — is the contractor whose reputation compounds. The contractor who shortchanges (in materials, in quality, in timeline) is the contractor whose reputation erodes.

### 8.2 Transparency (bayan)

The Quran commands transparency in commercial transactions: "O you who believe, do not consume one another's wealth unjustly, but only [in lawful trade] by mutual consent. And do not kill yourselves [or one another]. Indeed, Allah is to you ever Merciful" (al-Nisa 4:29). The "mutual consent" requires that the parties know what they are agreeing to — the contract must be transparent (the subject, the price, the timeline, the conditions must all be disclosed to both parties).

For the contractor, transparency is the foundation of the contractor's relationship with clients, suppliers, and partners. The contractor who discloses the cost (in a murabaha), the conditions (in a construction contract), the risks (in a mudarabah), the disputes (in any contract) is the contractor whose relationships endure. The contractor who hides (the cost, the conditions, the risks, the disputes) is the contractor whose relationships erode.

### 8.3 Mutual Consent (taradi)

The Quran commands mutual consent in commercial transactions: "Trade is only by mutual consent" (al-Nisa 4:29 — same verse as above, with the emphasis on the consent). The contract must be entered by both parties' free consent — the contract entered under duress, or by fraud, or by misrepresentation is voidable at the option of the wronged party.

For the contractor, mutual consent is the foundation of the contractor's contract practice. The contractor does not pressure a client to sign (no "sign now or the price goes up"; no "sign now or I cannot hold the schedule"). The contractor does not misrepresent (no inflated qualifications, no hidden defects, no false claims about materials or labor). The contractor does not coerce (no threat to walk off a project unless the client agrees to a variation, no threat to withhold the certificate of completion unless the client pays a disputed invoice). The contractor's contract practice is the practice of mutual consent — and the practice is the foundation of the contractor's enforceable contracts (a contract entered without mutual consent is voidable, and the contractor's reliance on it is fragile).

The three ethical principles — justice, transparency, mutual consent — are not decorative; they are operational. The contractor who treats them as the foundation of the contractor's contract practice is the contractor whose contracts are enforceable, whose reputation compounds, and whose business endures. The contractor who treats them as optional is the contractor whose contracts are voidable, whose reputation erodes, and whose business is fragile. The Islamic commercial fiqh, in this reading, is the systematic discipline of conducting business in a manner that the Quran and Sunnah command — and the discipline is the source of the durability that the halal businesses in Section 7 of `riba_free_finance.md` demonstrate.

## Where Experts Disagree

There is a genuine disagreement among contemporary Islamic scholars and finance practitioners on three questions:

**Question 1 — The permissibility of "tawarruq" (the use of two murabaha contracts to generate cash)**. Tawarruq is a structure in which a party buys a commodity from a bank on murabaha (deferred payment), then immediately sells the commodity to a third party for cash. The structure produces cash now in exchange for a deferred payment — a functionally similar result to a conventional loan, achieved through two murabaha contracts. Some scholars hold that tawarruq is permissible (it is a sequence of valid sales, each individually halal); other scholars hold that tawarruq is a hila (legal trick) that violates the purpose of the riba prohibition (Maxim 1 — matters are determined by their purposes). The dominant view (and the one applied here) is the latter — tawarruq is not permissible for the contractor's financing; the contractor should use a direct mudaraba or musharaka for the cash the contractor needs.

**Question 2 — The permissibility of "wa'd" (unilateral promise) as a binding commitment**. The classical fiqh holds that a unilateral promise is not contractually binding (it is a moral commitment, not a legal one). The modern Islamic finance practice (especially in murabaha through Islamic banks) uses a unilateral promise from the customer to buy the good from the bank, to commit the customer before the bank purchases the good from the supplier. Some scholars hold that the wa'd is not binding (the classical view); other scholars hold that the wa'd is binding in modern commercial practice (to avoid the injustice of the customer abandoning the bank after the bank has purchased the good). The dominant view in modern practice (and the one applied here) is the latter — the wa'd is binding in modern Islamic finance contracts, especially in the murabaha through Islamic banks.

**Question 3 — The permissibility of hedging through conventional derivative instruments**. The contractor who needs to hedge currency risk (e.g., for the purchase of imported materials) has limited Islamic alternatives. Some scholars hold that currency hedging through conventional forward contracts is not permissible (the forward contract embeds an interest rate differential, which is riba); other scholars hold that the hedging is permissible if it is for the purpose of risk management (not speculation). The dominant view (and the one applied here) is the former — the contractor should use Islamic alternatives (Islamic currency murabaha, natural hedging by matching the currency of revenues and costs) rather than conventional forward contracts.

The contractor's religious advisor (a trusted scholar with finance expertise) is the reference for any specific question on the disagreements.
