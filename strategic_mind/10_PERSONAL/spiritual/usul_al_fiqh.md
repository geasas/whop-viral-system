# Usul al-Fiqh — The Principles of Islamic Jurisprudence for the Contractor

> **Layer**: 10_PERSONAL/spiritual/
> **File**: usul_al_fiqh
> **Subject**: Usul al-fiqh — the method by which Islamic rulings are derived — applied to the contractor's decisions
> **Domain**: Spiritual — the jurisprudential method beneath the rulings
> **User**: Egyptian Muslim electrical contractor
> **Why this file exists**: A contractor who only memorizes rulings (fiqh) without understanding their sources (usul) is a brittle strategist. When the situation does not match a memorized ruling, he is lost. Usul al-fiqh is the method — knowing it allows the contractor to derive the ruling when the situation is novel, and to know when to defer to a scholar. This file is not a fiqh manual; it is the operating system beneath the fiqh.

---

## The Premise

Fiqh is the human understanding of the sharia. Sharia is divine. Fiqh is human. The distinction is critical: a contractor who treats every fiqh opinion as if it were divine scripture will be paralyzed by the disagreement among scholars. A contractor who treats fiqh as merely human opinion will lose reverence for the rulings he follows. The correct frame: sharia is the divine source; fiqh is the best human effort to extract rulings from that source; usul is the method by which the extraction is done.

Imam al-Shafi'i (d. 204 AH) is credited with codifying usul al-fiqh as a distinct discipline in his *Risala*. The discipline matured over the next two centuries and is now shared, with variations, by all four Sunni schools. This file draws from the classical consensus, with attention to the Egyptian context (where both the Shafi'i and Maliki schools have historically been strong, alongside the dominant Hanafi rite of al-Azhar and the contemporary Salafi influence which often follows the Hanbali school).

The core claim: **usul al-fiqh is a decision-making method.** It is not just a body of rules to learn. It is a structured way of moving from a question (is this contract valid?) to a ruling (yes, no, with conditions) using a defined hierarchy of sources and a defined set of rules. The contractor who masters this method becomes more autonomous in his decisions and more precise in his consultations with scholars.

## The Four Sources (Adilla Shar'iyya)

The vast majority of Sunni jurists agree on four sources from which rulings are derived. They are arranged in a hierarchy, not in a list — the higher source overrides the lower when they appear to conflict.

### Source 1: The Quran

The Quran is the speech of Allah revealed to the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ. It is the first source. The Quran states its own function:

> "This is the Book about which there is no doubt, a guidance for the conscious." [Quran, Surah al-Baqara (2):2 — Tier 1].

The Quran contains approximately 6,236 ayat. Of these, the jurists estimate roughly 500 contain legal content (ayat al-ahkam). The remainder deal with theology, narrative, worship, and ethics. The legal ayat are concentrated in the Medinan surahs.

The Quran's function in usul is primary and final. Where a ruling is established from the Quran (with a clear text — qat'i al-dalala), there is no recourse to other sources. Where the Quran is silent, the Sunnah is consulted. Where the Quran is ambiguous, the Sunnah and the other sources clarify.

**Application to contractor**: The Quran prohibits riba (interest) explicitly. The contractor who is offered an interest-bearing loan to fund a project does not need to consult a scholar about whether the loan is permissible — the ruling is established by the Quran (Surah al-Baqara 2:275-279). The contractor's question to a scholar becomes operational, not foundational: "How do I structure a halal financing alternative?" The foundation is settled by the Quran.

### Source 2: The Sunnah

The Sunnah is the sayings, actions, and tacit approvals of the Prophet Muhammad ﷺ. It is recorded in the Hadith literature. The Quran commands obedience to the Prophet:

> "And whatever the Messenger has given you — take it; and what he has forbidden you — refrain from." [Quran, Surah al-Hashr (59):7 — Tier 1].

The Sunnah functions in three ways in usul:

1. **Reinforcing the Quran** — confirming what the Quran states.
2. **Explaining the Quran** — detailing what the Quran states in summary (e.g., the Quran commands prayer; the Sunnah demonstrates how to pray, as in the famous Hadith: "Pray as you have seen me pray" [Narrated by al-Bukhari, no. 631, from Malik ibn al-Huwayris — Tier 1]).
3. **Establishing rulings not in the Quran** — the Sunnah legislates on matters the Quran does not address (the prohibition of certain transactions, the rules of inheritance in some details, etc.).

The science of Hadith criticism (mustalah al-hadith) developed to verify the authenticity of narrations. The grade of a Hadith (sahih, hasan, da'if) is decisive for whether it can establish a ruling.

**Application to contractor**: The Prophet ﷺ said:

> "Whoever cheats is not of us." [Narrated by Abu Hurayra, Sahih Muslim, no. 102, with the fuller version "whoever cheats us is not of us" — Tier 1].

A contractor who is considering substituting a lower-grade wire for the specified grade in a way that the client would not detect — the ruling is established by this Hadith. Cheating in transactions is prohibited. The ruling is not from the Quran directly; it is from the Sunnah.

### Source 3: Ijma (Consensus)

Ijma is the consensus of the scholars of the Muslim community on a ruling after the death of the Prophet ﷺ. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> "My community will not agree on error." [Narrated by Ibn Mas'ud and others, graded hasan in various compilations — Tier 2, hasan].

Ijma is the third source. It is rarely a source of new rulings in the contemporary period — most ijma was established in the early centuries on foundational matters (the obligation of the five prayers, the prohibition of intoxicants, the validity of sales with specific conditions). The contractor will rarely encounter a question that turns on the application of ijma.

The function of ijma for the contractor is mostly to settle foundational matters so that they are not re-litigated. The prohibition of riba is established by the Quran, the Sunnah, and the ijma of the Umma. A contractor who tells himself, "perhaps there is a difference of opinion," is wrong — there is no meaningful difference of opinion among the schools on the prohibition of riba in its clear forms.

### Source 4: Qiyas (Analogy)

Qiyas is the extension of a ruling from an original case (asl) to a new case (far') by a shared effective cause ('illa). Qiyas is the method by which fiqh handles new situations that the original sources did not explicitly address.

The example: the Quran and Sunnah prohibit the consumption of wine (khamr) because of its intoxicating effect. The ruling extends by analogy to whiskey, beer, and any intoxicant — the shared 'illa is intoxication. The new cases were not known in 7th century Arabia; the ruling extends to them by qiyas.

The four conditions of valid qiyas (per the classical usul manuals, following al-Shafi'i, al-Jassas, and al-Ghazali):
1. The original case must be established in the Quran, Sunnah, or ijma.
2. The new case must share the effective cause with the original case.
3. The ruling must be derived by the effective cause, not by a mere surface similarity.
4. The new case must not have an existing ruling in the original sources (otherwise there is no need for qiyas).

**Application to contractor**: The classical fiqh prohibits the sale of what one does not own (bay' ma'dum) — e.g., selling a car one does not possess. The 'illa is that the seller is exposing the buyer to risk on a thing the seller cannot deliver. The contemporary case of a contractor who signs a contract to deliver electrical work on a project that has not yet been financed by the client — is this like bay' ma'dum? The analogy is debatable; the contractor should consult a scholar. This is the kind of question qiyas resolves.

## The Five Rulings (Al-Ahkam al-Khamsa)

Every action in Islamic law falls into one of five categories. This is the operational map for the contractor. Knowing which category a decision falls into is most of the battle.

### 1. Wajib (Obligatory)

An act the doer is rewarded for and the abandoner is punished for. Examples: the five daily prayers, zakat for one who has the nisab, fasting Ramadan, the duty to fulfill contracts.

The Quran states:

> "O you who believe, fulfill the contracts." [Quran, Surah al-Ma'ida (5):1 — Tier 1].

Fulfilling contracts is wajib. The contractor who agrees to deliver a project by a date is religiously obligated to deliver by that date. A delay without a valid sharia-recognized excuse is a sin, not just a contractual breach.

### 2. Mustahab (Recommended)

An act the doer is rewarded for and the abandoner is not punished for. Examples: the sunnah prayers, fasting on Mondays and Thursdays, sadaqa above the zakat obligation.

**Application to contractor**: Paying subcontractors early (before the contract requires it) is mustahab. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> "Give the worker his wage before his sweat dries." [Narrated by Ibn Umar, Sunan Ibn Maja, no. 2443, hasan — Tier 2, hasan].

The contractor who pays a subcontractor the day the work is done, rather than waiting the 30 days the contract allows, is doing a mustahab act. It is not a sin to wait; it is a virtue to pay early.

### 3. Mubah (Permissible)

An act for which there is no reward or punishment. The default for all worldly matters is mubah, unless a text establishes otherwise. The foundational principle: everything is permitted until proven prohibited. The Quran states:

> "He has explained in detail to you what is prohibited to you." [Quran, Surah al-An'am (6):119 — Tier 1].

The implication is that what is not explained as prohibited is permitted. This is the doctrinal ground for commercial activity — business, building, contracting, profit-making are all mubah by default.

**Application to contractor**: A contractor may engage in any line of business that is not specifically prohibited. Electrical contracting, real estate development, retail, services — all are mubah. The contractor does not need to find a text permitting electrical contracting; he needs to confirm that what he is doing does not fall into a prohibited category (riba, gharar in excess, fraud, harm).

### 4. Makruh (Disliked)

An act the doer is not punished for but the abandoner is rewarded for. Makruh is of two kinds: makruh tanzihi (disliked, lesser) and makruh tahrimi (disliked to the point of prohibition, but with a weaker text).

**Application to contractor**: Wasteful spending (israf) is makruh or haram depending on degree. The contractor who buys a more expensive vehicle than his business needs is in the makruh zone. The Quran states:

> "And do not be excessive. Indeed, He does not like those who are excessive." [Quran, Surah al-An'am (6):141 — Tier 1].

### 5. Haram (Prohibited)

An act the doer is punished for and the abandoner is rewarded for. Examples: riba, fraud, theft, intoxicants, gambling.

**Application to contractor**: Riba in its clear forms is haram. The Quran states:

> "Allah has permitted trade and forbidden riba." [Quran, Surah al-Baqara (2):275 — Tier 1].

A contractor who structures a deal with a clear interest-bearing loan is committing haram. There is no "Egyptian context exception." The ruling is universal.

## The Maqasid al-Sharia (Higher Objectives)

The maqasid are the higher objectives of the sharia. The classical articulation (developed by al-Juwayni, al-Ghazali, and most fully by al-Shatibi in his *Muwafaqat*) identifies five objectives that the sharia universally seeks to protect. Every ruling in fiqh serves one or more of these five.

### Objective 1: Preservation of Religion (Hifz al-Din)

Religion is the foundation. The sharia seeks to preserve the religion of the Muslim. The duty of worship, the prohibition of compulsion in religion, the protection of mosques, the obligation to defend against attacks on the faith — all serve this objective.

**Application to contractor**: The contractor must structure his work so that it does not prevent him from his religious obligations. A contract that requires him to miss the Friday prayer every week is one he should not sign — the preservation of religion is above the preservation of wealth. A work schedule that prevents the dawn prayer should be adjusted or declined.

### Objective 2: Preservation of Life (Hifz al-Nafs)

Life is sacred. The sharia prohibits the taking of innocent life, requires the preservation of health, and imposes liability for harm caused.

**Application to contractor**: The contractor's site safety is a religious obligation, not just a regulatory one. A contractor who cuts safety corners and causes a worker's injury is liable before Allah, not just before the Egyptian labor courts. The Quran states:

> "And do not throw yourselves into destruction with your own hands." [Quran, Surah al-Baqara (2):195 — Tier 1].

This ayah is directly applicable to the contractor who under-equips his team or who uses unsafe scaffolding.

### Objective 3: Preservation of Intellect (Hifz al-Aql)

The sharia preserves the human intellect from substances and practices that impair it. The prohibition of intoxicants is the clearest application.

**Application to contractor**: The contractor should not operate on sleep deprivation so severe that it impairs his judgment. Pushing oneself to physical collapse is contrary to the preservation of intellect (and life). This connects directly to physical/sleep_and_recovery.md.

### Objective 4: Preservation of Lineage (Hifz al-Nasl)

The sharia preserves the family structure. The rules of marriage, the prohibition of zina (fornication/adultery), the rules of inheritance — all serve this objective.

**Application to contractor**: The contractor's work schedule that keeps him away from his family for weeks on end strains the preservation of lineage. This is not a fiqh ruling that says "do not travel for work" — the Prophet ﷺ himself traveled for trade. But the contractor should audit the proportion of his time that the work is taking from his family, and make structural corrections (delegation, hiring, time management) before the family is damaged.

### Objective 5: Preservation of Property (Hifz al-Mal)

The sharia preserves property. The rules of contract, the prohibition of theft, the rules of inheritance, the validity conditions of sales — all serve this objective.

**Application to contractor**: The contractor's contracts should be written clearly. Ambiguity in contracts (gharar in excess) is prohibited because it threatens the preservation of property — one party may end up paying for what was not delivered, or delivering what was not paid for. The contract that specifies the work, the price, the timeline, and the penalties is not just good commercial practice; it is a religious obligation grounded in the preservation of property.

## The Five General Maxims (Al-Qawa'id al-Fiqhiyya al-Kulliyya)

The five maxims are the highest abstraction of fiqh. They were codified in the 8th century AH by Imam al-Suyuti (Egyptian, d. 911 AH) and others. They apply across the entire body of fiqh and serve as decision shortcuts when the contractor cannot consult the full ruling.

### Maxim 1: "Matters are judged by their purposes" (Al-umur bi-maqasidiha)

The form of an act is judged by its intent. A contract that has the form of a sale but the substance of a loan with interest is, in substance, a riba transaction and prohibited. The Prophet ﷺ said:

> "Actions are but by intentions, and every person will have what he intended." [Narrated by Umar ibn al-Khattab, Sahih al-Bukhari and Muslim, no. 1 — Tier 1].

**Application to contractor**: A contractor who structures a deal as a "sale with a buyback guarantee" to evade the prohibition of riba is fooling no one. The substance is a loan with interest; the form is irrelevant. The maxim cuts through the legal fiction.

### Maxim 2: "Certainty is not removed by doubt" (Al-yaqinu la yazulu bi'l-shakk)

A certainty remains certain until doubt is established with proof. If one is certain of being in wudu, and doubts whether it broke, the wudu is still valid.

**Application to contractor**: A contractor who is certain he fulfilled a contract (delivered the work to spec, on time) does not owe a penalty even if the client claims a defect; the burden of proof is on the claimant. The maxim protects the contractor's certainty against the client's doubt.

### Maxim 3: "Harm is to be removed" (Al-dararu yuzal)

Harm should be eliminated. The principle underlies tort liability, neighborhood nuisance rules, and many commercial regulations.

**Application to contractor**: A contractor whose work causes harm (a poorly installed electrical system that creates a fire risk) must remove the harm at his own cost, even if the original contract has been signed off. The signature does not absolve the harm.

### Maxim 4: "Hardship begets facilitation" (Al-mashaqqatu tajlibu al-taysir)

Where there is genuine hardship, the ruling is facilitated. The traveler shortens the prayer; the sick breaks the fast.

**Application to contractor**: A contractor in genuine financial hardship (defined by fiqh, not by self-assessment) has options to restructure obligations. But this maxim is widely misused — "hardship" is a specific fiqh category, not a general excuse for any discomfort.

### Maxim 5: "Custom is the basis of judgment" (Al-'adatu muhakkama)

Where a text is silent, the custom of the community governs. What is "reasonable time" for delivery? The custom of the trade. What is "suitable quality"? The custom of the trade.

**Application to contractor**: In Egyptian contracting, the custom includes a 10% retention (the "garanti") held by the client for a defect-liability period. This custom governs unless the contract explicitly overrides it. The contractor who knows the custom knows what is reasonable to expect.

## How to Make Decisions That Align with Usul al-Fiqh

The contractor's decision procedure:

1. **Identify the question precisely** — "Is it permissible to sign this specific contract?" not "Is contracting permissible?" (the latter is settled).
2. **Check the Quran** — does it address the question?
3. **Check the Sunnah** — does it address the question?
4. **Check the established ijma** — has the Umma's scholars agreed on this?
5. **Apply qiyas** — what analogous ruling applies?
6. **Identify the five-category ruling** — is this wajib, mustahab, mubah, makruh, or haram?
7. **Check the maqasid** — does the action preserve or violate the five objectives?
8. **Apply the five maxims** — do any of the five maxims settle the question?
9. **Consult a qualified scholar** — when steps 1-8 do not produce a confident answer, consult. The contractor is not a mufti.

This procedure produces a decision that is grounded, defensible, and aligned with the divine sources.

## The Four Schools and the Egyptian Context

The four schools of Sunni jurisprudence (madhahib) developed in the first three centuries of Islam. They agree on the foundations (the four sources, the five rulings, the maqasid) and differ in the details (which Hadith is preferred, how qiyas is applied, which analogy is stronger).

### The Hanafi School

Founded by Abu Hanifa al-Nu'man (d. 150 AH / 767 CE), based in Kufa, Iraq. The Hanafi school is the most widely followed school in the Muslim world — it dominates Central Asia, South Asia, Turkey, the Balkans, and large parts of Egypt. The Hanafi school emphasizes rational analysis (ra'y) alongside text, and is generally considered the most flexible of the four schools in applying qiyas and istihsan (juristic preference).

**Egyptian context**: The Hanafi school is the official rite of al-Azhar (since the Ottoman period) and of the Egyptian state's religious affairs. The Egyptian Personal Status Law is largely Hanafi. Most Egyptian Muslims who follow a school follow the Hanafi school for personal law.

### The Maliki School

Founded by Malik ibn Anas (d. 179 AH / 795 CE), based in Medina. The Maliki school dominates North and West Africa — Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia, Libya, Mauritania, Mali, Senegal, and parts of Egypt (especially Upper Egypt and historically the Western Desert). The Maliki school emphasizes the practice of the people of Medina (amal ahl al-Madina) as a source alongside the Quran and Sunnah.

**Egyptian context**: The Maliki school has a strong historical presence in Egypt, particularly in Upper Egypt and among the scholarly families of al-Azhar. Many Egyptian scholars were Maliki (including al-Shatibi, who was from Andalusia but taught in Egypt, and al-Qarafi, an Egyptian Maliki). The Maliki school is the second most-followed school in Egypt.

### The Shafi'i School

Founded by Muhammad ibn Idris al-Shafi'i (d. 204 AH / 820 CE). Al-Shafi'i lived in Gaza, Mecca, Baghdad, and Cairo — he died and is buried in Cairo. The Shafi'i school dominates Egypt (historically — al-Shafi'i's tomb in Cairo is a major religious site), the Levant, the Hejaz, Yemen, East Africa, and Southeast Asia (Indonesia, Malaysia). The Shafi'i school is considered the most methodologically strict of the four — it limits the use of ra'y and emphasizes the text.

**Egyptian context**: The Shafi'i school was historically dominant in Egypt before the Ottoman period (which promoted the Hanafi rite). Many of Egypt's greatest scholars were Shafi'i — including al-Nawawi (Damascene but part of the Shafi'i school's golden age), al-Suyuti (Egyptian, but technically Shafi'i), and Ibn Hajar al-Asqalani (Egyptian, Shafi'i). The Shafi'i school is the third most-followed school in contemporary Egypt, but its intellectual contribution to Egyptian Islamic scholarship is enormous.

### The Hanbali School

Founded by Ahmad ibn Hanbal (d. 241 AH / 855 CE), based in Baghdad. The Hanbali school is the smallest of the four in geographic distribution — historically concentrated in Najd (central Arabia) and parts of Iraq and Syria. The Hanbali school emphasizes text over rational analysis; it is considered the most conservative of the four in methodology.

**Egyptian context**: The Hanbali school has had limited historical presence in Egypt, but the contemporary Salafi movement in Egypt draws heavily on Hanbali texts (particularly the writings of Ibn Taymiyya, Ibn al-Qayyim, and the Hanbali tradition generally). The contemporary Egyptian contractor who is Salafi-influenced may follow Hanbali positions on many matters.

### The Egyptian Contractor's Practical Position

The Egyptian contractor is most likely either Hanafi (the dominant rite of Egyptian state religion and al-Azhar) or Shafi'i (the historical Egyptian school and common in personal practice) or Maliki (in Upper Egypt and among certain families). For commercial matters, the four schools are largely in agreement on the foundational rulings (prohibition of riba, validity conditions of sales, prohibition of fraud). The differences emerge on detailed matters.

**The recommended practice**: The contractor follows one school consistently for personal law (so he does not "shop" between schools for personal convenience). For commercial matters, he consults a qualified scholar and follows the scholar's school. The contractor does not need to master all four schools; he needs to know his own school's positions on the matters relevant to his business and to know when to defer to a scholar.

The danger to avoid is **tazallub al-madhahib** (school-shopping for the easiest ruling). A contractor who follows the Hanafi position on one matter, the Shafi'i position on another, and the Maliki position on a third — chosen for convenience rather than conviction — is undermining the very purpose of having a school. The schools exist to provide methodological consistency, not a buffet of preferences.

## Where the Schools Disagree on Contractor-Relevant Matters

For completeness, the most relevant disagreements:

1. **The validity of certain sales contracts** — the Hanafi school allows the sale of an item one does not yet possess under certain conditions; the Shafi'i school is more restrictive. The contractor should follow the position of his school and his scholar.

2. **The conditions of a binding offer** — the schools differ on whether an offer, once made, is binding on the offeror. The contractor's contracts should be drafted to be valid across the schools (avoiding the contested conditions).

3. **The definition of gharar** — all four schools prohibit gharar (excessive uncertainty), but they differ in what counts as excessive. The Maliki and Hanbali schools tend to be more restrictive; the Hanafi school more flexible.

4. **The permissibility of certain modern contract forms** — Islamic finance products (murabaha, ijara, sukuk) are structured to be valid across the schools. The contractor using an Islamic bank should verify that the structure has been reviewed by a board representing the major schools.
