# Human Nature Axioms — What Humans Are, Always

> **Purpose**: A set of axioms about human nature that hold across cultures, eras, and contexts. These are the assumptions the system makes about people unless proven otherwise.

---

## The Premise

Most strategic failure comes from wrong assumptions about human nature. The advisor who assumes people are rational, the manager who assumes employees are loyal, the partner who assumes the other partner will always be reasonable — all are setting up for predictable disappointment.

The axioms below are not cynical. They are realistic. They describe how humans ACTUALLY behave, not how we'd like them to behave. From these axioms, strategy can be built. From idealized assumptions, only disappointment.

---

## The Ten Axioms

### Axiom 1: People Act on Perceived Interest, Not on Truth

**Statement**: When someone makes a decision, they decide based on what they BELIEVE serves their interest, not on what ACTUALLY serves their interest.

**Implication**: To predict behavior, you must understand the OTHER person's perception of their interest, not the objective fact.

**Application**: A client who insists on a particular material isn't arguing about quality — they're acting on what they believe is best. Show them how their perceived interest aligns with your recommendation, and they'll agree. Show them they're wrong, and they'll resist — even if they later realize you were right.

**Source**: Sun Tzu (know the enemy), Machiavelli (men are governed by their perceptions, not by reality).

---

### Axiom 2: Fear of Loss Is Stronger Than Desire for Gain

**Statement**: A person will work harder to avoid losing EGP 100 than to gain EGP 100, even though the net effect is identical.

**Implication**: Frame proposals in terms of what the other party will LOSE by not acting, not what they'll gain by acting. This is not manipulation — it's recognizing how the brain works.

**Application**: When selling a contract, frame it as "without this electrical system you risk [specific loss: fire, code violation, insurance refusal]" rather than "with this system you gain [safety, compliance]."

**Source**: Kahneman and Tversky (Prospect Theory — the foundational paper on loss aversion).

---

### Axiom 3: People Value What They Help Create

**Statement**: A person values a thing more when they participated in creating it, even if the participation was minor.

**Implication**: When you want someone to value a solution, get them to "co-author" it. Ask for their input. Let them suggest a modification. The investment makes the solution theirs.

**Application**: Don't present a fully-formed plan to a client. Present a draft, ask for their input, let them modify it. The plan they "co-authored" will be more readily accepted than the plan you imposed.

**Source**: IKEA Effect (Norton, Mochon, Ariely).

---

### Axiom 4: Loyalty Has a Half-Life

**Statement**: Loyalty decays over time unless actively reinforced. It is not a permanent state but a renewable resource.

**Implication**: Don't assume past loyalty guarantees future loyalty. Reinforce loyalty periodically: through payment, recognition, inclusion, and shared success.

**Application**: A subcontractor who worked loyally for 3 years will not be loyal in year 4 if you've taken him for granted. He'll go to the contractor who pays 10% more. The loyalty wasn't fake — it just expired.

**Source**: Machiavelli (men are quickly ungrateful), the Quran (relationships need active maintenance).

---

### Axiom 5: Pride Is the Strongest Driver and the Most Hidden

**Statement**: People will sacrifice money, time, and relationships to protect their pride. They will rarely admit pride is the driver.

**Implication**: When someone rejects a good offer, look for the pride trigger. When someone accepts a bad offer, look for the pride satisfaction. Pride is the variable that often overrides economic logic.

**Application**: A client who rejects your revised quote (higher than original) may not be rejecting the price — they may be reacting to the perception that you're "changing the deal on them." Give them a face-saving way to accept (a new scope clarification, a value-add) and they'll accept the new price.

**Source**: Sun Tzu (do not press a desperate enemy — they will fight to the death for pride), Machiavelli (wound a man lightly and he survives to take revenge; wound him mortally and he cannot — the implication is to either not wound or to wound beyond recovery).

---

### Axiom 6: People Believe What They Want to Believe, Then Find Evidence

**Statement**: Most reasoning is post-hoc justification for an emotional conclusion. The emotion comes first; the logic comes after.

**Implication**: To change someone's mind, change the emotional frame, not the logical argument. Logical arguments against an emotional commitment strengthen the commitment (the person defends it harder).

**Application**: A client who has emotionally decided on a particular design will reject your technical objections — even if you're right. Don't argue technical merit; reframe the design choice so they emotionally prefer a different option. ("This design is what your competitors are doing — you'd want to differentiate, right?")

**Source**: Haidt (the elephant and the rider — the elephant chooses direction; the rider explains it).

---

### Axiom 7: Power Reveals Character; It Does Not Create It

**Statement**: When someone gains power, they become more of what they already were. Power does not corrupt; it removes constraints.

**Implication**: Before giving someone power (or partnering with someone powerful), observe how they behaved when they had less power. The kind person becomes more kind. The petty person becomes more petty. The honest person becomes more honest. The cruel person becomes more cruel.

**Application**: Before taking on a partner, watch how they treat their current employees, subcontractors, and family. The behavior they show now is amplified when they have power over you.

**Source**: Plato (the Ring of Gyges), modern research on power and disinhibition.

---

### Axiom 8: People Default to Inaction Unless Forced to Choose

**Statement**: Most people, given the option to do nothing, will do nothing. The status quo is the default.

**Implication**: To get someone to act, make inaction the costly option. Don't ask "do you want to do X?" — ask "X will happen unless you tell me otherwise by [date]."

**Application**: Don't wait for clients to confirm a contract change. Send the change with "I'll proceed with this on [date] unless I hear otherwise." Most will accept; the few who object will tell you.

**Source:** Status quo bias (Samuelson and Zeckhauser).

---

### Axiom 9: People Trust the Familiar, Even When the Familiar Is Worse

**Statement**: People prefer what they know to what is better. This is not stupidity; it is risk aversion. The familiar feels safer even when it isn't.

**Implication**: To introduce a new solution, frame it as an extension of the familiar, not a replacement. "This is just like what you're using, but with [small change]" is more persuasive than "This is completely different and better."

**Application**: When proposing a new electrical design to a client who's used to old methods, frame it as "an upgrade of the [old method]" rather than "a new approach." They'll consider it; otherwise they'll reject it as risky.

**Source**: Kahneman (System 1 prefers the familiar).

---

### Axiom 10: Honesty About Reality Builds Trust; Honesty About Self Doubt Builds Trust More

**Statement**: People trust people who tell them hard truths. They trust people even more who admit their own uncertainty.

**Implication**: Saying "I don't know" doesn't undermine credibility; it strengthens it. Saying "I'm not sure, here are the options" makes the listener trust you MORE than saying "I'm certain."

**Application**: When advising a client, admit the limits of your advice. "I'm 80% confident this will work. The 20% uncertainty is [specific scenario]." The client trusts you more for the uncertainty than they would for false certainty.

**Source**: Research on humility and trust, the Quranic principle of those who "speak words of measured meaning."

## How These Axioms Combine

The axioms interact. The strategic mind sees combinations:

**Combination 1**: Axiom 1 (perceived interest) + Axiom 6 (post-hoc reasoning) = To change behavior, change the perceived interest through emotional frame, not through logical argument.

**Combination 2**: Axiom 2 (loss aversion) + Axiom 8 (status quo bias) = People will stay in a losing position to avoid the perceived loss of change. To unstick them, frame the change as preservation, not disruption.

**Combination 3**: Axiom 3 (co-creation) + Axiom 4 (loyalty half-life) = Co-create repeatedly. One co-creation builds one moment of loyalty; ongoing co-creation sustains it.

**Combination 4**: Axiom 5 (pride) + Axiom 10 (honesty about uncertainty) = When you must give hard feedback, do it in a way that preserves the other person's pride. Don't humiliate. But don't lie.

---

## The Anti-Cynicism Check

These axioms may sound cynical. They are not.

The cynical view: "All people are selfish, so I'll be selfish."
The realistic view: "All people act on perceived interest, including me. Understanding this lets me align my real interest with theirs, so we can both benefit."

The strategic mind uses these axioms to COOPERATE more effectively, not to manipulate. When you understand that loyalty has a half-life, you maintain it more carefully — you become a better partner, not a worse one.

The axioms describe what IS. They don't prescribe what to do. The ethics (`14_ETHICS/`) prescribe what to do.
