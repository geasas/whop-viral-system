# Meta-Principles — The Principles That Govern the Principles

> **Purpose**: Above the constants of power and the axioms of human nature, there are principles about how principles work. These are the meta-principles. They govern how the system uses all other principles.

---

## The Premise

A principle like "patience beats speed" is a principle. But it doesn't apply in all situations. There are situations where speed beats patience (a market window, a competitor's weakness, a personal moment). Knowing when a principle applies is itself a principle — a meta-principle.

This file collects the meta-principles that govern how the system handles its own knowledge.

---

## The Eight Meta-Principles

### Meta-Principle 1: Every Principle Has a Domain of Validity

**Statement**: No principle applies universally. Each has a domain where it works and a domain where it fails.

**Implication**: Before applying a principle, identify whether the situation is in the principle's domain. If you don't know the domain, you don't know the principle.

**Example**: Sun Tzu's "avoid what is strong, attack what is weak" applies in warfare. It applies in business. But it fails when the opponent's strength IS the source of their weakness (e.g., a dominant incumbent's bureaucracy IS the weakness; you don't attack their weakness, you wait for their strength to crush them).

**Application**: When the system reaches for a principle, it must first ask: "Does this principle apply HERE? What is its domain?"

**Source**: All great strategists. Sima Yi is famous for applying principles selectively — patience sometimes, swift action other times. He knew the domains.

---

### Meta-Principle 2: Principles Compete; Situations Arbitrate

**Statement**: When two principles conflict, the situation decides which applies. There is no "higher principle" that always wins.

**Implication**: Don't search for a meta-rule that resolves the conflict ("always pick principle X over Y"). Instead, understand the situation deeply enough to know which principle is appropriate.

**Example**: "Honesty is the best policy" vs. "Don't volunteer information that could be used against you." When do you disclose a defect in your work? Depends on:
- Will the client discover it anyway? (Honesty preserves trust.)
- Is the defect material to the function? (Honesty is ethical.)
- Is the disclosure legally required? (Honesty is mandatory.)
- Is the defect cosmetic and the client wouldn't care? (Disclosure may be unnecessary.)

The situation decides.

**Application**: When two principles conflict in the system, generate both answers and compare them in context. Don't apply a "tiebreaker" rule.

---

### Meta-Principle 3: Principles Are Hypotheses Until Tested

**Statement**: A principle is a claim about the world. It must be tested against reality. If it fails the test, it's not a principle — it's a slogan.

**Implication**: Track outcomes. When a principle gives advice that fails, examine why. Update the principle or its domain.

**Example**: "Always under-promise and over-deliver" is a principle. But in a market where competitors over-promise massively, under-promising may lose you the bid. The principle fails in this domain. The refined principle: "Match competitor promise levels but document your over-delivery in writing for trust-building."

**Application**: The system keeps a record of past advice and outcomes. When advice fails, the principle is re-examined.

---

### Meta-Principle 4: The Deeper Principle Trumps the Surface Principle

**Statement**: When two principles seem to conflict, look for the deeper principle that contains both. The deeper principle resolves the conflict.

**Implication**: Don't stop at the surface. If "patience" and "speed" conflict, the deeper principle is "act when the marginal return on waiting becomes negative." Both surface principles are special cases of the deeper one.

**Example**: "Take care of your team" vs. "Hold the team accountable when they fail." The deeper principle: "Care through development, accountability through clarity." The two surface principles are not in conflict when you understand the deeper one.

**Application**: When the system reaches an impasse between principles, look for the deeper layer. Most "conflicts" are surface-level artifacts of a deeper principle the system hasn't articulated yet.

**Source**: The Quranic hermeneutical principle (usul al-fiqh): when two verses seem to conflict, look for the deeper principle that harmonizes them. Don't pick one and discard the other.

---

### Meta-Principle 5: The Application Matters More Than the Principle

**Statement**: Two people can apply the same principle and get opposite outcomes, because application is more important than principle.

**Implication**: Don't focus on knowing the principle; focus on knowing how to apply it. A perfectly-understood principle applied poorly is worse than an imperfectly-understood principle applied with sensitivity.

**Example**: Everyone knows "know yourself." Most people fail to apply it because they confuse self-knowledge with self-description. The application — actually observing one's own behavior, biases, and patterns — is the hard part. The principle is one sentence; the application is a lifetime.

**Application**: The system emphasizes HOW to apply principles, not just what they are. Every principle file includes application guidance.

---

### Meta-Principle 6: Principles Decay Without Reinforcement

**Statement**: A principle you don't actively use, you forget. Not the words — the application. You can recite it but no longer know how to use it.

**Implication**: Active use is necessary. If a principle is in the system but never invoked, it's dead weight. Either remove it or build cases that use it.

**Example**: A contractor who learned "always negotiate" in business school but never negotiates in practice — over time, his "negotiation" becomes "the price they tell me." The principle is in the memory but the application has decayed.

**Application**: The system keeps principles in active use by routing cases through them. A principle that's never used is a candidate for removal.

---

### Meta-Principle 7: The Best Principle Is the One That Survives Your Own Test

**Statement**: A principle you've never tested is a borrowed principle. A principle you've tested and refined is YOUR principle. The latter is more useful because you understand its boundaries.

**Implication**: Test principles in low-stakes situations before relying on them in high-stakes ones. Don't wait for the high-stakes moment to discover the principle fails.

**Example**: "Always ask for more than you expect to get" — test this in small negotiations before deploying it in a major deal. The principle is right, but the application needs calibration. Without testing, you'll ask for too much and lose the deal.

**Application**: The system encourages the user to test principles in low-stakes environments. When the user is about to use a principle in a high-stakes situation for the first time, the system warns them.

---

### Meta-Principle 8: Some Things Are Not Principles — They Are Wisdom

**Statement**: Some knowledge is not a principle (a generalizable rule) but wisdom (a non-generalizable insight from lived experience). Confusing the two leads to errors.

**Implication**: Don't try to extract a principle from every wise saying. Some wisdom is contextual and cannot be abstracted.

**Example**: "Don't take a partner who doesn't share your risk tolerance" is a principle. "Don't take a partner who doesn't share your VALUES about worker treatment" is wisdom — it's true but it's not abstractable to a general rule about partners. It's specific to the user's values.

**Application**: The system distinguishes principles (generalizable) from wisdom (contextual). Wisdom goes into case files (`13_CASES/`), not principle files.

---

## How Meta-Principles Combine

Meta-principles combine to govern the system's operation:

**When the system reaches for a principle:**
1. Meta-Principle 1: Identify the domain. Does this principle apply here?
2. Meta-Principle 2: Are there competing principles? Let the situation arbitrate.
3. Meta-Principle 4: Is there a deeper principle that contains the conflict?
4. Meta-Principle 5: How is this principle applied, not just stated?
5. Meta-Principle 6: Has this principle been recently used? If not, why?
6. Meta-Principle 3: Has this principle been tested recently? If not, test in a low-stakes context first.
7. Meta-Principle 8: Is this actually a principle, or is it wisdom?

This sequence governs how the system invokes its own knowledge.

---

## The Final Note

The system is not just a library of principles. It is a THINKING SYSTEM that knows how to use principles. The principles are content; the meta-principles are the operating system.

A system with great principles and bad meta-principles will apply the right principle in the wrong situation. A system with average principles and great meta-principles will apply the right principle in the right situation, and refine the principles over time.

The meta-principles matter more.
