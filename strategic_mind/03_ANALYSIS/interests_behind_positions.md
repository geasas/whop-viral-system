# Interests Behind Positions — The Fisher & Ury Discipline

> **Purpose**: Positions are what people say they want. Interests are why they want it. Most negotiations fail because the parties argue positions when the underlying interests — if explored — would reveal a deal. This file teaches the system to never argue positions, only to explore interests.

**Layer**: 03_ANALYSIS
**File**: interests_behind_positions.md
**Subject**: The position vs. interest distinction, the discovery of interests behind positions, and the Egyptian cultural dimension of indirect communication and face preservation.
**Source**: Fisher & Ury, *Getting to Yes* (1981); manga analysis of Yuan Fang, Jia Xu, and Chen Gong (advisors who read interests while their masters argued positions); Sun Tzu's *Art of War* ("Know the enemy and know yourself").
**Application domain**: Egyptian electrical contractor operating in a market where indirect communication, intermediaries, and face preservation are dominant.

## The Premise

A position is a declared demand. An interest is the underlying need, fear, desire, or constraint that the position serves. Positions are visible; interests are hidden. Positions are usually rigid; interests are usually flexible. Positions are negotiable as fixed points only because the interests behind them have not been explored.

The Fisher & Ury principle is simple to state and difficult to execute: do not argue positions; explore interests. When two parties argue positions, they are fighting over the surface. When they explore interests, they discover the system underneath the surface — and almost always find options that satisfy both sides' interests without requiring either side to abandon its position.

The strategist who masters this discipline gains two advantages. First, they make more deals because they discover terms the position-arguers miss. Second, they preserve the relationship because they never forced the other party into a public capitulation. In the Egyptian market — where face preservation (wagh, Arabic: وجه) is a strategic variable as important as price — the second advantage is often more valuable than the first.

## The Position vs. Interest Distinction

### The position is the demand

A position is what a party says they want, in the form they have asked for it. Positions are concrete, specific, and often stated as ultimatums.

**Example (contractor)**: A developer says, "I need the electrical package at 12% below your quoted price, and I need the work to start in three weeks."

This is a position. It is two demands: a price (12% below quote) and a schedule (start in three weeks). It is stated as a single combined demand.

### The interest is the underlying need

An interest is why the party holds the position. Interests are the needs, fears, constraints, and desires that the position serves. One position can hide multiple interests, and one interest can be served by multiple positions.

**The same example, interest-decoded**:

The position "12% lower price + three-week start" can hide several distinct interests:
- *Interest A*: The developer's total project budget was cut by the lender, and they need to reduce every package by approximately 12% to balance the project.
- *Interest B*: The developer's investor meeting is in three weeks, and they need an active construction site to show progress.
- *Interest C*: The developer knows a competitor offered 12% less, and they want to confirm they are not overpaying (the interest is not the price reduction itself; it is the assurance that the price is fair).
- *Interest D*: The developer has a cash-flow crunch in month 2 of construction, and they need to push the largest payments outside that month (the interest is cash-flow timing, not total price).

Each interest implies a different deal. If the interest is A, the contractor can offer scope reduction rather than price reduction. If the interest is B, the contractor can offer a mobilization advance with deferred work. If the interest is C, the contractor can offer transparency on costs and margins rather than a price cut. If the interest is D, the contractor can offer a payment schedule shifted to month 3 and 4 rather than a price cut.

The position-arguer offers one option (12% lower, three-week start) and forces the other party to accept or refuse. The interest-explorer offers four options, each addressing a different interest, and lets the other party choose the one that fits.

## The Discipline of Interest-Discovery

The system does not assume interests; it discovers them. The discovery discipline has five techniques, used in combination.

### Technique 1: The "Why" question (and the third "why")

Ask why the position is held. Then ask why again. Then ask a third time. The first answer is usually still a position; the second answer is closer to an interest; the third answer is usually the actual interest.

**Example**:
- Position: "12% lower price."
- First why: "Because the project budget is tight this year."
- Second why: "Because the bank reduced the loan disbursement."
- Third why: "Because the bank's sector exposure was capped after the devaluation."

By the third why, the interest is revealed: the developer's lender has reduced disbursement, and the developer needs to fit the project into a smaller loan envelope. The contractor cannot solve the bank's sector exposure, but can solve the project's loan envelope by restructuring the payment schedule to match the bank's revised disbursement timeline. The price need not drop 12%. The payment schedule can be reshaped.

The discipline is to ask the third why even when the second why sounds reasonable. The second why is often still a position.

### Technique 2: The hypothetical reversal

Ask the other party to consider the reverse: "If you were in my position, with my costs and my obligations, what would you do?" Or: "If the price could not move at all, what would you want instead?"

The reversal surfaces the secondary interests — the interests that are not the price but that the price was supposed to compensate for. Common secondary interests in contracting: payment timing, scope flexibility, materials specification, dispute resolution mechanism, warranty period, references for future work.

**Example**: When asked "if the price could not move at all, what would you want instead?", the developer might say: "Then I want the warranty extended from one year to three years, and I want a payment schedule that aligns with my sales milestones." The interest is risk reduction (warranty) and cash-flow alignment (payment schedule). The price was a proxy for these.

### Technique 3: Observation of what is over-emphasized

What the other party emphasizes beyond what's natural is a clue to the interest they're not stating. (Cross-reference: `02_PERCEPTION/detecting_hidden_signals.md` — over-emphasis as reverse evidence.)

**Example**: If the developer repeats "I need to know this is a fair price" three times in a 30-minute meeting, the interest is not the price itself; it's the assurance that the price is fair. The repetition is the signal. The contractor who responds by lowering the price misses the interest. The contractor who responds by showing the cost breakdown (materials, labor, overhead, margin) satisfies the interest and often keeps the price.

### Technique 4: Third-party information

The other party's interests are often visible to people who are not at the table — the secretary, the project manager, the supplier, the previous contractor, the engineer who reviewed the project. The discipline is to gather this information before the negotiation, not during it.

**Example**: The contractor who talks to the developer's project manager before the meeting learns that the developer's last contractor was fired for delays. The interest at the upcoming meeting is not "lowest price" — it is "guaranteed schedule." The contractor who arrives with a fixed-price bid misses the interest; the contractor who arrives with a schedule guarantee (penalty clauses for delay, milestone bonuses for early completion) captures the interest and often wins at full price.

### Technique 5: The "what would you do if you didn't have to negotiate with me" question

This question, asked conversationally, surfaces the interest that the negotiation itself is hiding. "If we weren't negotiating, what would your ideal outcome look like?" The answer is often the actual interest, stripped of the negotiation posturing.

**Example**: The developer answers: "I'd want the project done in eight months, with no callbacks, and a contractor I can call for the next three projects." The interest is now visible: schedule, quality, and a multi-project relationship. The price is a secondary concern. The contractor who hears this can offer a three-project framework agreement with a price lock for the first project — capturing the multi-project interest at full price for the first project.

## The Four Interest Categories

Interests fall into four categories. Mapping all four before a negotiation prevents the most common error: treating the substantive interest as the only interest.

### Category 1: Substantive interests

What the party tangibly wants from the deal. In contracting: price, scope, schedule, quality, materials.

**Example**: The developer wants the electrical package delivered on schedule and within budget.

### Category 2: Procedural interests

How the party wants the deal to be made and executed. In contracting: decision process, communication frequency, dispute resolution, change-order procedure, payment process.

**Example**: The developer wants weekly progress reports and a single point of contact, not multiple.

### Category 3: Psychological interests

How the party wants to feel during and after the deal. In contracting: respect, recognition, control, certainty, face (wagh), status.

**Example**: The developer wants the contractor to treat the developer as the senior party, not as a peer. The contractor who treats the developer as a peer — even if technically correct — violates a psychological interest and creates friction that no price discount can repair.

### Category 4: Relational interests

What the party wants from the relationship, beyond this deal. In contracting: future work, references, introductions, exclusivity, loyalty.

**Example**: The developer wants the contractor to be available for the next three projects and to give those projects priority. The relational interest is often the most valuable interest, and the most often ignored.

The discipline is to map all four before the negotiation, for both parties. A negotiation that satisfies only the substantive interest and ignores the procedural, psychological, and relational interests produces a deal that feels bad even when it looks good — and bad-feeling deals don't survive.

## The Egyptian Cultural Dimension

The Fisher & Ury discipline is universal, but its execution is culturally shaped. The Egyptian context adds three dimensions that the system must account for.

### Dimension 1: Face preservation (wagh, وجه)

In the Egyptian business context, face is a strategic variable. A party whose face is publicly damaged will refuse a deal they would otherwise accept, will break a deal they would otherwise keep, and will retaliate in ways that surface much later.

The implication for interest-discovery: interests must be explored privately, not in front of the other party's audience. A developer who has his project manager in the meeting cannot admit that the bank cut his loan — to admit it would lose face in front of his subordinate. The contractor who asks "is the budget tight?" in front of the project manager gets a denial. The contractor who asks the same question privately — over coffee, without the project manager — gets the truth.

The discipline: do the interest-discovery in private. The formal meeting confirms what was discovered privately; it does not discover it.

### Dimension 2: Indirect communication

Egyptian business communication is high-context. The direct statement is often the least informative; the indirect statement is often the most informative. "The price is too high" is the position. "The price is higher than we expected" is the interest — it tells you the budget was set before the quote, not after. "The price should be reviewed" is the most informative — it tells you the price was acceptable to the person you spoke to, but a third party (a partner, a lender, an investor) has weighed in, and you need to identify the third party.

The discipline: read the indirect statements as the primary signal. The direct statement is the position; the indirect statement is the interest.

### Dimension 3: Intermediaries (wasta, وَسَطَة)

In the Egyptian market, intermediaries are not optional. They are the mechanism by which interest-discovery happens. A direct question from the contractor to the developer may be answered with the position. The same question asked through an intermediary — a mutual contact, a previous client, an engineer who works with both — gets the interest.

The intermediary is not a corruption of the negotiation; in the Egyptian context, the intermediary IS the negotiation. The intermediary carries the question without face loss, brings back the answer without commitment, and lets both parties test positions without locking in.

The discipline: use intermediaries deliberately. Identify who carries information between you and the other party, feed them the questions you cannot ask directly, and treat their answers as more reliable than the direct answers you receive in formal meetings.

## The Application: Every Negotiation Is an Interest-Discovery Exercise

The principle applied: every negotiation the contractor enters is reframed as an interest-discovery exercise, not a position-arguing exercise.

### The pre-negotiation protocol

Before any negotiation, the contractor completes the following:
1. Map the other party's likely substantive, procedural, psychological, and relational interests (each category, separately).
2. Identify the third-why for the most likely substantive position (the price or the schedule).
3. Identify the intermediary who can verify or correct the interest map.
4. Identify the private setting where the interest-discovery will happen (the coffee before the meeting, not the meeting itself).
5. Identify the secondary interests that the price or schedule is a proxy for (warranty, payment timing, references, multi-project relationship).

### The in-negotiation protocol

During the negotiation:
1. Never argue the position directly. If the other party states a position, respond with a question that surfaces the interest ("What's driving that target?").
2. Use the third-why technique even when the first answer sounds reasonable.
3. Watch for over-emphasis as the signal of the unspoken interest.
4. Protect the other party's face — never corner them into a public admission.
5. Distinguish the substantive interest (often flexible) from the psychological interest (often rigid). Satisfy the substantive interest; honor the psychological interest.

### The post-negotiation protocol

After the negotiation:
1. Record the interests discovered, not just the terms agreed.
2. Compare the discovered interests to the pre-negotiation map. Where they differ, update the system's model of the other party.
3. Identify the interests that were not satisfied and design the next interaction to address them (the unsatisfied interest is the seed of the next problem).

## Worked Example

**Situation**: A New Cairo developer has asked the contractor to reduce the electrical package quote by 20%. The developer cites "a competing offer" and asks for an answer in 48 hours. The contractor has a 2-year relationship with this developer and has delivered three projects on time.

### Pre-negotiation interest map

**Substantive interests (likely)**:
- Budget pressure (bank cut, lender cap, investor pressure)
- Competing offer is real and they want to confirm fairness

**Procedural interests (likely)**:
- They want a fast answer (the 48-hour deadline signals they need to commit to another contractor soon, OR they want to test the contractor's responsiveness)

**Psychological interests (likely)**:
- They want to feel they got the best deal (face-saving even if they don't take it)
- They want the contractor to acknowledge the value of the relationship without taking it for granted

**Relational interests (likely)**:
- They want the contractor available for the next project regardless of this deal's outcome
- They want the contractor to treat them as a priority client

### Intermediary check

The contractor calls the engineer who works for both. The engineer reports: the developer's main lender (a state-affiliated bank) reduced the construction loan disbursement by 18% last week after a sector-wide review. The developer is asking all contractors for 15-20% reductions, not just this one. The competing offer is real but from a contractor the developer doesn't trust (the engineer's word).

### Private conversation

The contractor meets the developer for coffee before the formal meeting. The conversation is private — no project manager, no engineer. The contractor asks: "What's driving the 20% target? Is it the budget envelope, the competing offer, or something else?" The developer admits, privately: "The bank cut the loan. I need to fit the project into the new envelope. The competing offer is real, but I'd rather work with you — I just can't pay 20% more than the competitor."

### Interest-decoded

The substantive interest is NOT "20% lower price." The substantive interest is "fit the project into the new loan envelope of 18% lower disbursement." The competing offer is real but is from a contractor the developer doesn't trust — so the relational interest is "stay with you, but I need a number I can justify to the bank."

### The solution offered

The contractor offers:
- A 12% price reduction (not 20%), justified by a documented scope adjustment (fewer premium fixtures, standard rather than custom cable trays — visible cost reductions the bank can review).
- A restructured payment schedule that matches the bank's revised disbursement timeline (the bank releases the construction loan in three tranches; the contractor's payments align to the tranches).
- A schedule guarantee that the contractor knows the competing offer cannot match (the competitor has a known 6-week mobilization delay).
- A multi-project framework agreement that locks in this contractor for the next two projects at the same adjusted price.

The developer gets a 12% reduction (close to the 18% envelope after the scope adjustment is accounted for), a payment schedule his bank will accept, a schedule guarantee the competitor cannot match, and a relationship that protects the next two projects. The contractor gets a 12% reduction instead of 20%, scope reduction instead of margin erosion, a payment schedule that protects cash flow, and a locked-in multi-project relationship.

The position was "20% lower." The interest was "fit the new loan envelope and stay with a contractor I trust." The deal that satisfied the interest was 12% lower with scope adjustment and payment restructuring — a deal the position-arguer would never have found, because the position-arguer would have argued 20% vs. 15% and either lost the deal or accepted a bad margin.

## Common Errors in Interest-Discovery

### Error 1: Treating the first why as the interest

The first why is usually still a position. The discipline is the third why.

### Error 2: Ignoring the psychological interest

The substantive interest can be satisfied; the psychological interest must be honored. A contractor who satisfies the substantive interest (price reduction) but violates the psychological interest (treats the developer dismissively) loses the deal anyway — the developer finds a reason to refuse even after the substantive terms are met.

### Error 3: Public interest-discovery

In the Egyptian context, public interest-discovery produces face loss and denial. The discipline is private discovery, public confirmation.

### Error 4: Treating the intermediary as a middleman to be bypassed

The intermediary is the negotiation. The contractor who tries to bypass the intermediary to "negotiate directly" loses the interest-discovery channel and is left with the position.

### Error 5: Satisfying only the substantive interest

The deal that satisfies only the substantive interest and ignores the procedural, psychological, and relational interests produces a deal that doesn't survive the first dispute. The discipline is to map all four and satisfy all four.
