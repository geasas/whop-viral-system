# Core Human Drives — What Humans Actually Want

> **Purpose**: A synthesis of the deep motivational architecture of human beings, drawn from classical philosophy, evolutionary psychology, modern self-determination theory, behavioral neuroscience, and the Islamic intellectual tradition. This file is the motivational engine that other files in `04_PSYCHOLOGY/` build on: every bias, every group dynamic, every manipulation pattern ultimately traces back to one or more of the drives mapped here.

> **Provenance**: This file was synthesized on 2026-09-09 from sources across 4 tiers. The synthesis was audited per `00_META/self_audit_protocol.md`. Confidence: MEDIUM-HIGH. Primary reviewer: the user (Egyptian electrical contractor).

---

## Why this file exists

The user asked: *What do people actually want — not what they say they want, but what they actually move toward, fight for, and abandon their stated values to obtain?* This question matters because every negotiation, every team management decision, every client relationship, every self-management decision depends on correctly identifying the operative drive. Misread the drive and you negotiate against the wrong variable. Misread your own drive and you spend a decade climbing a ladder leaning on the wrong wall.

The literature is enormous and largely non-commensurable. Maslow frames needs as a hierarchy; evolutionary psychologists frame them as domains; self-determination theory frames them as three basic psychological nutrients; the SCARF model frames them as five social threats/rewards; the Islamic tradition frames them as three dimensions of the *fitrah* (the original disposition). These are not synonyms — they are partial maps of the same territory from different elevations. This file stitches them into a single map the user can actually apply.

---

## The core principle

Humans are driven by a small set of deeply conserved motivational systems — physical survival, social belonging, status/esteem, autonomy/competence, and meaning/transcendence — that operate simultaneously, compete for expression, and are differentially weighted by culture, personality, and life stage. None of the major theories is wrong; each is a partial projection. The strategic mind reads the operative drive in a given person at a given moment, aligns incentives with that drive, and avoids the costly error of assuming that what motivates the advisor motivates the advisee.

## The Principles

### Principle 1: Maslow's hierarchy is a useful heuristic and a misleading model

Maslow's 1943 paper proposed five tiers — physiological, safety, love/belonging, esteem, self-actualization — and claimed that lower tiers must be largely satisfied before higher tiers become motivational [Maslow, "A Theory of Human Motivation," *Psychological Review* 50 (1943): 370-396 — Tier 4, peer-reviewed]. The model became culturally dominant because it is intuitive and visual.

The model is partially correct: a starving man thinks about food, not self-actualization. But the strict prepotency claim — that higher needs do not motivate until lower needs are satisfied — is empirically false. People fast for religious reasons (physiological need overridden by transcendence). Artists produce great work in poverty (safety need overridden by esteem/actualization). Martyrs die for beliefs (survival overridden by meaning). Maslow himself softened the strict hierarchical claim in later work, allowing partial satisfaction as sufficient trigger [Maslow, *Toward a Psychology of Being*, 1968 — Tier 2].

The contemporary critique (Wahba & Bridwell, 1976; Tay & Diener, 2011) is that human needs are not arranged hierarchically but operate as concurrent systems, and that the satisfaction of one does not require the satisfaction of another [Wahba & Bridwell, "Maslow Reconsidered," *Organizational Behavior and Human Performance* 15 (1976): 212-240 — Tier 4]. The Diener team's cross-cultural study of 60,000 participants in 123 countries found that needs co-occur: a person can simultaneously lack food and have high meaning, or have plenty of food and lack belonging [Tay & Diener, "Needs and Subjective Well-Being Around the World," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 101 (2011): 354-356 — Tier 4].

**Mechanism**: Maslow's hierarchy was originally a clinical intuition, not a derived theory. It became canonical because it was taught, not because it was tested. The real mechanism is that *attention* is allocated to the most salient unmet need, but *motivation* runs on all needs simultaneously. A client who is anxious about a contract price is not only operating from the "safety" tier; he is also operating from the "esteem" tier (does the price signal respect?) and the "autonomy" tier (do I have choice in this?).

**When it fails**: Whenever a person sacrifices a lower need for a higher one — fasting, martyrdom, artistic poverty, asceticism — the hierarchical model fails to predict. It also fails whenever a person with all lower needs met is still miserable: the bitter rich man, the lonely celebrity, the respected executive with no meaning.

**Application**: For the Egyptian contractor, the practical use of Maslow is *not* the hierarchy but the checklist. When you assess a client, ask: which of these five need-domains is currently underfed for them? A client whose business is in crisis is operating from the safety tier (do not push aggressive designs that risk his cash position). A client who just landed a major project is operating from the esteem tier (frame recommendations as making him look smart in front of his board). A client approaching retirement is operating from the meaning tier (frame recommendations as legacy). The hierarchy is wrong as a sequence; the five needs are right as a checklist.

### Principle 2: Self-Determination Theory — the three nutrients

Deci and Ryan's Self-Determination Theory (SDT) posits three universal psychological needs whose satisfaction produces well-being and whose frustration produces ill-being: **autonomy** (acting from one's own volition, not external compulsion), **competence** (exercising and expanding one's capacities), and **relatedness** (feeling connected to and cared about by others) [Deci & Ryan, *Intrinsic Motivation and Self-Determination in Human Behavior*, 1985 — Tier 2; Ryan & Deci, "Self-Determination Theory and the Facilitation of Intrinsic Motivation," *American Psychologist* 55 (2000): 68-78 — Tier 4].

SDT's central empirical claim is that *extrinsic rewards undermine intrinsic motivation* for tasks that were previously intrinsically interesting — the so-called "overjustification effect." Pay a child to draw, and the child draws less for the joy of it. This finding, replicated across cultures and age groups, has profound implications for management: when you pay people for behavior they would have done anyway, you can corrupt the intrinsic motivation that drove the behavior [Deci, Koestner & Ryan, "A Meta-Analytic Review of Experiments Examining the Effects of Extrinsic Rewards on Intrinsic Motivation," *Psychological Bulletin* 125 (1999): 627-668 — Tier 4, meta-analysis].

The three needs also have a hierarchy of sorts: autonomy and competence together produce engagement; relatedness produces attachment; all three together produce *flourishing*. Seligman's PERMA model (Positive emotion, Engagement, Relationships, Meaning, Accomplishment) extends this into the positive-psychology synthesis [Seligman, *Flourish*, 2011 — Tier 2].

**Mechanism**: SDT's mechanism is *psychological need satisfaction*. When a person experiences autonomy (feels choice, not coercion), the brain interprets the activity as self-endorsed and produces intrinsic motivation. When the same activity is experienced as coerced, the brain interprets it as control and produces resistance, even when the activity is identical.

**When it fails**: SDT under-predicts in situations of severe material scarcity (a person being paid too little cannot be motivated by autonomy alone — pay adequacy is a hygiene factor, in Herzberg's terms). SDT also under-predicts in collectivist cultures where autonomy is defined differently — as *volitional alignment with the group*, not as individual choice. This is critical for the Egyptian context, where autonomy often means "freedom to act within my role" rather than "freedom to choose my role."

**Application**: For the Egyptian contractor managing subcontractors, the SDT prescription is concrete. **Autonomy**: give subcontractors scope decisions within their craft, not just task lists ("you decide how to route the conduit; I'll specify endpoints"). **Competence**: publicly acknowledge craftsmanship ("the way you terminated that panel is the cleanest I've seen this year"). **Relatedness**: include the subcontractor in the project's social fabric (eat with them on site, not separately; learn their children's names). For client management, **autonomy** means giving the client three viable options instead of one recommendation — the choice itself satisfies the autonomy need even if they end up choosing your original recommendation. For self-management, the question is: am I building my business to satisfy my own autonomy, competence, and relatedness — or am I building it to satisfy an image need (esteem) that leaves the other three starved?

### Principle 3: Evolutionary psychology — the domain-specific drives

Evolutionary psychology (Tooby & Cosmides; Buss) argues that the human mind is a bundle of domain-specific adaptations, each shaped by a recurrent selection pressure in ancestral environments. The major motivational domains are: **self-protection** (avoiding physical harm), **disease avoidance** (disgust, contamination sensitivity), **affiliation** (belonging to a group), **status** (relative position in a hierarchy), **mate selection** (acquiring mates), **mate retention** (keeping mates), **kin care** (protecting genetic relatives), and **reciprocal altruism** (managing exchange relationships) [Buss, *Evolutionary Psychology: The New Science of the Mind*, 6th ed., 2019 — Tier 2; Tooby & Cosmides, "The Psychological Foundations of Culture," in *The Adapted Mind*, 1992 — Tier 4].

Each domain has its own trigger conditions, its own emotional palette, and its own behavioral output. Status triggers pride and shame; affiliation triggers warmth and rejection-pain; kin care triggers tenderness; disease avoidance triggers disgust; self-protection triggers fear and anger. The drives are not arranged hierarchically — they are arranged situationally. The dominant drive is whichever one is most activated by the current environment.

The most powerful of these for business contexts is the **status drive**. Status is not money — money is a means to status, but status is the underlying currency. Status triggers pleasure when raised and pain when lowered, and the brain processes social pain in the same regions that process physical pain (anterior cingulate cortex) [Eisenberger, Lieberman & Williams, "Does Rejection Hurt?," *Science* 302 (2003): 290-292 — Tier 4]. This is why a public insult feels physically painful, and why a client will reject a good deal that lowers their status in front of their peers.

**Mechanism**: Each domain has its own neural circuitry, but they converge on the dopaminergic reward system and the threat system (amygdala, hypothalamus). The drive that wins expression is the one with the highest combined activation (current environmental salience × chronic individual sensitivity).

**When it fails**: Evolutionary psychology is criticized for being "just-so stories" — plausible but unfalsifiable narratives about ancestral pressures. The strongest findings (status hierarchy, kin selection, reciprocal altruism) are well-documented cross-culturally; the weaker findings (specific mate preferences) are more culturally variable than the theory predicts. The application discipline is to use evolutionary psychology as a *hypothesis generator*, not as a definitive explanation.

**Application**: For the Egyptian contractor, the evolutionary lens reveals three drives most relevant to business: **status** (never publicly correct a client or partner in front of their subordinates — the status wound is too costly), **reciprocal altruism** (the subcontractor who did you a favor expects a return favor; failure to reciprocate damages not just one relationship but the network's trust in you), and **kin care** (a subcontractor who has been with you for years is partly kin-categorized; firing him without care damages your standing with the entire team). The disease-avoidance drive also matters in subtle ways — clients will reject designs, materials, or partners that *feel* contaminated by association with a previous failure, even if the association is irrational.

### Principle 4: The SCARF model — social threats and rewards

David Rock's SCARF model (2008) synthesizes the neuroscience of social motivation into five domains where the brain experiences threat or reward: **Status** (relative importance), **Certainty** (predictability), **Autonomy** (sense of control), **Relatedness** (safety with others), and **Fairness** (perceived just exchange) [Rock, "SCARF: A Brain-Based Model for Collaborating With and Influencing Others," *NeuroLeadership Journal* 1 (2008): 1-14 — Tier 2]. The model's central claim is that the brain treats social threats in these five domains with the same intensity and circuitry as it treats physical threats. A status threat activates the amygdala similarly to a physical attack.

The practical implication is the threat-reward asymmetry: threats produce stronger, faster, more lasting responses than equivalent rewards (Baumeister's "bad is stronger than good" principle) [Baumeister et al., "Bad Is Stronger Than Good," *Review of General Psychology* 5 (2001): 323-370 — Tier 4]. So a single status wound (public criticism) can undo years of status satisfaction (private praise). The asymmetry is consistent with the loss-aversion finding from Kahneman and Tversky — losses loom larger than gains — extended into the social domain.

**Mechanism**: The brain's threat system (amygdala, insula, anterior cingulate) is more easily activated and slower to deactivate than the reward system (ventral striatum, vmPFC). This is an evolutionary design feature: missing a reward is recoverable; missing a threat is fatal. The cost is that we over-respond to social threats long after the threat has passed.

**When it fails**: SCARF is a model, not a measurement instrument. The five domains overlap (status and fairness often co-activate; autonomy and certainty often co-activate), and the model does not specify the relative weights of the five for a given individual. It is best used as a *diagnostic checklist* — "which of these five am I threatening in this client right now?" — rather than as a predictive theory.

**Application**: Before any difficult conversation with a client, subcontractor, or partner, run the SCARF checklist: *Am I about to threaten their status?* (e.g., correcting them in public) *Their certainty?* (e.g., springing a price change without warning) *Their autonomy?* (e.g., forcing a choice rather than offering options) *Their relatedness?* (e.g., treating them as a transaction rather than a relationship) *Their fairness?* (e.g., paying them later than you pay others for equivalent work). Threatening any one of these creates resistance. Threatening two creates hostility. Threatening three creates an enemy. The cost of avoiding the threat is small (a sentence, a delay, a phone call); the cost of triggering it is large (lost client, sabotage, lawsuit).

### Principle 5: The Islamic perspective — the *fitrah* and the three dimensions

The Islamic intellectual tradition posits that the human being is born with a *fitrah* — an original disposition oriented toward recognition of God (tawhid) and toward moral uprightness. The Quranic verse "the nature (fitrah) of Allah upon which He created people" (30:30) is the foundational text. The tradition further distinguishes three dimensions of the human being, each with its own needs and its own proper satisfaction:

1. **The physical dimension** (*jasad / badan*): needs food, shelter, rest, sexual regulation, physical safety. Satisfied through halal provision and moderation.
2. **The intellectual/rational dimension** (*'aql*): needs knowledge, understanding, problem-solving, the exercise of reason. Satisfied through learning, reflection (*tafakkur*), and the pursuit of wisdom.
3. **The spiritual dimension** (*ruh / qalb*): needs remembrance of God (*dhikr*), connection to the transcendent, moral meaning. Satisfied through worship, prayer, and ethical action.

Al-Ghazali in *Ihya 'Ulum al-Din* (Revival of the Religious Sciences) develops this tripartite anthropology in detail, treating each dimension as a domain with proper and improper satisfactions [Al-Ghazali, *Ihya 'Ulum al-Din*, Book 1, "Kitab al-'Ilm" — Tier 1, classical]. Ibn Sina's medical and psychological works (*Kitab al-Nafs*, *The Book of the Soul*) similarly treat the human being as a layered composite whose health requires attention to each layer [Ibn Sina, *Kitab al-Najat*, Book 6 (on the soul) — Tier 1, classical].

The Islamic view differs from Maslow in that no dimension is hierarchically prior — all three are intrinsic and co-original. The body without the soul is dead matter; the soul without the body has no earthly action; the intellect bridges them and is itself a divine gift. The differences from SDT: autonomy is real but bounded by divine law; competence is real but bounded by the requirement of obedience; relatedness is real but is subordinated to relatedness with God (the famous hadith: "no one truly believes until he loves for his brother what he loves for himself" — which extends relatedness outward but orients it upward).

The Islamic view also introduces a category largely absent from the Western frameworks: the **nafs** (the self/lower soul) and its stages. Al-Ghazali describes the *nafs al-ammara* (the commanding self, the lower drives), the *nafs al-lawwama* (the self-reproaching self, where conscience begins), and the *nafs al-mutma'inna* (the tranquil self, at peace with God). This developmental model anticipates the contemporary stages-of-change literature and is more honest about the *internal conflict* between drives than most Western models.

**Mechanism**: The Islamic mechanism is not neuroscience but theology and ethical cultivation. The drives are real; they are not denied. The discipline is to channel them — physical drives toward halal satisfaction within marriage, intellectual drives toward beneficial knowledge, status drives toward the rank of the pious, relatedness drives toward the believers and beyond.

**When it fails**: The Islamic framework is normative, not descriptive — it tells you what drives *should* be supreme and how they *should* be ordered, not what people actually do. As a descriptive psychology, it under-predicts much modern behavior (consumerism, status-seeking through luxury brands, social media attention-seeking). As a prescriptive framework, however, it offers something the Western descriptive models do not: a *telos*, a purpose, against which drives can be measured and chosen.

**Application**: For the Egyptian contractor, the Islamic three-dimensional view offers a self-management discipline that the Western models lack. Am I feeding all three dimensions of myself? Most contractors feed the physical dimension well (income, security, family provision), feed the intellectual dimension intermittently (some professional development), and starve the spiritual dimension. The cost is a particular kind of emptiness that no amount of business success fills. The tripartite view also offers a client-management lens: a client who is starving spiritually will seek meaning in the project itself — will want the project to "say something," to be a legacy. A client who is starving intellectually will seek control over technical decisions he doesn't understand, to feel competent. A client who is starving physically (cash-flow crisis) cannot be sold vision; he must be sold certainty.

### Principle 6: Drives compete; the operative drive is contextual

The single most useful insight from cross-theory synthesis is that drives compete for expression, and the *operative* drive at any moment is determined by the interaction of (a) chronic individual sensitivities (a status-driven person activates the status drive more easily than a relatedness-driven person), (b) acute environmental triggers (an insult activates status; a deadline activates certainty; a death in the family activates relatedness), and (c) the resource constraints (a person with no money cannot satisfy status through consumption, so they satisfy it through aggression).

This competition is why people are inconsistent: a client who is calm and reasonable in June can be hostile and irrational in November if their cash position has deteriorated. The chronic personality has not changed; the operative drive has shifted because the environment has changed. Strategy must read the operative drive, not the chronic personality.

The practical discipline: at the start of any significant interaction, ask "what is the operative drive here?" not "what kind of person is this?" The first question is answerable from observable behavior in the last 24 hours. The second is a stable attribution that the last 24 hours may have already invalidated.

**Application**: A subcontractor who has been loyal for three years becomes unreliable on the next project. The advisor's instinct is "his personality changed" (stable attribution). The advisor's discipline is "what drive is now operative for him?" — and the answer is often that he received a better offer from a competitor (status + autonomy), or that his wife is ill (kin care overwhelming reciprocity), or that he was publicly criticized on the last project (status wound producing disengagement). Each of these has a different remedy. The first is met with matching pay; the second with patience and accommodation; the third with a private, face-saving apology.

### Principle 7: The hidden drives — meaning, control, and esteem

Three drives are systematically under-reported by humans and under-modeled by the classical theories: **meaning** (the sense that one's life and actions matter in a larger narrative), **control** (the sense that one can affect outcomes — distinct from autonomy, which is about choice; control is about efficacy), and **esteem** (the social mirror of one's worth — distinct from status, which is rank; esteem is recognition).

Viktor Frankl's *Man's Search for Meaning* (1946) argued that the will to meaning is more fundamental than the will to pleasure (Freud) or the will to power (Adler) [Frankl, *Man's Search for Meaning*, 1946 — Tier 2]. The evidence is the survivorship literature: people endure extreme deprivation when meaning is preserved and collapse when meaning is lost, even when physical conditions are equivalent.

The control drive is documented in Langer's research on "the illusion of control" and in the learned helplessness literature (Seligman, 1972): animals and humans exposed to uncontrollable stressors become passive, depressed, and unable to act even when control becomes available [Seligman, *Helplessness: On Depression, Development, and Death*, 1975 — Tier 2]. The drive for control is so strong that people prefer certain suffering they control over uncertain relief they don't.

The esteem drive is the silent governor of much business behavior. A client who pays a premium for a brand-name consultant over a more competent local one is buying esteem, not competence. A contractor who buys equipment he doesn't need to impress clients he doesn't have is feeding esteem. The esteem drive is the most likely to be denied by the person experiencing it — almost no one says "I'm doing this for the esteem" — which is why it is the most strategically important to detect.

**Application**: For the Egyptian contractor, three diagnostic questions for any significant decision: (1) *Whose meaning is being served by this?* — if the answer is "no one's, it's just a deal," the deal is fragile. (2) *Who is losing control by this?* — anyone losing control will sabotage the deal unless given an alternative lever. (3) *Whose esteem is at stake?* — the person whose esteem is most at stake will be the most vocal and the least rational. Identify that person first and address their esteem need first; everything else becomes tractable.

## Where experts disagree

**On whether needs are hierarchical or concurrent.** Maslow (1943, 1968) defends a soft hierarchy; Wahba & Bridwell (1976) and Tay & Diener (2011) find no strict hierarchy in cross-cultural data; evolutionary psychologists treat needs as concurrent domain-specific adaptations. The synthesis adopted here is that the *attention* is hierarchical (the most salient unmet need absorbs attention) but the *motivation* is concurrent (all needs are operating).

**On whether autonomy is universal or culturally variable.** SDT's authors (Deci, Ryan) defend universality; Iyengar & Lepper's famous "choice" studies found that Asian-American children performed better on tasks chosen by their mothers than on tasks they chose themselves, suggesting that autonomy is mediated by relational frames in collectivist cultures [Iyengar & Lepper, "Rethinking the Value of Choice," *Journal of Personality and Social Psychology* 76 (1999): 349-366 — Tier 4]. The synthesis adopted here is that the *experience* of autonomy is universal, but the conditions that produce it are culturally variable — choice in the West, volitional alignment in the East, role-based autonomy in Egypt.

**On whether evolutionary psychology is science or narrative.** Cosmides, Tooby, and Buss defend the empirical rigor; Gould's critique (in "The Spandrels of San Marco," 1979, and elsewhere) attacks the adaptationist program as unfalsifiable; contemporary critics (Buller, *Adapting Minds*, 2005) argue that many EP claims fail replication. The synthesis adopted here uses EP as a hypothesis generator for the drives that are well-documented cross-culturally (status, reciprocity, kin care) and treats the more speculative claims with caution.

**On whether the Islamic *fitrah* model is descriptive or prescriptive.** Classical scholars treat it as both — descriptive of the original nature, prescriptive of the proper order. Modern Muslim psychologists (Badri, *The Dilemma of Muslim Psychologists*, 1979) argue that the Islamic framework is empirically testable where it makes descriptive claims (e.g., that humans flourish when all three dimensions are fed) and normative where it makes prescriptive claims (e.g., that sexual drives should be satisfied within marriage). The synthesis adopted here uses the framework descriptively where it overlaps with empirical findings and prescriptively where the user has chosen the framework as his own.

## Applied to the Egyptian contractor's domain

Egypt amplifies some drives and dampens others, in culturally specific ways:

**Status is paramount.** Egyptian business culture is high-context and high-status-sensitive. A client's status in front of his family, his employees, and his peers is not a side concern — it is a primary economic variable. The contractor who manages to make the client *look good* in front of his board (clear technical explanations, professional documentation, on-time delivery) wins repeat business at rates 2-3x the contractor who delivers equivalent technical work without the status management. Tier P observation from the user's own market.

**Relatedness is dense.** Egyptian business is relationship-first, transaction-second. A cold-call approach that works in Germany fails in Egypt. The first three meetings are about establishing the relationship (family, origins, mutual contacts) before any business is discussed. The contractor who tries to skip this stage is read as untrustworthy — not because the relationship stage reveals anything material, but because the willingness to invest in it signals commitment.

**Certainty is amplified by macro instability.** Egypt's macroeconomic volatility (currency devaluations, regulatory shifts, supply chain disruptions) makes the certainty drive hypersensitive. Clients will pay a premium for predictable pricing, fixed-scope contracts, and binding timelines — even when the premium is actuarially unjustified. The contractor who can offer certainty (through hedging, through inventory, through contractual mechanisms) captures that premium.

**Autonomy is role-based.** Egyptian workers and subcontractors value autonomy within role — freedom to do their craft their way — but do not expect or want autonomy over their role. Asking a master electrician how he wants to route his conduit is respectful; asking him whether he wants to be the electrician on this project is disorienting. SDT's Western assumption that "more autonomy = more motivation" does not transfer unmodified.

**Fairness is tribal.** Fairness in Egyptian business culture is calibrated to the in-group, not to the universal standard. A contractor is expected to pay his own subcontractors preferentially, to favor his own relatives in hiring, to give his own clients better terms than strangers. Western-style "fairness as impartiality" reads as betrayal of the in-group. The strategic implication: be transparent about the in-group structure rather than pretending to universal fairness that no one believes.

**The spiritual dimension is non-optional.** In a Western framework, spiritual needs are private. In Egypt, they are public and structural. Prayer times structure the workday. Ramadan structures the work year. The contractor who fails to plan around prayer times loses productivity (workers leave anyway) and respect (he is read as hostile to religion). The contractor who plans around them gains both.

## What I still don't know

- The specific operationalization of these drives for the Egyptian construction sector has not been formally studied; the applications above are principled extrapolations from cross-cultural research and the user's own market observation. They need validation through systematic data collection on the user's own projects.
- The interaction between the macroeconomic environment (devaluation, supply chain) and the certainty drive has not been modeled quantitatively; the user could track this by logging client decisions before and after major devaluation events.
- The age-cohort differences in drive priority (younger Egyptian clients vs. older ones) are intuitive but not formally documented; a generational study would refine the model.
- The dark-triad personality structure (see `dark_triad_recognition.md`) modifies all the drives above — for such personalities, status dominates and the other drives atrophy. The interaction between drive profile and personality structure is a frontier.
- The Islamic framework's developmental model (the stages of the *nafs*) has not been integrated with contemporary stage-of-change psychology (Prochaska & DiClemente) in a systematic way; this is a research opportunity for a Muslim psychologist.
