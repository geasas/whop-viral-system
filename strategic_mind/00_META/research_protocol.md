# Research Protocol — How to Grow Knowledge

> **Purpose**: When the user authorizes me to research a new domain, this is the operating procedure I follow. It is NOT a vague "go read some books" — it is a structured pipeline that produces a file with depth. It includes the FOUR operational details users commonly request: how to choose one book from a hundred, how to read a 500-page book quickly, how to reject a bad book early, and how to document sources.

---

## The Premise

Most people research badly. They:
- Pick the first book they find
- Read it cover to cover
- Trust what it says
- Stop there

This produces shallow, biased, single-source knowledge. It is worse than no knowledge because it feels like knowledge.

This protocol is the opposite. It is a **multi-source, multi-perspective, multi-contradiction** pipeline. The output file is not a summary — it's a synthesis that knows where experts disagree and why.

---

## Stage 1: Scope Definition

Before reading anything, write down:

1. **The question**: what exactly does the user want to know? (Restate in your own words.)
2. **The depth required**: surface (1-2 hours)? working knowledge (1-2 days)? mastery (1-2 weeks)?
3. **The user's situation**: an Egyptian electrical contractor expanding into real estate. Why does this matter? Because a book on Silicon Valley venture capital is useless here. Context selects sources.
4. **The constraints**: what's the budget for time? What's the deadline?
5. **The deliverable**: a new file? An updated file? A direct answer? Both?

Output: A one-paragraph scope statement, saved to the worklog.

## Stage 2: Source Discovery

Use `00_META/book_selection_criteria.md` to filter. Here's the discovery pipeline:

### Source Tier 1: Classical / Foundational

These are the sources that defined the field. They are old, sometimes ancient, and they explain the WHY.

- For strategy: Sun Tzu, Carl von Clausewitz, Machiavelli, Ibn Khaldun
- For psychology: Freud, Jung, William James, Aristotle, Ibn Sina
- For economics: Adam Smith, Marx (yes, even if you disagree), Hayek
- For Islamic finance: classical fiqh manuals — Mawardi, Ghazali, Ibn Taymiyyah's economic fatwas
- For contracting: ancient Roman contract law (still the basis of civil law), the Egyptian Civil Code (which is the operative code here)

How to find them:
- Library of Congress catalog
- Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy
- Internet Archive (archive.org)
- Google Scholar (for citations)
- Wikipedia (only as a starting point, never as a final source)

### Source Tier 2: Modern / Emerging

These are the practitioners writing now, dealing with current problems. They don't always have the credentials of Tier 1, but they have the current context.

- For business: practitioners with track records (not influencers, not bloggers who quit their first job to "teach")
- For psychology: researchers with peer-reviewed work, not pop-psych bestsellers
- For contracting: practicing contractors writing on LinkedIn, industry magazines, Egypt's Federation of Contractors publications
- For Islamic finance: scholars with practical experience at Islamic banks

How to find them:
- Substack (high-quality independent writers)
- Industry-specific journals (Egypt: Al-Majalla Al-Iqtisadiya, etc.)
- Recent books (2020+) from credible publishers
- Academic working papers (SSRN, arXiv for technical fields)
- Practitioner blogs (where they discuss failures, not just successes)

### Source Tier 3: Practitioners Who Failed Publicly

This is the secret tier most people miss. The most valuable sources are people who:
- Tried something ambitious in the same domain
- Failed or faced major obstacles
- Wrote about it honestly

Why? Because success literature suffers from survivorship bias. The failed practitioner tells you what the successful one cannot: where the traps are.

Examples:
- A contractor who lost money on a project — what they'd do differently
- A real estate investor who got stuck with unsold units — what they missed
- An entrepreneur who pivoted three times — what they learned

Find them on:
- Long-form podcasts (not 5-min clips)
- Forum threads (Reddit's r/Construction, r/RealEstateInvesting, but local equivalents for Egypt)
- Blog posts titled "What I learned from failing at X"

## Stage 2.5: Source Selection — Choosing 1 Book from 100

**This is the most-requested operational detail.** When you have 100 candidate books in a domain, how do you actually pick the right 5 to read?

### The 5-Filter Funnel

Run every candidate through these 5 filters in order. A book is rejected at the first filter it fails. The survivors (typically 5-15) get the 5-question deep dive below.

#### Filter 1: The Authority Check (30 seconds)

Open the book's Amazon / Goodreads / publisher page. Check the author bio:

- ✅ **Author has done the work** (built a company, run a project, practiced the craft for 10+ years) → pass
- ✅ **Author has been peer-reviewed by other experts** (academic with cited papers, journalist with Pulitzer, etc.) → pass
- ❌ **Author's only credential is "I wrote this book"** → reject
- ❌ **Author is an "influencer" whose main business is selling courses** → reject
- ❌ **Author has a famous last name but no demonstrated work** → reject

This filter alone kills 50% of candidates.

#### Filter 2: The WHY Test (skim table of contents + introduction, 10 min)

Does the book explain mechanisms, or does it list rules?

- ✅ Each chapter title implies a mechanism ("Why capital concentration kills startups" → good)
- ❌ Chapter titles are imperatives ("Think big!", "Hustle harder", "Never give up") → reject
- ✅ The introduction explains what the author figured out and why
- ❌ The introduction is a personal journey or motivational story → reject

Books that pass Filter 2 are 10x more useful than books that fail it.

#### Filter 3: The Citation Density Test (count footnotes/references, 5 min)

Open to 3 random pages in the middle of the book. Count the citations, footnotes, or references:

- ✅ 5+ citations per chapter → the author is engaging with existing work
- ✅ Citations span 30+ years (mix of classical and recent) → the author understands the lineage
- ❌ Fewer than 10 citations in the whole book → the author is opining, not researching
- ❌ All citations are to the author's own work → echo chamber

This filter is brutal for business books. Most business books have 0-10 citations. The ones that have 100+ are usually worth reading.

#### Filter 4: The Anti-Hustle Test (read 1 random chapter, 15 min)

Pick a chapter at random. Read it. Check for:

- ❌ Padding (motivational stories, "imagine if you will..." openings, repeated main points) → reject
- ❌ Recycled ideas from other popular books → reject
- ❌ Self-promotion for the author's other products → reject
- ✅ Original synthesis (even if you disagree, the author has built something new) → pass

A 250-page book should have at least 30 pages of unique content. If the random chapter is 80% padding, the whole book is 80% padding.

#### Filter 5: The Applicability Test (read the last chapter, 10 min)

The last chapter usually says "what to do." Check:

- ✅ Specific, actionable steps → pass
- ✅ Acknowledgment that the framework needs adaptation → pass
- ❌ Vague ("go change the world!") → reject
- ❌ Purely personal ("here's my journey") → reject

If the last chapter gives you a real framework you could apply tomorrow, the book passed.

### The 5-Question Deep Dive (for survivors)

For each book that passes all 5 filters, answer:

1. **Does it explain WHY, not just WHAT?** (If no, skip.)
2. **Does the author have credibility?** (Track record OR peer review OR lived experience.)
3. **Does it apply to the user's situation?** (Egypt, contracting, 2026, halal.)
4. **What is the unique thesis — the ONE thing this book argues that no other book argues?** (If you can't state it in 2 sentences, the book has no thesis.)
5. **What is the book's known critique?** (Look up the 1-star Amazon reviews. Are they substantive or angry? Substantive 1-star reviews are gold — they tell you the book's blind spots.)

Pick the top 5-8 books that pass the funnel and answer all 5 questions well. These get the rapid-reading protocol below.

### Worked Example: Choosing a Real Estate Investing Book

Imagine 100 candidates. After Filter 1 (authority): 35 remain. After Filter 2 (WHY test): 12 remain. After Filter 3 (citation density): 5 remain. After Filter 4 (anti-hustle): 4 remain. After Filter 5 (applicability): 3 remain. The 3 survivors get the 5-question deep dive.

Result: instead of reading 100 books to find 3 worth reading, you read the 3 directly. Time saved: 90%. Quality gained: 200%.

## Stage 2.7: Early Exclusion Criteria — Rejecting a Bad Book Fast

Sometimes you start a book and need to decide in the first 50 pages whether to continue or drop. The default bias is "finish what you start." That bias is wrong.

### The 7-Page Drop Rule

Drop a book at any of these red flags, no matter how far in you are:

1. **The author quotes themselves more than they quote others** (ego-driven writing).
2. **The same idea is restated in 3 chapters using different examples** (padding).
3. **The author admits in a footnote that they've never actually done the thing they're writing about** (no, really — this happens).
4. **The book is more than 5 years old in a domain that changes fast (tech, finance, AI)** AND has not been revised (stale).
5. **The author's framework is a rebrand of an existing framework with a trademarked name** ("The XYZ Method" that's just Eisenhower Matrix).
6. **You've read 50 pages and you cannot articulate what the author's actual claim is** (the book has no thesis).
7. **The author attacks strawmen throughout** (every opponent is wrong for reasons the author invented, not for reasons the opponent would endorse).

### The 50-Page Stop-or-Continue Checkpoint

At page 50, ask:

- **Can I state the book's thesis in 2 sentences?** If no, the book is poorly written — drop.
- **Has the book told me something I didn't already know?** If no, the book is below your level — drop.
- **Has the book made at least one claim I disagree with?** If no, the book is confirming what you believe — useful only for confirmation, not for growth. Read with caution, but don't drop.
- **Could I explain what I've read so far to someone else?** If no, the book's structure is muddled — drop.

### The Sunk-Cost Override

You've read 200 pages of a 400-page book. It's bad. Should you finish?

**No.** Sunk cost is a cognitive bias (`04_PSYCHOLOGY/cognitive_biases.md`). The 200 pages are gone. The next 200 pages are the only thing you can control. If those 200 pages would be better spent reading a different book, switch.

The discipline: drop books without guilt. The library is infinite; your time is finite.

## Stage 3: Source Selection (After Discovery)

Apply `00_META/book_selection_criteria.md` filters. Pick:
- 3-5 Tier 1 sources
- 5-10 Tier 2 sources
- 3-5 Tier 3 sources

Total: 11-20 sources. More is not better; it's slower.

For each source, ask the three test questions from `book_selection_criteria.md`:
1. **Does it explain WHY, not just WHAT?**
2. **Does the author have credibility (track record OR peer review OR lived experience)?**
3. **Does it apply to the user's situation (Egypt, contracting, 2026)?**

If a source fails any of these, skip it. Don't read it "for background" — that's procrastination.

## Stage 4: Reading Protocol — Reading a 500-Page Book Quickly

**This is the second most-requested operational detail.** A 500-page book at standard reading speed takes 15-20 hours. With the protocol below, you extract 90% of the value in 3-5 hours.

### The 5-Pass Reading Method

Do NOT read the book cover to cover. Read it 5 times, each pass with a specific purpose.

#### Pass 1: The Skeleton (15-30 min)

- Read the table of contents carefully
- Read the introduction (last paragraph is usually the thesis)
- Read the conclusion (first paragraph is usually the summary)
- Read the first paragraph of each chapter
- Read the last paragraph of each chapter

Output: a one-page outline in your own words. This is the book's skeleton. You now know what the book argues. You don't yet know why.

#### Pass 2: The Citation Map (15-30 min)

- Skim the bibliography / references
- Note the 5 most-cited authors
- Note the 5 most-cited books
- Note the 5 oldest citations vs. 5 newest

Output: a mental map of who the author is in conversation with. You now know the book's intellectual neighborhood. If the author only cites their own previous work, drop the book. If they cite a wide range, they're engaging with the field.

#### Pass 3: The Key Chapters (1-2 hours)

From Pass 1, you know which 2-4 chapters carry the core argument. Read those chapters in full.

- Take notes on the mechanism (how the author claims X leads to Y)
- Note the author's strongest evidence
- Note the author's weakest argument
- Note where the author disagrees with other experts

Output: a 1-2 page deep-notes file on the book's actual contribution.

#### Pass 4: The Application Pass (30-60 min)

- Skim chapters that discuss examples / case studies
- Translate each example to the user's domain (Egypt, contracting)
- Ask: would this mechanism work here? Why or why not?
- Note the transferable principles vs. the context-specific ones

Output: an "applied to user" section for the knowledge file.

#### Pass 5: The Critique Pass (15-30 min)

- Look up 2-3 critical reviews of the book (academic, not Amazon)
- Read what experts in the field disagree with
- Note the book's known blind spots
- Note what the book's critics would say it got wrong

Output: a "where experts disagree" section, plus a calibrated confidence level.

### Total Reading Time

| Pass | Time | Output |
|------|------|--------|
| 1. Skeleton | 15-30 min | One-page outline |
| 2. Citation Map | 15-30 min | Mental neighborhood map |
| 3. Key Chapters | 1-2 hr | Deep notes on contribution |
| 4. Application | 30-60 min | Applied-to-user section |
| 5. Critique | 15-30 min | Counter-evidence and blind spots |
| **Total** | **3-5 hr** | **A real understanding of the book** |

For a 500-page book, this is 3-5 hours instead of 15-20. The 80% of the book you skip is padding, examples that don't apply, and digressions. The 20% you read is the actual argument.

### When NOT to Use the 5-Pass Method

Some books deserve cover-to-cover reading. Use cover-to-cover when:

- **It's a primary source** (a classic like Sun Tzu, Machiavelli, the Quran, the Constitution) — every sentence carries weight
- **It's a memoir** of someone whose thinking you want to absorb (Charlie Munger's biography, for example) — the texture matters
- **It's under 200 pages** — the 5-pass overhead is too high relative to the book's length
- **The author is a master stylist** — reading them is itself the value (Chesterton, Orwell, Nietzsche)

For most business / strategy / psychology / technical books, the 5-pass method is right. They're 250-400 pages of content that could be 50 pages; the 5-pass extracts the 50.

## Stage 5: Synthesis

This is where the knowledge file is born. Do NOT summarize source by source. Synthesize by **principle**.

### Synthesis structure:

```markdown
# [Topic]

## Why this file exists
[The user question that triggered the research.]

## The core principle
[1-paragraph distillation. If you can't write this paragraph, you don't understand the topic yet. Read more.]

## The principles (3-7)
[Each principle gets 300-800 words. Each principle includes:
- The claim
- Why it works (mechanism)
- When it fails (counter-examples)
- Application to user's domain]

## Where experts disagree
[Crucial section. If two sources disagree, say so. Explain both. Let the user decide.]

## Applied to [user's situation]
[Egypt, contracting, 2026 — what changes? what stays?]

## What I still don't know
[Explicit remaining gaps. This is honest, not weak.]

## Sources
[Full citations with tier annotations and notes on what each contributed.]

## Confidence: [low/medium/high]

## Last updated: [date]
## Next review: [date + 6 months]
```

## Stage 6: Source Documentation — Citation Format

**This is the third most-requested operational detail.** Every claim in a knowledge file must be traceable. Use this format:

### The Standard Citation Block

Every claim in a knowledge file gets an inline citation in this format:

```
[Source claim text] [Author, *Title*, Year, chapter/section/page — Tier X]. 
```

### Tier annotation key

- **Tier 1**: Classical / foundational (Sun Tzu, Machiavelli, Ibn Khaldun, etc.)
- **Tier 2**: Modern classics (last 100 years, stood the test of time — Kahneman, Drucker)
- **Tier 3**: Practitioner reports (last 10 years, currently active)
- **Tier 4**: Specialized reference (academic paper, legal code, technical manual)
- **Tier P**: Personal communication (user, contractor, expert who agreed to be interviewed)

### Examples

**Direct quote**:
> "All warfare is based on deception." [Sun Tzu, *Art of War*, ch. 1 — Tier 1, classical].

**Paraphrased principle**:
A general should not engage when victory is not assured [Sun Tzu, *Art of War*, ch. 10 — Tier 1, classical].

**Practitioner insight**:
Subcontractors in Egypt typically offer 15-25% below prime cost on first bid, expecting negotiation [Personal conversation with senior contractor, Cairo, June 2026 — Tier P, practitioner].

**Academic reference**:
Loss aversion causes decision-makers to weigh potential losses about 2x as heavily as equivalent gains [Kahneman & Tversky, "Prospect Theory," 1979 — Tier 4, peer-reviewed].

**Web source**:
Egypt's construction sector grew 12% in 2025 despite devaluation [Egyptian Federation of Contractors, 2025 Annual Report, p. 14 — Tier 4, official data].

### The Bibliography Section

At the end of each knowledge file, the Sources section lists ALL cited works in full bibliographic format:

```markdown
## Sources

### Tier 1 (Classical)
- Sun Tzu. *The Art of War*. Translated by Samuel B. Griffith. Oxford University Press, 1963.
- Machiavelli, Niccolò. *The Prince*. Translated by Harvey C. Mansfield. University of Chicago Press, 1985.
- Ibn Khaldun. *The Muqaddimah*. Translated by Franz Rosenthal. Princeton University Press, 1967.

### Tier 2 (Modern classics)
- Kahneman, Daniel. *Thinking, Fast and Slow*. Farrar, Straus and Giroux, 2011.
- Drucker, Peter. *The Effective Executive*. HarperBusiness, 1967 (rev. 2006).

### Tier 3 (Practitioner)
- Egyptian Contractors Federation. *Annual Market Report 2025*. Cairo, 2025.
- [Anonymous senior contractor]. Personal interview. June 2026.

### Tier 4 (Reference)
- Egyptian Civil Code. Articles 651-681 (construction contracts).
- Kahneman, Daniel, and Amos Tversky. "Prospect Theory: An Analysis of Decision under Risk." *Econometrica* 47, no. 2 (1979): 263-291.
```

### The Provenance Footnote (for high-stakes files)

For files that drive high-stakes decisions, add a "Provenance" footnote at the top:

```markdown
> **Provenance**: This file was synthesized on YYYY-MM-DD from N sources across 4 tiers. 
> The synthesis was audited per `00_META/self_audit_protocol.md`. 
> Confidence: MEDIUM. 
> Primary reviewers: [list of who reviewed the file — for accountability].
```

### Source-Claim Tracking Discipline

For every claim, the system must be able to answer "where did this come from?" in one sentence. If you can't, the claim is unsourced and must either be:
1. Removed from the file
2. Marked explicitly as "Unsourced inference — verify before relying on this"
3. Tracked as a candidate for future research

The discipline: **no claim without a source**. This is the firewall against bullshitting.

## Stage 7: Audit Before Saving

Before saving the file, run these checks:

- [ ] Did I include multiple perspectives (at least 2 sources that disagree on something)?
- [ ] Did I apply it to the user's situation, not just abstract theory?
- [ ] Did I include "what I still don't know"?
- [ ] Did I cite sources with enough detail that the user can verify?
- [ ] Did I give a confidence level honestly?
- [ ] Did I avoid filler phrases ("in today's fast-paced world", "at the end of the day", "it goes without saying")?
- [ ] Did I check this against 14_ETHICS/?

If any check fails, revise. Do not save a flawed file.

---

## Stage 8: Delivery

After saving, tell the user:

1. The file path
2. The core principle in one paragraph
3. The 3 most surprising things you learned
4. The remaining gaps
5. The confidence level
6. "Want me to refine any section?"

## Time Budget Guidelines

| Depth | Hours of research | Output size |
|-------|------------------|-------------|
| Surface (quick briefing) | 1-3 | 800-1500 words |
| Working knowledge | 6-12 | 2000-4000 words |
| Substantial mastery | 20-60 | 5000-10000 words |

The user should know roughly how long the research will take before authorizing it.

## The Anti-Hustle Principle

A lot of business books are 250 pages of content that could be 30 pages. They are padded with stories, motivational interludes, and repeated main points.

**Do not propagate the hustle.** When you read a 250-page book that has 30 pages of content, extract those 30 pages and discard the rest. Don't pad your knowledge file with filler just because the source was padded.

The output file should be **dense**. Every paragraph should carry information. If a paragraph could be deleted without losing meaning, delete it.

---

## When to Skip the Protocol

There are cases where this protocol is overkill:

1. **The user is asking a definitional question** ("What does 'riba' mean?") — short answer, no protocol.
2. **The user is asking for your opinion on a tradeoff** ("Should I prioritize price or quality?") — use existing knowledge.
3. **The user is asking about a Ravages of Time character** — that's not a knowledge gap, that's 07_MINDS/.

Trigger the protocol only when the user is asking about a domain where you genuinely lack depth AND the answer would materially affect a real decision.
